Quantum Computing and Quantum-Safe Cryptography

This page explains why future quantum computers threaten today's public-key locks and how quantum-safe algorithms protect data before those machines arrive.

Why quantum computers change the rules

Normal computers store information in bits that are strictly 0 or 1. A quantum computer uses quantum bits (qubits) that can exist in a combined wave-like state called superposition and cancel out wrong answers through interference. That does not make a quantum computer faster at everything, but it allows clever algorithms to solve certain structured math puzzles—especially finding repeating patterns in huge numbers—vastly faster than any classical supercomputer.

Why it matters

Today's RSA and elliptic-curve locks rely on factoring and discrete-log puzzles that would take classical computers billions of years. A large, error-corrected quantum computer could break those locks in hours. Even though such machines do not exist yet, attackers can record encrypted internet traffic today and store it until a quantum computer is built—a threat known as Harvest Now, Decrypt Later.

Post-quantum (or quantum-safe) algorithms run on ordinary laptops and servers today, using different math puzzles—such as multidimensional grids called lattices or pure hash trees—that neither classical nor quantum computers know how to shortcut.

How to think about it

Imagine two different locks on a safe: a combination dial (RSA/ECC) and a thousand-dimensional maze (lattice). A quantum computer turns out to have a master stethoscope for listening to the repeating clicks of the combination dial, opening it effortlessly—while still getting lost in the multidimensional maze just like an ordinary computer.

New words

Quantum
Physics of atomic-scale discrete states, leveraged by qubits to compute via superposition and wave interference.
Post-quantum cryptography
Classical encryption and signature algorithms designed to withstand attacks by both ordinary and quantum computers.
Lattice
A repeating grid of points in hundreds of dimensions whose shortest-vector and noisy-equation puzzles resist quantum speedups.

Goal: you will be able to inspect the post-quantum and hybrid algorithms already active in OpenSSL and OpenSSH on your workstation.

What just happened

  1. OpenSSL lists NIST's standardized ML-KEM, ML-DSA, and SLH-DSA primitives directly in the default provider.
  2. Hybrid groups such as X25519MLKEM768 combine classical X25519 with post-quantum ML-KEM-768 so an attacker must break both algorithms to recover the session key.

Try it

Run ssh -G localhost | grep '^kexalgorithms' to verify that your OpenSSH client already places mlkem768x25519-sha256 and sntrup761x25519-sha512 ahead of classical-only ECDH.

If it goes wrong

Older remote server rejects hybrid KEX or TLS X25519MLKEM768
Middleboxes with tiny TCP initial buffers occasionally drop large post-quantum ClientHello packets (~1.2 KB). Updating the server or middlebox is the proper fix; never permanently disable PQ hybrids globally.

On Tamlinux, OpenSSH 10.6+ and OpenSSL 3.6+ already negotiate mlkem768x25519-sha256 and X25519MLKEM768 by default, mitigating Harvest-Now-Decrypt-Later risks transparently.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

Why do protocols deploy hybrid key exchange like X25519MLKEM768 instead of switching solely to ML-KEM overnight?

Hybrid key exchange combines a battle-tested classical primitive (X25519) with a newer post-quantum primitive (ML-KEM-768). Even if a subtle mathematical flaw were discovered in ML-KEM, the session remains classically secure—and vice versa against a quantum computer.

Does AES-256 need to be replaced by a lattice cipher to stay quantum-safe?

No. Quantum computers do not break AES in polynomial time; Grover's algorithm only offers a quadratic speedup, reducing AES-256 from 2^256 to roughly 2^128 quantum operations, which remains secure.

How it works: quantum threats and post-quantum hardness

Shor's Algorithm (1994): Factoring N = p·q reduces to finding the period r of f(x) = a^x mod N; similarly, DLP reduces to finding the period of f(x_1, x_2) = g^(x_1) · y^(-x_2) mod p. A quantum computer prepares an equal superposition over indices, computes f(x) coherently, and applies the Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT) so constructive interference peaks at integer multiples of 2^m / r, recovering r via continued fractions in O((log N)³) gates. Every deployed RSA, DH, DSA, ECDSA, and EdDSA system falls completely once fault-tolerant logical qubits scale to thousands.

Grover's Algorithm (1996): Given an unstructured black-box predicate f: {0,1}^n → {0,1} with a unique solution, amplitude amplification rotates the state vector toward the marked item in O(2^(n/2)) sequential oracle queries. Because Grover parallelizes poorly (splitting among P machines saves only √P), AES-256 retains ≥128-bit quantum security.

Lattice-Based Cryptography (ML-KEM & ML-DSA): A lattice L(B) = { B·z : z ∈ ℤ^n } has hard worst-case problems such as Shortest Vector Problem (SVP). Regev's Learning With Errors (LWE) asks to distinguish (A, b = A·s + e mod q) from uniform, where e is a small discrete-Gaussian/binomial error vector—proved at least as hard as worst-case quantum GapSVP. Module-LWE operates over polynomial rings R_q = ℤ_q[X]/(X^n + 1) (n = 256, q = 3329 for ML-KEM; q = 8380417 for ML-DSA), enabling fast Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) multiplication. ML-KEM uses Fujisaki–Okamoto (FO) transformation to lift CPA-secure PKE to IND-CCA2 KEM; ML-DSA uses Fiat–Shamir with Aborts.

Stateless Hash-Based Signatures (SLH-DSA / SPHINCS+): Built purely from WOTS+ one-time signatures, FORS few-time signatures, and a hypertree of Merkle trees, SLH-DSA requires no algebraic assumption—only second-preimage / tweakable hash security—trading larger signatures (~8–50 KB) for conservative mathematical certainty.

Code-Based & Isogeny Lessons: Code-based KEMs (Classic McEliece, HQC) rely on syndrome decoding of linear error-correcting codes. Conversely, supersingular isogeny key exchange (SIKE/SIDH) was completely broken classically in 2022 by Castryck–Decru using Kani's theorem on auxiliary torsion-point information—underscoring why crypto-agility and hybrid deployments are essential.

Edge cases and failure modes

  • FO-transform decapsulation timing side channels: In ML-KEM, implicit rejection re-encrypts the decrypted message and compares ciphertexts; any branch or timing difference during re-encryption or ciphertext comparison leaks the secret key (KyberSlash). Constant-time division-free arithmetic is mandatory.
  • Statefulness hazard in XMSS/LMS vs. SLH-DSA: Stateful hash-based schemes (XMSS/LMS) catastrophically lose security if an index is ever reused (e.g., VM snapshot rollback); stateless SLH-DSA avoids this via randomized signing and a large hypertree.

Check yourself

Why does Module-LWE remain secure against Shor's algorithm even though it uses algebraic rings?

Shor's algorithm solves the Hidden Subgroup Problem (HSP) over abelian groups where the secret is a global periodic subgroup. LWE and Module-LWE inject small non-algebraic error terms e into modular linear equations; the noise destroys exact periodicity, and no efficient quantum reduction to an abelian HSP is known.
