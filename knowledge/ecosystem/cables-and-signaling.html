Transmission media: electrical cables, optical fiber, and wireless waves

This page explains the physical transmission media that carry computer data — electrical copper cables, glass optical fibers, and radiating wireless electromagnetic waves.

All digital communications depend on manipulating physical energy across space. Whether transmitting voltage pulses across copper wires, flashing laser beams through strands of purified glass, or radiating high-frequency electromagnetic waves across the atmosphere, each medium is governed by precise physical laws. Understanding how conductors, optical waveguides, and radio antennas operate, how electrical signals become light or radio waves, and how noise and reflections degrade signal integrity allows you to design, deploy, and troubleshoot computer communication systems.

The three media: copper wire, light in glass, and radio waves

Goal: you will be able to describe the three physical media used to carry computer signals — copper cables, optical fiber, and radio waves — and explain how signals travel through each.

Why it matters: selecting the wrong physical medium causes communication links to fail completely. A copper network cable running past heavy factory motors will pick up devastating electrical noise that corrupts data; running copper cables between separate buildings risks lightning destruction and ground loops; and trying to route high-speed video over long distances without optical fiber results in black screens. Knowing how each medium behaves ensures you select the correct cables and interfaces for your environment.

Before you start: open a terminal window (The Shell and Streams). The commands in this section query physical network link parameters without altering network connections.

New to this? Cables are the physical highways of computing. A cable contains one or more insulated conductors surrounded by protective outer jackets. The terminal commands below show how Linux identifies whether an interface is using a physical cable or wireless radio.

The three fundamental transmission media

All computer data travels through one of three physical channels:

  1. Electrical (Copper) Cables: Signals travel as pulses of electric current (moving electrons) along metal wires.
    • Construction: Central copper conductors wrapped in plastic insulation, surrounded by metallic foil or braided copper shielding, enclosed in an outer jacket.
    • Connectors: RJ-45 (8P8C for Ethernet), USB Type-A and Type-C, DB-9 and DB-25 (RS-232 serial), and flat IDC ribbon cables.
    • Strengths: Cheap, rugged, easily terminated, and can carry electrical power alongside data (Power over Ethernet / USB-PD).
    • Limitations: Suffers electrical noise (EMI), limited distance (typically 100 meters for Ethernet, 2 to 3 meters for high-speed USB), and vulnerable to ground loops.
  2. Fiber Optic Cables: Signals travel as pulses of light (photons) guided inside ultra-pure glass or plastic strands.
    • Construction: A microscopic glass core surrounded by a glass cladding layer with a different optical density, covered in protective aramid yarn (Kevlar) and a colored outer jacket.
    • Connectors: LC (Lucent Connector, small snap-in), SC (Subscriber Connector, push-pull), ST (bayonet twist), and MPO/MTP (multi-fiber ribbon).
    • Strengths: Immune to all electrical interference, radio noise, and lightning surges; zero ground loop risk; transmits multi-gigabit data over tens of kilometers.
    • Limitations: Glass fibers cannot carry electrical power; fragile if bent too tightly (macrobend loss); requires precision optical transceivers.
  3. Wireless (Electromagnetic Waves): Signals travel through free space as radiating invisible waves of coupled electric and magnetic energy without any physical wires.
    • Technologies: Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11), Bluetooth, cellular networks (4G LTE / 5G NR), and satellite communications.
    • Strengths: Total physical mobility; eliminates cabling costs across open spaces.
    • Limitations: Highly vulnerable to physical obstacles (walls, foliage), radio interference, atmospheric moisture absorption, and shared broadcast bandwidth.

Why is a fiber optic cable completely immune to electromagnetic interference (EMI) from nearby power lines or electric motors?

Because fiber optic cables carry data using pulses of light (photons) traveling through non-conductive glass, rather than electric current (electrons) traveling through metal. Magnetic fields from power lines only induce noise in electrical conductors, having zero effect on light beams in glass.

What is the difference between how data travels through copper wire versus how it travels through an optical fiber?

In copper wire, data travels as electrical voltage and current pulses (moving electrons) pushing against physical resistance. In optical fiber, data travels as flashes of light (photons) reflecting inside a transparent glass core.

Electrical signaling: single-ended versus differential

When sending electrical pulses through copper, circuits use one of two fundamental methods:

  • Single-Ended Signaling: Uses one signal wire per data channel, with all channels sharing a common ground wire as their voltage reference.
    • Characteristics: Used in legacy interfaces (RS-232, parallel printer ports, early RAM).
    • Vulnerability: If external static or motor noise induces an electrical spike onto the signal wire, the receiver cannot distinguish the noise from a legitimate data bit. Furthermore, return currents from multiple signals sharing a ground wire cause ground bounce, corrupting high-speed data.
  • Differential Signaling: Uses two tightly twisted wires for each data channel. The transmitter sends the true signal on Wire A ($V_+$) and an exact inverted copy on Wire B ($V_-$).
    • Characteristics: Used in modern high-speed standards (Ethernet, USB, HDMI, DisplayPort, PCIe, RS-485).
    • Common-Mode Noise Rejection: Because the two wires are twisted closely together, any incoming electromagnetic noise ($N$) strikes both wires equally. The receiver measures strictly the difference between the two wires: 
        V_received = (V_+ + N) - (V_- + N) = V_+ - V_-
      The external noise cancels out completely. This allows differential links to run over hundreds of meters at low voltage swings (a few hundred millivolts) with extraordinary noise immunity.

How fiber optics work: Total Internal Reflection

Light naturally travels in a straight line, but optical fibers can bend light around corners. This is achieved through the optical physics of Total Internal Reflection (TIR):

  1. Refractive Index: When light crosses from a dense medium with a higher refractive index (n1) into a less dense medium with a lower refractive index (n2), the light ray bends away from the normal surface.
  2. The Critical Angle: If the light strikes the boundary at an angle shallower than the critical angle (calculated by Snell's law: critical angle = arcsin(n2 / n1)), the light ray cannot escape into the outer cladding. Instead, 100% of the light reflects completely back into the glass core, bouncing down the strand for kilometers with negligible loss.
  3. Single-Mode vs Multi-Mode Fiber:
    • Single-Mode Fiber (SMF, yellow jacket): Has a tiny glass core (only 9 micrometers in diameter — narrower than a human hair). Light travels in a single direct ray, eliminating modal dispersion. Driven by infrared lasers (1310 nm or 1550 nm), SMF carries signals over 10 to 40 kilometers without repeaters.
    • Multi-Mode Fiber (MMF, aqua or orange jacket): Has a wider glass core (50 or 62.5 micrometers). Light bounces along multiple simultaneous reflective paths (modes). Driven by lower-cost LEDs or VCSEL lasers (850 nm), MMF is used for high-bandwidth connections inside datacenters up to 300 to 500 meters.

Electro-optical conversion: from electrons to photons

Computers process electrical signals on silicon, but fiber optics carry light. The conversion occurs inside hot-pluggable optical transceiver modules (such as SFP+ or QSFP):

  • Electrical to Optical (Transmitter): Digital voltage pulses drive a semiconductor Laser Diode or Vertical-Cavity Surface-Emitting Laser (VCSEL). The laser switches on and off in nanoseconds, launching photons into the fiber core.
  • Optical to Electrical (Receiver): Incoming light pulses strike a semiconductor Photodiode (a reverse-biased P-N junction). Photons dislodge electrons via the photoelectric effect, creating a tiny electrical current. A Transimpedance Amplifier (TIA) boosts this tiny current into clean, standard digital voltage swings that motherboard controllers understand.

Radio waves: from electrical circuits to wireless radiation

How does an electrical computer broadcast data across a room without wires?

  1. Accelerating Charge: Under the laws of classical electrodynamics (Maxwell's equations), whenever electrical charges accelerate — such as high-frequency alternating current rushing back and forth inside a metal rod — they create an oscillating electric field (E).
  2. Coupled Magnetic Field: A changing electric field generates an oscillating magnetic field (B), which in turn regenerates an electric field. The two fields couple together and detach from the metal conductor, radiating outward into space at the speed of light (300,000 km/s) as an electromagnetic radio wave.
  3. Antenna Reception: When the radiating electromagnetic wave passes across a distant antenna, the oscillating electric field pushes electrons inside the metal rod, inducing an alternating electric current. The receiving radio chip amplifies and demodulates this microvolt signal back into binary digital bits.
  4. Wi-Fi and Cellular Frequency Bands: Wi-Fi modulates radio frequencies in unlicensed Industrial, Scientific, and Medical (ISM) spectrum bands (2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, and 6 GHz). Cellular networks (4G LTE / 5G NR) operate across licensed regional frequency bands (700 MHz to 3.5 GHz for long-range coverage, and 24 GHz to 40 GHz millimeter-wave for ultra-dense short-range capacity).

Independent task

Explain why an RS-485 industrial communication bus uses a 120-ohm resistor at each end of its differential cable, and predict what happens to digital signal pulses if those resistors are missing.

A 120-ohm resistor matches the cable's characteristic impedance ($Z_0$). When an electrical pulse reaches the end of an un-terminated wire (open circuit), the electrical energy cannot leave and reflects back down the cable like an echo. These reflected waves collide with incoming data pulses, creating ringing and false edges that corrupt bit reading. Terminating the line with a resistor matching $Z_0$ absorbs 100% of the pulse's energy with zero reflection.

How does differential signaling eliminate electromagnetic noise picked up along a computer cable?

Differential signaling transmits equal and opposite voltages across a tightly twisted pair of wires. When external noise strikes the cable, it introduces an identical voltage spike on both wires (common-mode noise). When the receiver subtracts the two wire voltages from each other, the identical noise terms cancel out completely.

What physical condition is required for total internal reflection to trap light inside an optical fiber core?

The glass core must have a higher refractive index than the surrounding cladding glass, and the light ray must strike the boundary at an angle shallower than the critical angle (critical angle = arcsin(n2 / n1)).

How it works: Transmission Line Theory and Impedance

When the transit time of an electrical signal across a cable exceeds roughly one-tenth of its rise time (t_transit > 0.1 * t_rise), wires can no longer be modeled as simple lumped zero-resistance connections. They behave as transmission lines with distributed capacitance (C) and distributed inductance (L) per unit length:

  1. Characteristic Impedance (Z_0): The ratio of voltage to current for a wave propagating along the line: 
    Z_0 = sqrt( L / C )
    Characteristic impedance depends solely on conductor geometry, spacing, and the dielectric constant of the insulating material — not on cable length.
  2. The Reflection Coefficient (Gamma): When a propagating wave encounters a load impedance (Z_L) at the end of the cable, the fraction of voltage reflected back toward the transmitter is: 
    Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0) / (Z_L + Z_0)
    If the line end is open (Z_L = infinity), Gamma = +1 (a wave of equal amplitude reflects back in phase). If the line is shorted to ground (Z_L = 0), Gamma = -1 (the wave reflects back inverted). Only when the terminating resistor exactly matches the cable impedance (Z_L = Z_0) does Gamma = 0, absorbing 100% of the energy without reflections.

Optical link budgets and dispersion

Engineers calculate whether an optical signal can traverse a fiber link using the optical power budget equation:

P_rx = P_tx - (alpha * Length + N_connectors * Loss_connector + N_splices * Loss_splice + Margin)

Where P_tx is minimum laser transmitter output power (in dBm), P_rx is receiver sensitivity, and alpha is fiber attenuation (typically 0.35 dB/km at 1310 nm, or 0.20 dB/km at 1550 nm). If calculated P_rx is lower than the receiver's minimum sensitivity threshold, photons cannot reliably trigger electron transitions in the photodiode, producing high bit error rates. At high gigabit speeds, pulse width expands due to chromatic dispersion (different wavelengths of laser light traveling at slightly different speeds in silica glass), setting an upper limit on modulation symbol rate.

Signal integrity and eye diagrams

To evaluate transmission quality across physical media, engineers probe differential pairs with high-speed digital oscilloscopes to generate an eye diagram (overlaying thousands of consecutive bit transitions triggered on the clock):

  • Eye Height: The vertical opening between high and low voltage levels. Represents the channel's noise margin. A collapsed eye height indicates severe line attenuation or high contact resistance.
  • Eye Width: The horizontal time opening between rising and falling edges. Represents timing jitter. A narrow eye indicates clock instability, Phase-Locked Loop phase noise, or inter-symbol interference (ISI).
  • Eye Mask Violations: Standards define a geometric diamond mask in the center of the eye. If signal ringing, overshoot, or impedance reflections cross into the mask, bit decoding errors are guaranteed.

Why Tamlinux chose this

Understanding transmission line impedance and optical physical layers directly impacts Tamlinux system deployment. From selecting low-loss twinaxial Direct Attach Copper (DAC) cables for 10GbE server interlinks to diagnosing Wi-Fi beacon losses on congested RF channels, hardware maintenance requires knowing the physical constraints of signals. Tamlinux tools expose physical link metrics through unified sysfs interfaces, giving engineers instant access to real-time transceiver health data.

Edge cases and failure modes

  • Optical Receiver Saturation: Connecting a high-power long-range optical transceiver (e.g. 40 km optics with +3 dBm output) directly into a receiver with a short 1-meter patch cord without an inline optical attenuator will overpower the photodiode, causing optical saturation distortion or permanently destroying the receiver semiconductor.
  • Skin Effect at High Frequencies: At gigahertz frequencies, alternating current flows exclusively on the microscopic outer perimeter of a copper conductor (the skin depth is less than 2 micrometers at 1 GHz). This exponentially increases effective AC resistance, causing sharp high-frequency signal attenuation that rounds off square-wave digital edges.
  • Dielectric Absorption and Inter-Symbol Interference (ISI): High-frequency energy absorbed by cheap PCB laminate materials or cable PVC insulation delays discharging, causing residual energy from a previous bit to smear into the next bit period.

What causes signal reflections at the end of an electrical cable, and how does proper termination prevent them?

Signal reflections are caused by an impedance discontinuity — an abrupt change between the cable's characteristic impedance (Z_0) and the load impedance (Z_L) at the connector. When the propagating electromagnetic wave hits a mismatch, boundary conditions dictate that excess energy must reflect back toward the source (Gamma != 0). Placing a terminating resistor equal to Z_0 across the line allows the wave to deposit 100% of its energy as heat in the resistor, completely absorbing the pulse with zero reflected wave.
