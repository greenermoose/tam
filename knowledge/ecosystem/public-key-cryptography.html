Public-Key Cryptography, Elliptic Curves, and PKI

This page explains how two computers agree on a secret over an open network and verify identities using linked public and private keys.

Sharing secrets with strangers without meeting first

Asymmetric encryption (also called public-key cryptography) solves a puzzle that baffled code-makers for centuries: how can two people who have never met—and who can only talk in a room full of eavesdroppers—agree on a secret key or prove who sent a message?

Why it matters

You cannot drive to a website's data center to exchange a secret USB stick before shopping online. Public-key cryptography lets your browser and a server invent a fresh secret key in milliseconds over public Wi-Fi, and lets software authors attach tamper-proof digital signatures to updates.

How to think about it

Imagine a special padlock with two keys: you hand out open copies of the padlock (your public key) to anyone in the world. Anyone can snap that padlock shut on a box, yet only your single private key—kept locked in your pocket—can open it. For signatures, the direction flips: you stamp a wax seal with your private key, and anyone can check that the seal matches your public key.

To know which public key truly belongs to your bank rather than an imposter, a trusted certificate authority signs a digital passport (X.509 certificate) binding the bank's name to its public key.

New words

Asymmetric encryption
Cryptography using a public key for locking/verifying and a separate private key for unlocking/signing.
Diffie-Hellman
A mathematical trick allowing two parties to agree on a shared secret over a public channel.
RSA
An early public-key system built on the difficulty of factoring huge numbers into prime factors.
ECC
Elliptic Curve Cryptography: modern public-key math using points on curves for smaller, faster keys.
Digital signature
A mathematical seal made with a private key and checked by anyone with the public key.
Certificate authority
A trusted organization that signs certificates vouching for who owns a public key.
X.509
The standard digital certificate format used by TLS and HTTPS.

Creating Ed25519 signatures and inspecting X.509 certificates

Goal: you will be able to generate an Ed25519 key pair, sign and verify a scratch file, and inspect the fields inside an X.509 certificate.

Before you start: run all key-generation experiments inside a temporary mktemp -d directory and remove it when finished.

Worked example

Generate an Ed25519 private/public key pair, sign a file, verify the signature, and create and inspect a self-signed X.509 certificate:

$ work=$(mktemp -d) && cd "$work"
$ printf 'Secret message for Tamlinux.\n' > msg.txt
$ openssl genpkey -algorithm Ed25519 -out ed_priv.pem
$ openssl pkey -in ed_priv.pem -pubout -out ed_pub.pem
$ openssl pkeyutl -sign -inkey ed_priv.pem -rawin -in msg.txt -out msg.sig
$ openssl pkeyutl -verify -pubin -inkey ed_pub.pem -rawin -in msg.txt -sigfile msg.sig
Signature Verified Successfully
$ openssl req -x509 -new -key ed_priv.pem -subj "/CN=tamlinux.example" -days 30 -out cert.pem
$ openssl x509 -in cert.pem -noout -subject -issuer -dates -pubkey
subject=CN=tamlinux.example
issuer=CN=tamlinux.example
notBefore=Nov 14 11:41:25 2026 GMT
notAfter=Dec 14 11:41:25 2026 GMT
-----BEGIN PUBLIC KEY-----
MCowBQYDK2VwAyEAZ5sIPnh5KSAKNreUSd9SkiCm33Uxdl7qtT32huXwHpo=
-----END PUBLIC KEY-----
$ rm -rf "$work"

Captured on Arch Linux, 2026-11-14; generated keys and timestamps will differ on your system.

What just happened

  1. openssl genpkey -algorithm Ed25519 created a 256-bit Ed25519 private key in PEM format, and openssl pkey -pubout extracted its 32-byte public key.
  2. openssl pkeyutl -sign signed msg.txt with the private key, and -verify proved the file matched using only ed_pub.pem.
  3. openssl x509 displayed the certificate's subject, issuer, validity window, and embedded public key. Notice that in a real CA-issued certificate, issuer is the CA rather than the subject itself, and hostnames are listed in the Subject Alternative Name (SAN) extension.

Try it

Before deleting your scratch directory, modify one character of msg.txt and rerun openssl pkeyutl -verify to see signature verification fail immediately.

If it goes wrong

Signature Verification Failure
The file was altered after signing, the wrong public key was supplied, or the signature file was truncated.
Self-signed certificate rejected by browsers or curl
Browsers trust only certificates chained to a root CA in the system trust store (/etc/ssl/certs/ on Linux) unless explicitly pinned or trusted.

On Tamlinux, system CA trust anchors live under /etc/ca-certificates/ and /etc/ssl/certs/ (managed via Arch's ca-certificates package), and SSH (SSH keys) defaults to Ed25519 rather than legacy RSA.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

Why do modern systems prefer Ed25519 / X25519 over 2048-bit RSA?

A 256-bit Ed25519/X25519 key achieves roughly the same 128-bit classical security level as a 3072-bit RSA key while being far faster, immune to RSA padding-oracle pitfalls, and deterministic in signing.

If an attacker intercepts your public key, can they derive your private key from it?

Not with classical computers: deriving the private key requires solving integer factorization (RSA) or the discrete logarithm problem on the group/curve (DH/ECC), which is computationally infeasible for properly sized keys.

How it works: algebraic foundations

Extended Euclidean Algorithm & CRT: Given a, b ∈ ℤ, the Extended Euclidean Algorithm computes g = gcd(a, b) and Bézout coefficients x, y such that a·x + b·y = g in O((log a)²) bit operations. When gcd(e, φ(N)) = 1, this yields the modular inverse d = e^(-1) mod φ(N). The Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT) states that for coprime p, q, ℤ_(pq) ≅ ℤ_p × ℤ_q, accelerating RSA private operations by ~4× by computing modulo p and q separately.

RSA & Padding: KeyGen chooses primes p, q, sets N = p·q, φ(N) = (p-1)(q-1), and e·d ≡ 1 (mod φ(N)). Textbook RSA (m^e mod N) is deterministically malleable ((m_1^e)(m_2^e) ≡ (m_1·m_2)^e mod N) and fails IND-CPA. Secure RSA requires randomized padding: RSA-OAEP (Bellare–Rogaway, proved IND-CCA2 in the ROM) for encryption and RSA-PSS (proved EUF-CMA in the ROM) for signatures.

Diffie–Hellman (DH) & ElGamal: In a prime-order subgroup G = ⟨g⟩ of order q, Alice picks a ← ℤ_q, sends g^a; Bob picks b ← ℤ_q, sends g^b; both compute g^(ab). Computing g^(ab) from (g, g^a, g^b) is CDH; distinguishing g^(ab) from random g^c is DDH. Unauthenticated DH is vulnerable to active Man-in-the-Middle (MITM), so protocols bind ephemeral DH shares with signatures or MACs.

Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC): Over a prime field 𝔽_p (p > 3), a Weierstrass curve E: y² ≡ x³ + ax + b (mod p) (with 4a³ + 27b² ≠ 0) plus a point at infinity O forms an abelian group under the chord-and-tangent law. Scalar multiplication [k]P takes O(log k) point additions/doublings, while recovering k from (P, [k]P) (ECDLP) resists subexponential index-calculus attacks, taking O(√q) via Pollard's rho. Curve25519 (y² = x³ + 486662x² + x mod (2^255 - 19)) enables the constant-time x-only Montgomery ladder (immune to branch/cache timing and invalid-curve attacks via clamping), while Ed25519 derives its signing nonce deterministically as r = H(prefix || M), eliminating catastrophic ECDSA nonce-reuse failures.

X.509 & PKI Trust Anchors: X.509 certificates (RFC 5280) encode ASN.1 DER structures binding a DNS SAN to a SubjectPublicKeyInfo. Because any compromised CA could issue a rogue certificate, modern web PKI enforces Certificate Transparency (CT)—append-only Merkle hash trees logging every issued certificate publicly—alongside OCSP stapling and DNS CAA records.

Edge cases and failure modes

  • ECDSA nonce reuse & biased nonces: In ECDSA, signing two messages with the same ephemeral nonce k leaks the private key algebraically: k = (H(m_1) - H(m_2)) / (s_1 - s_2) mod q, then d = (s·k - H(m)) / r mod q. Even a few bits of nonce bias across many signatures falls to lattice reduction (LLL/BKZ) attacks.
  • Bleichenbacher's RSA PKCS#1 v1.5 oracle (1998): Server error differences distinguishing valid vs. invalid PKCS#1 v1.5 padding let an attacker decrypt ciphertexts or forge signatures in ~10^6 adaptive queries—and variants (ROBOT, Marvin) continue to resurface. Always prefer X25519/Ed25519 or OAEP/PSS.

Check yourself

Why do elliptic curves achieve 128-bit security with 256-bit keys, whereas finite-field Diffie-Hellman and RSA require ~3072-bit keys?

In Z_N* and F_p*, subexponential algorithms (Number Field Sieve, index calculus) exploit smooth numbers and algebraic structure in L_N[1/3] time. On well-chosen elliptic curves without special structure (non-supersingular, embedding degree large), no index-calculus subexponential attack is known, leaving generic O(sqrt(q)) Pollard's rho algorithms as the best classical attack.
