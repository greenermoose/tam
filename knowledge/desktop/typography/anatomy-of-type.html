Guide / Desktop and applications / Typography / Anatomy of a letterform

Every letter is drawn against a small set of invisible lines and built from a few named parts. Knowing those names lets you describe why one font reads clearly and another looks cramped.

The shape of one letter

What this is

A glyph is one drawn letter or mark. Letters sit on an imaginary line called the baseline. The height of a small letter such as "x" is its x-height. Letters such as "b" and "d" rise above it with an ascender; letters such as "g" and "p" drop below it with a descender. Capital letters reach a separate cap-height.

Inside a letter, a stroke is a drawn line. A bowl is a curved stroke, and the counter is the open space it surrounds. A straight stroke is often called a stem.

Why it matters

These shapes affect reading. A large x-height can make small text easier to read. An open counter keeps letters such as "e" and "a" from blurring into solid blobs. Even spacing between lines, called leading, gives the eye room to move from one line to the next.

How to think about it

Picture a sheet of ruled exercise paper. The thick rule is the baseline; a shorter rule above it marks the x-height; a higher rule marks the cap-height; a lower rule marks the descender. A letter is a shape fitted between those rules.

Now think of a font as a family of related drawings. The regular drawing, its bold drawing and its italic drawing are members of one font family. Each member is a font face, and the chosen variant is a font style.

New words

Baseline
The line on which most letters rest.
X-height
The height of a typical small letter.
Ascender
The part of a letter that rises above the x-height.
Descender
The part that drops below the baseline.
Bowl
A curved stroke that encloses space.
Counter
The enclosed or partly enclosed white space in a letter.
Serif
A small foot or finish at the end of a stroke.
Sans-serif
Letterforms without those small finishes.

If it goes wrong

You counted the counter as a stroke.
It is space, not ink. A bowl is the curved stroke that surrounds the counter.
You thought em and en were letter parts.
They are units based on the size of the type, not shapes inside a glyph.

These terms matter most in a code editor, where a monospace family is chosen. Tamlinux's fontconfig configuration strongly assigns generic monospace to JetBrainsMono Nerd Font. The command tam-font-current prints the first alias for monospace from fc-match, and the menu uses that result to offer a font selector. A change of x-height or line spacing is therefore visible every time you read code.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

Why can two fonts at the same point size look different in size?

Because point size sets the overall em, not the x-height. A font with a large x-height draws its small letters bigger inside that em, so a paragraph looks larger even at the same nominal size.

What is the difference between italic and oblique?

An italic is a designed letterform, often with different shapes and a true lowercase style. An oblique is the regular letterform slanted by a mathematical transform. Both lean, but only one is redrawn.

How it works

A font stores design coordinates in an em square, then scales them to the requested size. The point is a traditional print unit; the em is the font's own scale, and the en is half an em. OpenType records metrics such as units per em and ascender and descender values in its tables, so the same numbers drive book layout and browser layout OpenType specification.

On the web, CSS exposes these ideas as font-size, line-height and font-variant settings. The specification defines how a chosen font family and font style are matched and how figures and caps are selected CSS Fonts Module Level 4. Accessibility guidance treats adequate spacing and distinguishable letter shapes as part of readability rather than ornament WCAG 2.2. Bringhurst's discussion of size, leading and measure remains the classic statement of the craft The Elements of Typographic Style.

Advanced usage

These read-only queries show how a desktop resolves a family and reports its metrics:

fc-match monospace -f '%{family}\n'
fc-match 'Liberation Serif' -f '%{family} %{style}\n'
fc-list : family | sort -u | head -n 10

The first line answers which family a monospace request resolves to. The second shows the family and style strings fontconfig reports for a named serif. Fontconfig treats family, face and style as separate matching fields, which is why a single family can offer several faces Fontconfig user manual.

Edge cases

  • Stem is a working term for a straight stroke, but it is not always a separate glossary entry; treat it as plain language unless a definition is linked.
  • A counter can be closed, as in "o", or open, as in the upper part of "c". Calling every counter a hole is too narrow.
  • Some italic fonts are drawn with genuinely different letterforms, not just a slant; the lowercase "a" and "g" are common giveaways.
  • Lining figures align by height but may break the rhythm of body text; oldstyle figures sit more comfortably in prose but line up poorly in tables.
  • Tabular figures are not always monospaced overall; only the digits share a fixed width.
  • In a code editor, line height depends on the tallest ascender and deepest descender in the chosen font. Changing only the font size may not fix clipped accents.

Check yourself

You need a column of amounts to line up. Which figures do you choose and why?

Tabular figures, because every digit shares a width so decimal points align. Proportional figures look better in prose but produce ragged columns. If the amounts sit inside body text, lining figures may also help them stand out.

What breaks if leading is set below the ascender-to-descender height?

Adjacent lines overlap. Ascenders can touch descenders from the line above, which makes words hard to separate. The fix is to raise leading or choose a face with shorter extents, not to shrink the text indefinitely.
