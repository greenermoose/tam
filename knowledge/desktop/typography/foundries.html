Guide / Desktop and applications / Typography / Type foundries and the people who make fonts

A foundry is a place where type is made and sold. The word began with metal type and now covers companies and open projects that publish digital fonts you can install today.

Who makes the letters

What this is

Early books were printed with pieces of movable type, each piece carrying one raised letter. Shops that cast and sold those pieces became the first type foundries. Johannes Gutenberg's printing press made such books possible in fifteenth-century Europe, and Johann Fust and Peter Schöffer carried the work on after him.

Today a foundry usually publishes digital fonts instead of metal. Some are companies; others are volunteer projects that give their fonts away. A font family such as Noto is a set of related designs shared under one name.

Why it matters

The foundry behind a font tells you something about its purpose. A family made for newspapers may prioritise compact shapes; one made for screen reading may prioritise open counters and clear spacing. Knowing the source helps you choose a font that fits the job.

How to think about it

Think of a foundry as a kitchen for letters. Some cooks work in metal, some draw shapes for screens, and some add icons to an existing recipe. The finished dish is the font you install; every visible letter in it is a glyph.

Not every well-known name is a foundry. Beatrice Warde wrote about typography rather than running a type shop, and Nerd Fonts patches existing fonts to add icons rather than designing a family from scratch.

New words

Foundry
An organisation that makes and releases type, once in metal and now usually digital.
Printing press
A machine that presses inked type onto paper.
Movable type
Reusable letters that can be rearranged to set new text.
Font family
Related designs issued under one name, such as Noto or Liberation.
Font face
One specific member of a family, such as bold italic.
Nerd Fonts
A project that adds developer icons to existing fonts.
Atkinson Hyperlegible
A family designed for readers with low vision.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

Why is Aldus Manutius still discussed in a page about modern fonts?

Because he helped establish italic type and the portable book format. Those decisions shaped how families are built: a roman and an italic are still treated as related members of one design.

Name one difference between a foundry and a patching project.

A foundry designs and releases fonts as its own work. A patching project such as Nerd Fonts starts from other people's families and adds glyphs, usually icons, to them.

How it works

The chain from Gutenberg to today is a chain of production methods. Gutenberg's metal type worked with a press; Fust and Schöffer turned that into a commercial shop; Manutius added the italic and the small book. Britannica's account gives the sequence of Gutenberg's work and its consequences Gutenberg and the press. Later founders such as Baskerville, the Didots and Bodoni refined the letterform itself, pushing contrast and stroke stress in new directions; Bringhurst describes how such choices affect the page as a whole The Elements of Typographic Style.

Hot metal changed the unit again: Linotype set whole lines, Monotype set individual characters. In the digital era, Adobe, Microsoft and Apple defined competing font technologies, with Type 1 and TrueType later meeting in the shared OpenType format Adobe type notes OpenType specification TrueType Reference Manual. Free open libraries then made type a shared resource rather than a purchased product.

Atkinson Hyperlegible is a specific accessibility project with its own design goals and distribution Braille Institute, Atkinson Hyperlegible. Nerd Fonts differs in kind: it patches an existing family by adding icon glyphs, so it is best described as a project rather than a foundry.

Advanced usage

These read-only commands show which families are actually available and where fontconfig sends a request:

fc-list : family | sort -u | head -n 20
fc-match sans-serif -f '%{family}\n'
fc-match serif -f '%{family}\n'
fc-match monospace -f '%{family}\n'
fc-list | grep -i 'noto\|liberation' | head -n 10

The first line lists installed families; the next three resolve the generic requests that a desktop application makes. The last line finds families from the open libraries in fontconfig's cache. Fontconfig reports what it matches, not which foundry designed the family Fontconfig user manual.

Edge cases

  • A foundry can be a company, a university project or an individual designer. The word no longer implies a physical casting shop.
  • Revivals are new drawings of old designs. A digital Baskerville is not the same object as an eighteenth-century punch.
  • Beatrice Warde is a writer and advocate, not a punchcutter. Classifying her as a foundry would misstate her role.
  • Noto is a family collection rather than one face; it spans many scripts and many weights under one umbrella.
  • Nerd Fonts patches other families, so its result depends on the upstream font. Updates to the source family can change the patched output.
  • Atkinson Hyperlegible follows a stated legibility goal; choosing it for body text is a design decision, not a general rule for all reading.
  • Metric-compatible substitutes such as Liberation keep layout widths similar to common office fonts, so documents reflow less when opened on another system.

Check yourself

Why do Liberation and Noto appear in different parts of a desktop configuration?

They solve different problems. Liberation provides familiar generic families for ordinary text, while Noto supplies wide script coverage and language-specific choices such as Arabic and Urdu. Both are open libraries, but they are selected for different needs.

What is misleading about calling Nerd Fonts a foundry?

It suggests original type design. Nerd Fonts adds icon glyphs to fonts made elsewhere, so the result is a patched family. The distinction matters when you trace where a glyph came from or who designed a letterform.
