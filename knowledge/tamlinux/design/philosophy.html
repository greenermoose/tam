This page explains the ideas that guide the design of Tamlinux.

What principles guide Tamlinux?

Tamlinux is guided by a small set of design principles: keep the workstation calm, keyboard-first, offline-capable, energy-conscious, and understandable from the ground up so its user stays in control.

Why it matters

Design principles explain why Tamlinux chooses simple, durable tools over complex ones—while keeping in mind that a goal is a direction to measure against, not a claim about any single machine.

How to think about it

Think of design principles like a carpenter's rules of thumb: they do not build the chair by themselves, but they guide every choice of wood, joint, and finish so the pieces fit together as one coherent workshop.

Worked example

The four freedoms include the freedom to study and change a program. Compare that with the discussion of its license below.

Tamlinux builds on the free software tradition and on shared desktop standards, and treats resource efficiency as a design brief rather than an afterthought.

Four essential freedoms

The Free Software Foundation describes four freedoms (counting from zero) that users of free software have:

  1. run the program as you wish, for any purpose ("freedom 0")
  2. study how the program works and change it ("freedom 1")
  3. redistribute copies to help other ("freedom 2")
  4. distribute copies of your modified versions ("freedom 3")

Tamlinux is distributed under GPL-3.0-or-later. Copyleft keeps these freedoms attached to every copy and every modified version.

Guiding principles for Tamlinux

Fred (see About Tamlinux) has established seven core engineering and design principles that guide the creation of Tamlinux:

  1. One place to look: Put configuration and information where users naturally expect to find it. Make ownership of configuration discoverable; provide tam as an entry point for documentation and checks. Shell defaults and personal overrides can live in different files.
  2. 1 way to do 15 things, not 15 ways to do 1 thing: Curate packages decisively so users escape the "fiddling trap". Provide one excellent terminal (foot) and one standard shell (bash), eliminating paralyzing choice while providing a broad suite of software for diverse tasks.
  3. Ergonomics matter: Optimize keyboard workflows and minimize keystrokes for common tasks. Design intuitive pager keys, concise CLI syntax, and quick orientation shortcuts.
  4. Consistency counts: Standardize essential keybindings and behavioral contracts so muscle memory transfers reliably across terminal, desktop, and editor.
  5. Zero tolerance policy for bugs: Uncover root causes no matter how deep into the stack it is necessary to go. Verify code with automated test suites, memory audits, and strict compiler enforcement.
  6. Gather good ideas from everywhere: Adopt the best design elements from across computing history—Unix stream purity, Plan 9 plumbing, classic Amiga responsiveness, Mac accessibility, and modern Wayland protocols.
  7. Make computing fun for humans: Don't ask a human to do boring work like test a bunch of things. Instead, have AI agents and scripts do the boring work and let the human play with their workstation. A human will find plenty of bugs just by playing, while scripts and AI agents can do the drudgery. Computing should be fun, not a chore!

Standards and interoperability

Tamlinux follows freedesktop.org (XDG) specifications so that its parts work with the rest of the free desktop:

XDG directoriesconfiguration in ~/.config, data in ~/.local/share, and state in ~/.local/state.
Standard protocolsthe shell talks to the compositor through standard Wayland protocols such as wlr-layer-shell and ext-workspace-v1 wherever they exist.
Standard formatsapplications are described by .desktop files; icons and menus follow shared specifications.

Resource efficiency

Reducing unnecessary work can improve responsiveness or energy use. Verify the effect on the actual workload and hardware:

No polling loopswork is event-driven and bounded; software sleeps until something happens.
No unnecessary daemonsif a feature can be an on-demand command, it should not run permanently in the background.
Self-reportingtools say what they did and what failed, so problems can be diagnosed rather than guessed at.

Human direction

Tamlinux prefers deterministic tools that people direct. AI assistants help develop Tamlinux under human direction, and every public repository records how in its AI-BLAME.md; see AI tooling and human agency.
