Guide / Networking / The IETF and how RFCs work

The IETF writes open internet standards, and RFCs are the numbered documents that freeze those agreements so different machines can implement the same rules.

Who writes the rules of the internet?

When your computer loads a website, checks email, or connects over Wi-Fi, it follows shared technical rules written down by an open community called the IETF (Internet Engineering Task Force). Those written rules are published as free, numbered documents called RFCs (Requests for Comments).

Why it matters

No single company owns the internet. Because the IETF publishes its specifications openly and without paywalls, a Linux laptop, an Android phone, a web server, and a home router built by completely different teams can all understand each other.

How to think about it

Think of RFCs as a public, numbered recipe book for network protocols. Anyone can suggest a new recipe, test it in real software, and point out mistakes before it becomes an official standard. Once an RFC number is printed, that page of the recipe book is never silently rewritten—if a recipe needs a fix years later, the community publishes a new RFC number that points back to the old one.

New words

IETF

Protocol

Check yourself

Can an RFC be edited after publication?

No. Published text is frozen. Corrections become errata and protocol changes receive a new RFC number that points back to the old one.

IETF governance, RFC streams, and standards maturity

Reference: The IETF standards process is codified in BCP 9 (RFC 2026, as updated by RFC 6410 and related BCPs), while the RFC series governance model is defined in RFC 9280.

Organizational structure

  • Working Groups (WGs) and Areas: Technical work happens in chartered WGs grouped under functional Areas (Applications and Real-Time, General, Internet, Operations and Management, Routing, Security, and Web and Internet Transport). Each Area is led by Area Directors (ADs).
  • IESG (Internet Engineering Steering Group): Composed of the Area Directors and the IETF Chair; responsible for technical management of IETF activities, chartering WGs, and approving IETF-stream documents for publication.
  • IAB (Internet Architecture Board): Provides long-range architectural oversight, manages external liaisons, and acts as an appeals body above the IESG.
  • IRTF (Internet Research Task Force): Sister organization focused on longer-term research groups (such as the Crypto Forum Research Group, CFRG, which developed Curve25519 and Argon2 specifications) rather than immediate engineering standards.
  • RFC Production Center (RPC): Copy-edits, formats, assigns permanent numbers, and publishes approved drafts across all four streams under policies set by the RFC Series Approval Board (RSAB) and advised by the RFC Series Consulting Editor (RSCE).

Document lifecycle and maturity levels

An unapproved specification circulates as an Internet-Draft (named draft-ietf-<wg>-<topic>-NN for WG items or draft-<author>-<topic>-NN for individual submissions), which automatically expires after 185 days unless updated. Moving a WG draft to publication requires Working Group Last Call (WGLC), IETF-wide Last Call, an IESG ballot (where any AD may raise a blocking DISCUSS position), and final author review (AUTH48) with the RFC Production Center.

Under RFC 6410 (which reduced the original three-step Standards Track to two steps), Standards Track RFCs have two maturity levels: Proposed Standard (the entry-level specification after thorough review) and Internet Standard (elevated after widespread deployment and interoperable implementations; also assigned an STD number). Other categories are Best Current Practice (BCP) (operational or process rules with a separate BCP series number that can point to newer RFCs over time), Informational, Experimental, and Historic.

Trade-offs and common pitfalls

  • Confusing RFC existence with Standards Track status: An RFC number alone does not make a document a standard. Independent-stream, Informational, Experimental, and Historic RFCs are not IETF consensus standards.
  • Citing obsoleted specifications: Always check the RFC datatracker or Obsoletes:/Updates: headers before implementing; for example, RFC 5246 (TLS 1.2) is obsoleted by RFC 8446 (TLS 1.3), and RFC 2119 is updated by RFC 8174.
  • Misreading BCP 14 requirement keywords: In RFC 2119, SHOULD (RECOMMENDED) does not mean optional; it means valid reasons may exist to ignore an item, but the full implications must be understood and weighed before choosing a different course.
