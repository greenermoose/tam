Guide / Networking / Email / Deliverability and troubleshooting

Deliverability is the practical question of whether mail reaches the mailbox. When it does not, the useful evidence is a bounce, a queue entry, a header trail or a server log — each with limits.

Missing mail has several causes

A message might still be waiting in a queue, rejected by a server, filtered into a quarantine folder, or delivered to the wrong folder. A bounce is a report about a delivery attempt, not proof that a person never saw any copy.

Why it matters

Different causes need different actions. Resending immediately can duplicate mail if the first copy is only delayed. Checking the right evidence avoids making the problem worse.

How to think about it

Track a parcel. A delay notice, a returned parcel and a parcel left at a neighbour's desk are three different events. The same message can produce a delay and then a delivery.

New words

Bounce
A report that a delivery attempt failed.
Deferral
A temporary failure that leaves the message eligible for retry.
Backscatter
Unsolicited bounce messages caused by forged sender addresses.
Received header
A trace line added by a server that handled the message.

What just happened

  1. 451 was a temporary failure, so a retry is the correct action.
  2. 250 meant one receiving server accepted the message, not that the human read it.

Try it

Run grep quarantine trail.txt. Explain why a quarantined message is not the same as a rejected message.

If it goes wrong

You resent a delayed message immediately.
A temporary failure may still be queued. Waiting for the retry policy prevents duplicates.
You replied to a bounce that named an address you never used.
That may be backscatter caused by a forged sender. Reply to a genuine contact through another channel instead.

The guide gives portable diagnostic method, not provider-specific settings. Tamlinux does not add a private mail transport or a special logging format.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

Does a 250 acceptance prove the recipient read the message?

No. It proves one server accepted responsibility at that hop. Filtering, quarantine and mailbox state come later.

Why is a 4xx reply handled differently from a 5xx reply?

A 4xx condition may clear, so the sender keeps the message and retries. A 5xx condition is permanent for that recipient and needs a corrected address or another channel.

How it works

RFC 5321 defines reply-code classes and the model in which a server accepts or defers a message for each recipient. RFC 5598 explains why acceptance at one hop is not the end of delivery. Delivery status notifications report the outcome of a transfer attempt; they are evidence about the transfer, not a read receipt.

Advanced usage

A diagnostic order works well: confirm the envelope recipient, check the queue state, read the first definitive failure code, inspect authentication and Received: lines from the bottom upward, then compare with provider reports. A blocklist listing or rate limit may explain a deferral; an alignment failure may explain a policy rejection. Coordinate with the receiving postmaster when local evidence stops.

Edge cases

  • A message can be accepted and later bounced, producing confusingly successful local logs.
  • Backscatter can fill a mailbox with bounces for addresses forged by someone else.
  • A blocklist entry may be stale or shared; delisting and reputation recovery take time.
  • Privacy rules mean a receiver may not disclose why a message was filtered in detail.

Check yourself

Why read Received headers from the bottom upward?

The lowest line was added nearest to the receiver, so it is the least likely to have been fabricated by the original sender. It gives the most trustworthy starting point for tracing the last hop.
