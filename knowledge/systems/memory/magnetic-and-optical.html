Guide / Systems and administration / Memory technology / Magnetic and optical storage

Magnetic and optical media store data as physical patterns, using magnetism or reflected light. Disks, tape and optical discs have different speed and wear limits.

Spinning disks and shiny discs

A hard drive stores data as tiny magnetic regions on spinning platters. A read/write head floats extremely close to the surface and senses or changes magnetism. An optical disc stores data as patterns that affect how laser light reflects. The player reads those reflections as bits.

Why it matters

These technologies explain why some storage is fast for large files but slow to find a specific place. A disk has to move a head and wait for the right spot to spin under it. An optical drive has to seek and track the laser. That mechanical movement is much slower than electronic memory.

Key terms

Random access
The ability to go to a location without reading everything before it. Disks are more random-access than tape, but still far slower than RAM because they involve mechanical motion.
Latency
The delay before data starts arriving. For a disk, latency includes seek time and rotational delay.

How the media work

Goal: Compare magnetic and optical media by material, read/write mechanism, speed, and risk.

Hard disk drives (HDD)

An HDD has rigid platters coated with a magnetic film. Data is written by a magnetic head that sets tiny regions to one magnetic direction or another. Data is read by sensing the magnetic field changes. Modern drives pack bits extremely densely and use error correction to recover from small defects. Because the platter spins and the head moves, access time is usually in milliseconds. On the human-scale table, a 10 ms disk access is like 115 days if 1 ns were one second.

Floppy disks

A floppy disk uses a flexible plastic disk coated with magnetic material inside a protective shell. It works like a small, removable hard disk but with much lower density, slower speed, and less durability. Floppies were convenient for small files, but they are vulnerable to dust, magnetic fields, wear, and media decay.

CD-ROM, DVD, and Blu-ray

Optical discs use a laser to read patterns on a disc. A pressed CD-ROM has microscopic pits and flat lands molded into a polycarbonate layer with a reflective metal layer. The laser sees changes in reflection as the disc spins. Recordable discs (CD-R/DVD-R) use a dye layer that a writing laser changes. Rewritable discs (CD-RW/DVD-RW) use phase-change materials that switch between reflective and less-reflective states. Blu-ray uses a shorter-wavelength blue-violet laser and smaller features, allowing higher density.

Try it

On Linux, lsblk shows block devices, and udisksctl status or smartctl -a /dev/sdX can show disk information when available. For optical media, lsblk may show a sr0 device when a disc is present.

If it goes wrong

A disc or disk is readable on one machine but not another
Media degradation, dirty optics, weak drives, or marginal sectors can cause inconsistent reads. Copy important data while reads still succeed and verify checksums when possible.
A hard drive sounds unusual or becomes slow
Mechanical drives can fail. Back up data immediately and check SMART health; do not trust a failing drive with the only copy.

Check yourself

Why is a hard drive much slower than RAM even though both can access arbitrary locations?

An HDD needs mechanical motion: moving the head and waiting for rotation. RAM uses electronic addressing with no moving parts, so its latency is orders of magnitude lower.

Environmental risks and radiation

Magnetic media can be corrupted by strong magnetic fields, but ordinary stray background radiation is usually less of a concern than mechanical damage, heat, humidity, and material aging. High-energy radiation, including gamma rays, can damage electronics, controllers, and sensors, and in extreme doses can affect materials; for consumer storage, the more common risks are device failure, media decay, and bit errors. Optical discs avoid magnetic erasure, but they are sensitive to scratches, UV light, heat, and layer separation. No common consumer medium is immune to environmental risk; redundancy and verification matter.

Long-term retention

Retention depends heavily on manufacturing quality and storage conditions. Cool, dry, dark, stable storage helps. Archival-grade optical media and carefully stored tape can last many years, but claims should be treated as estimates, not guarantees. For important data, use multiple copies, multiple media types or locations, and periodic integrity checks.
