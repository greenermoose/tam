Message systems use several different success events. A service may accept a text, queue it, hand it to a network, and receive a report, while the person at the other end may never open it. Reliability means being clear about which event is being claimed.

Sent, delivered, read

When a message shows "sent", the sender's device has usually handed it to a service. "Delivered" normally means a network or device reported that it accepted the message. "Read" is a separate event that happens only when someone opens it, and not every service reports it.

Why it matters

These labels help people decide whether to try again. A message that was accepted but never delivered may need a retry; a message that was delivered but not read may simply be waiting on a person.

How to think about it

Think of a parcel. "Sent" means the depot took it. "Delivered" means the courier left it at the address. "Read" means the recipient opened the box. Those are three different facts.

New words

Acceptance
A service agrees to take responsibility for a message.
Delivery receipt
A report about a delivery attempt.
Retry
Sending again after a temporary failure.
Expiry
Giving up when a message has waited too long.

If it goes wrong

You read "sent" as proof of delivery.
Sent usually means acceptance by the sender's service. Look for a delivery report before claiming the receiver's network accepted it.
You assumed retries never duplicate.
If an acknowledgement is lost, the sender cannot know the earlier attempt succeeded. Systems use message identifiers to reduce duplicates, but cannot guarantee a single visible copy in every app.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

Why can a delivery receipt arrive after a message is already visible?

Reports and content can take different paths or be processed at different times. A receipt is evidence about a network event, not the moment the screen updated.

What is the difference between expiry and failure?

Failure can be a temporary attempt error that a retry may fix. Expiry is a policy decision: the service stops trying after a validity period, so later retries will not happen.

How it works

3GPP TS 23.040 defines short message status reports and validity handling; reports are optional and their exact meaning depends on the network path. SMPP adds message identifiers and delivery receipts for application-submitted traffic. 3GPP TS 23.140 adds delivery and read reports for multimedia messages. Internet apps define their own receipts above the transport. Across all these systems, retries make delivery closer to at-least-once than exactly-once; application-level identifiers are needed when a duplicate would matter.

Edge cases

  • Concatenated short messages can be delivered as segments. One segment may fail while others arrive; the phone may show nothing until all parts are present.
  • Roaming adds a visited network between the home subscriber record and the device. A stale mobility state can cause a failed page even when the message is correctly queued.
  • Push notification success for an internet app does not prove the app fetched the message. The two signals come from different systems.
  • Ordering is not universal. Messages can be stored, retried or routed independently, so a conversation view may need sequence metadata to display them sensibly.

Check yourself

Why is exactly-once delivery difficult across these systems?

A sender learns about success through acknowledgements that can be delayed or lost. If it retries after a lost acknowledgement, the message may arrive twice. True exactly-once behaviour needs cooperation from the receiver, such as stable message identifiers and deduplication.
