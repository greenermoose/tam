Guide / Systems and administration / Mobile communications / Texting and instant messaging / Spam, smishing and abuse

Not every message is welcome or honest. Bulk senders fill inboxes with advertising, and attackers send texts that try to make a person reveal a code or open a harmful link. The same delivery systems that make texting useful also make abuse cheap.

Unwanted and deceptive texts

Spam is an unwanted message sent to many people. Smishing (SMS phishing) is a text that pretends to be from a bank, a courier or a friend so that the reader will click a link or reveal a secret.

Why it matters

A text can look as if it came from a trusted organisation even when the visible name is false. A code sent by text is useful only if the right person still controls the number.

How to think about it

Think of a stranger knocking on your door wearing a uniform badge. The badge is easy to print. You check by asking your own bank or by going to the official app, not by replying to the stranger.

New words

Spam
Unwanted messages sent in bulk.
Smishing (SMS phishing)
A deceptive text that tries to steal information or install something.
Sender ID
The name or number displayed as the sender of a message.
Report
Telling a carrier, app or regulator about an unwanted message.

If it goes wrong

You trusted a familiar display name.
A display name is a label, not proof. Check through the official app or a saved phone number.
You assumed encryption makes a request safe.
An attacker can use an encrypted chat to ask for a secret. Encryption protects transport, not judgement.

Tamlinux does not install a spam filter or choose a carrier. These habits work with any phone or messaging app.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

Why is a short link a warning sign?

It hides the real destination. A reader can check by opening the official app directly instead of following the link from an unexpected message.

What makes a text-based one-time code weaker than an in-app approval?

The code can be obtained through SIM swap, forwarding, social engineering or signalling abuse. An in-app approval can bind the action to a logged-in session and device.

How it works

Application providers submit traffic to message centres through protocols such as SMPP; the sender fields carried there can include a display name that the receiver's device shows without cryptographic proof. Carrier signalling systems such as MAP, specified in 3GPP TS 29.002, move routing and subscriber information between network systems. RCS adds capability discovery and verified business messaging in the GSMA Universal Profile, but a verified badge is a policy control rather than end-to-end content authentication. Filters combine sender reputation, message content, link reputation and user reports; each signal produces false positives as well as catches.

Edge cases

  • A legitimate service can send an urgent text during a real emergency. Teach the habit of independent verification, not blanket distrust of urgency.
  • Short codes and verified sender IDs are namespaces with their own rules. A number that works for one country or carrier may not be reachable elsewhere.
  • End-to-end encryption does not stop an attacker who convincingly asks the reader for a code. Security training must cover both technical and human channels.
  • Blocking by content alone harms accessibility and multilingual users. Reputation and reporting need audit trails and appeals.

Check yourself

Why can verified business messaging reduce spoofing but not eliminate smishing?

It gives the receiver a stronger signal about some senders, but attackers can still use unverified numbers, compromised accounts or lookalike names. The reader still needs an independent way to confirm a sensitive request.
