Guide / Systems and administration / Mobile communications / Texting and instant messaging / Encryption and key management

When people say a chat is encrypted, they may mean different things. The important question is who can read the words, and whether an old message stays secret if a key is stolen later.

Who holds the key?

Encryption turns a readable message into scrambled data. A key is the secret that lets a device turn it back into words. If the service provider holds that key, the provider can read the message. If only the two people's devices hold it, the message is E2EE (End-to-End Encryption).

Why it matters

A message can travel through many systems. Link encryption protects one hop at a time. End-to-end encryption stays with the message from sender to receiver, so a store or relay in the middle cannot read the content.

How to think about it

Think of a locked box. With hop-by-hop protection, each courier opens the box and locks a new one for the next courier. With end-to-end protection, only the two people at the ends have the key, so every courier just carries the same locked box.

New words

Key
A secret value used to lock and unlock protected data.
Transport encryption
Protection for one connection, such as between a phone and a provider.
End-to-end encryption
Protection that only the communicating devices can undo.
Fingerprint
A short code people can compare to check that they share the right key.

If it goes wrong

You assumed transport encryption means the provider cannot read messages.
Transport encryption protects the path to the provider. If the provider holds the content key, it can still read stored messages.
You treated a changed safety number as always malicious.
A new phone, reinstall or restored backup can change keys. Verify out of band before concluding there was an attacker.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

What does end-to-end encryption not hide?

It does not by itself hide metadata such as who contacted whom, when, from which devices and how often. It also does not protect a device after an attacker has already unlocked it or a backup that stores plaintext.

Why compare safety numbers in person or through another trusted channel?

The chat channel could be controlled by the party you are trying to exclude. An independent channel lets two people confirm that no impostor replaced a key.

How it works

The Signal Protocol establishes a session with an authenticated key agreement and then uses a Double Ratchet: each message advances a symmetric key so that compromise of one key does not reveal older messages, and fresh randomness helps recover security after a compromise. MLS (Messaging Layer Security) is specified in RFC 9420 and uses a tree structure to update group keys when members join or leave. Carrier radio and core networks also encrypt traffic on their own links; 5G authentication and key agreement are described in 3GPP TS 33.501. Those protections are not the same as content encryption controlled by the people chatting.

Edge cases

  • Forward secrecy protects past messages after a later key theft. Post-compromise security aims to restore future sessions after an attacker's access ends; not every protocol supplies both.
  • Group membership changes are a hard problem: removing a member must stop future reading without rewriting history that the former member already stored.
  • Verified safety numbers can change legitimately when a device is re-registered. Verification systems need a policy for what to do after a change, not just a warning.
  • Metadata can be as sensitive as content. Research systems try to reduce it through private information retrieval, padding and mix networks, with latency and bandwidth costs.

Check yourself

Why is group encryption harder than one-to-one encryption?

Every membership change changes who should hold future keys. A scheme must add and remove members efficiently, keep old messages readable to the intended group, and prevent a removed member from reading new conversations, all without trusting the server with content keys.
