Guide / Systems and administration / Mobile communications / Texting and instant messaging / What is texting?

Texting is sending short written messages between phones or apps. The person sending a text does not need the receiver to be online at that exact moment, because a service keeps the message until the other side can receive it.

Words that wait

When you type a text, you use a program on your phone called a texting app. That app hands the words to a service. The service finds the other person, keeps a copy for a while and delivers it. The other person reads the words in their own app, perhaps much later.

Why it matters

People carry phones that are switched off, out of signal or in another country. A text can still be waiting when the phone comes back. That is why texting is more like leaving a note than making a phone call.

How to think about it

Think of a noticeboard with a clerk. You give the clerk a note with a name on it. The clerk writes it down, finds the right noticeboard and pins it there. You can go home before the other person arrives to read it.

New words

Texting app
The program a person uses to write, send and read messages.
Message service
A system in the background that carries and stores messages.
Store and forward
Accepting a message, keeping it safely, and sending it onward when possible.
Carrier
A company that runs the mobile radio network a phone uses.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

Why can a sender finish before the receiver is online?

Because a service stores the message and forwards it later. Store and forward does not require both people to be connected at the same moment.

Is the app on the phone the same thing as the message service?

No. The app is what a person sees and uses. A separate service carries, stores and delivers the message; one app may use more than one service.

How it works

A text is not one protocol. The word covers several service models with different trust boundaries. A carrier short message uses 3GPP TS 23.040; multimedia uses 3GPP TS 23.140; IMS (IP Multimedia Subsystem) underpins RCS as described by 3GPP TS 23.228. An internet messaging app instead uses ordinary IP connectivity and its provider's own service, which is why it is called over the top.

Edge cases

  • "Delivered" can mean accepted by a service, stored for the receiver, or displayed on a device. These are different events.
  • A message may cross more than one store. Retention, retry and privacy rules differ at each store.
  • An app may fall back from a rich route to a carrier message; the visible conversation can hide that change.

Check yourself

What is lost if a history of texting is taught as one protocol?

The model erases the boundaries that explain authentication, delivery, cost and privacy. A short message, a rich chat and an app message can look identical on screen while having different stores, identifiers and failure modes.
