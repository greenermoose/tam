Guide / Systems and administration / Mobile communications / Texting and instant messaging / SIM cards and subscriber identity

The small card in a phone tells a mobile network which subscription the phone is using. A phone can change without the subscription changing, and a subscription can move to another phone, because identity and hardware are recorded separately.

A key for an account

A SIM (Subscriber Identity Module) is a small secure card. It holds a number that names the subscription and a secret that the network can check. An eSIM (embedded Subscriber Identity Module) does the same job but is built into the phone instead of being a removable card.

Why it matters

Without this identity, the network would not know which account should pay for calls and data, or which texts belong to which person. Taking the card out of one phone and putting it in another moves the subscription, not the phone itself.

How to think about it

Think of a library card. The card identifies the borrower; the book bag they carry is not the borrower. A phone is like the bag, and the SIM is like the card that says whose account it is.

New words

SIM
A card that holds subscription identity and a secret used to prove it.
IMSI
A number stored on the card that names the subscriber in mobile networks.
IMEI
A number that names the phone hardware itself.
Roaming
Using another carrier's network while keeping your own subscription.

If it goes wrong

You treated the phone number and IMSI as the same thing.
A phone number is an address other people dial or text. The IMSI is an internal subscriber identity. A later page covers address translation.
You assumed roaming moves the account to the visited network.
The visited network gives radio and local access; the home network keeps the subscription record and authorisation.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

If a person moves their SIM to a new phone, what identity changed?

The subscription stayed the same and the hardware changed. The network still sees the same subscriber, while the phone's equipment identity is different.

Why is a SIM swap dangerous for text-based codes?

An attacker who convinces the carrier to move the subscription to their SIM can receive texts intended for the victim. The code may be valid even though the account holder never got it.

How it works

A USIM application on the card holds the subscriber identifiers and a long-lived key. The card performs authentication so that the secret need not be exposed to the phone's general software. 3GPP TS 31.102 defines the USIM application. In 5G, the permanent subscriber identity is concealed before it is sent over the air; the home network can recover it, while a passive observer should not be able to read it directly. Authentication and key agreement are specified in 3GPP TS 33.501.

Edge cases

  • The phone number, IMSI and device identity answer different questions. Confusing them produces wrong conclusions about routing, billing and fraud.
  • SIM swap is an account takeover problem, not a flaw in radio encryption: the network legitimately moves the subscription to different credentials.
  • eSIM removes the physical card swap but not the need for strong carrier proof of identity when a profile is transferred.

Check yourself

Why conceal a permanent subscriber identifier on the radio link?

A stable identifier lets an observer track a person across cells and sessions even when message contents are encrypted. Concealment reduces that tracking surface; it does not by itself hide all metadata or stop lawful or fraudulent identity recovery at the network.
