Opal (TCG Opal Self-Encrypting Drive)

Opal (TCG Opal) is a Trusted Computing Group standard for hardware-accelerated full-disk encryption built directly into NVMe and SATA solid-state drives.

Normally, encrypting a drive makes your computer’s main processor scramble and unscramble every file as it is saved or read. A TCG Opal drive works more like a self-locking safe: a chip inside the SSD itself scrambles all data automatically and only unlocks it when you enter the right passphrase at startup. That keeps encryption fast, though it means you are trusting the drive maker’s built-in chip rather than open-source software.

On Linux, cryptsetup (LUKS2) and utilities like sedutil can configure TCG Opal locking ranges so the SSD performs AES encryption in hardware. In practice, however, many Linux users and distributions prefer pure software encryption with dm-crypt (AES-XTS accelerated by CPU AES instructions): drive firmware is proprietary and cannot be audited, past firmware bugs in several self-encrypting drives have broken security guarantees, and software encryption is easier to inspect and move between drives.

Why do many Linux security setups prefer software dm-crypt over TCG Opal hardware encryption?

TCG Opal relies on closed, proprietary drive firmware that users cannot audit and that has suffered severe implementation flaws in the past, whereas modern CPUs make software dm-crypt fast, transparent, and independent of drive hardware.

Defined by the TCG Storage Architecture Core and Opal SSC 2.0 specifications, Opal divides an SED into configurable LBA locking ranges, a shadow MBR for pre-boot authentication (PBA), and a Physical Presence SID (PSID) revert mechanism, wrapping Media Encryption Keys (MEKs) inside the controller. Linux exposes Opal management via block/sed-opal.c (IOC_OPAL_* ioctls) and cryptsetup --hw-opal. Key operational and security trade-offs include unverifiable controller firmware (highlighted by historical flaws such as weak key derivation or wear-leveling metadata exposure) and S3 sleep/resume handling, where the kernel must cache and replay unlock credentials to re-open locking ranges after drive power loss.
