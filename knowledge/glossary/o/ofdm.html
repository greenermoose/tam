OFDM (Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing)

OFDM (Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing) is a radio transmission technique that splits a wide channel into many closely spaced subchannels sent at the same time.

Imagine trying to shout a long message across a gymnasium: if you talk too fast, the echoes from the walls blur your words together. OFDM solves this by having a whole choir sing different parts of the message slowly at the same time on carefully chosen pitches that do not clash. In Wi-Fi and cellular networks, splitting one wide radio channel into many slower subchannels keeps data clear and reliable even when signals bounce off walls and furniture.

OFDM is the modulation foundation behind modern Wi-Fi (802.11a/g/n/ac/ax/be), 4G/5G cellular, and DOCSIS 3.1 cable internet. Because each subcarrier carries a much lower symbol rate than a single wide carrier, indoor multipath reflections cause far less blur between consecutive symbols. Newer standards such as Wi-Fi 6 (802.11ax) extend this to OFDMA, assigning different groups of subcarriers to different devices simultaneously, which you can observe in Linux wireless diagnostics (for example, iw dev wlan0 link showing MCS rates and channel width).

Why does splitting one fast radio stream into many slower subcarriers help indoors?

Indoors, radio waves bounce off walls and arrive slightly delayed, smearing symbols together. Slower symbols on each narrow subcarrier are much longer than those echo delays, so the echoes do not overlap into the next symbol.

OFDM synthesizes a time-domain symbol by applying an Inverse Fast Fourier Transform (IFFT) to complex QAM-mapped frequency-domain subcarriers and demodulates it with an FFT at the receiver. Orthogonality holds when subcarrier spacing equals the reciprocal of the useful symbol duration (Δf = 1/T_u), allowing overlapping sinc spectra with zero inter-carrier interference (ICI) at sampling instants. Prepending a cyclic prefix (CP) longer than the channel’s maximum delay spread converts linear channel convolution into circular convolution, eliminating inter-symbol interference (ISI) and enabling simple single-tap frequency-domain equalization per subcarrier using pilot tones. Key trade-offs include high Peak-to-Average Power Ratio (PAPR) requiring linear RF power-amplifier backoff and sensitivity to carrier-frequency offset and phase noise.
