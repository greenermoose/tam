OLED - Organic Light-Emitting Diode

An OLED (Organic Light-Emitting Diode) display is a screen whose individual pixels generate their own light instead of relying on a separate backlight.

A traditional LCD screen works like a bright flashlight shining behind colored window blinds—even when the screen shows black, some light still leaks through. An OLED screen is more like a grid of millions of tiny colored lightbulbs: to show black, a pixel simply turns completely off. That gives you deep, inky blacks, vivid contrast, and thinner screens that can even bend.

Because OLED panels are emissive and have no backlight, power draw depends directly on what is on screen: dark themes and black pixels use very little energy, while bright full-screen white windows draw the most. Pixel response times are also near-instantaneous, avoiding the motion blur seen on slower LCDs. On Linux laptops with OLED panels, practical habits like auto-hiding static status bars, using screen blanking, and avoiding fixed bright icons for hours help reduce uneven pixel wear over time.

Why does a dark theme save battery on an OLED screen more than on a standard LCD?

On a standard LCD, the backlight stays lit across the panel even for dark areas. On an OLED screen, each pixel emits its own light, so black or very dark pixels draw little or no current.

An OLED panel sandwiches electroluminescent organic emitter layers between an anode and cathode, driven per pixel by an active-matrix TFT backplane (commonly LTPS or variable-refresh LTPO). Non-standard subpixel geometries—such as diamond PenTile—can introduce color fringing on sharp text unless subpixel hinting is tuned or disabled in FreeType and Wayland compositors. Key hardware trade-offs include differential emitter aging (burn-in, fastest in blue subpixels) mitigated by pixel shifting, wear-leveling compensation tables, and Automatic Brightness Limiting (ABL) at high average picture levels, as well as low-frequency PWM flicker at low brightness on panels without DC dimming.
