Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with O / OCP (Over-Current Protection)

A power-supply safeguard that cuts or limits electrical current before wires, connectors, or chips overheat.

Just like a circuit breaker in your house trips if too many appliances draw power at once, OCP watches how much electricity flows through a USB port or computer power supply. If a damaged cable or short circuit tries to pull dangerous amounts of current, OCP shuts the power off in milliseconds to prevent melted wires or fried components.

In PC power supplies (PSUs), USB-C Power Delivery (PD) ports, and motherboard voltage regulators, OCP differs from Over-Voltage Protection (OVP) and Over-Temperature Protection (OTP): OCP specifically monitors current (amps) on a rail or port. On Linux laptops, a tripped USB-C OCP event often shows up in dmesg or journalctl -k as a port power-cycle or over-current warning from the USB hub or typec driver, which usually clears once the faulty device is unplugged.

How does OCP differ from a traditional glass fuse?

A traditional fuse melts permanently when overloaded and must be replaced. OCP is an active electronic circuit that detects excess current in microseconds and either limits current or shuts down the output temporarily (“hiccup” or latched mode), allowing recovery once the fault is cleared.

OCP is implemented via a low-side or high-side current-sense shunt resistor, Hall-effect sensor, or lossless MOSFET R_DS(on) sensing feeding a fast comparator against a threshold I_ocp. Upon tripping, power stages enter either hiccup mode (periodic soft-start retries, reducing average dissipation during a sustained short) or latched shutdown (requiring PS_ON# or AC power-cycle to reset). On multi-rail ATX PSUs, each 12 V rail has an independent I_ocp limit—meaning high-transient GPU load spikes (exceeding 2× TDP for tens of microseconds) can false-trip poorly balanced rails even when total wattage is within the PSU’s continuous rating.
