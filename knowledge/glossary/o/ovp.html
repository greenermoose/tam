OVP (Over-Voltage Protection)

OVP (Over-Voltage Protection) is a hardware safeguard that disconnects or clamps electrical power when voltage rises above a safe limit.

Think of OVP like an automatic floodgate on a canal: if a sudden surge pushes the water level dangerously high, the gate slams shut before downstream homes are flooded. Inside phone chargers, USB-C ports, and computer motherboards, OVP watches the incoming voltage and cuts power in an instant if a faulty adapter or surge sends too much voltage toward delicate chips.

Modern USB-C Power Delivery (USB-PD) ports negotiate anything from 5 V up to 20 V or 48 V on the same power pins, so laptops and motherboards pair OVP with Over-Current Protection (OCP) to survive misbehaving chargers or shorted cables. Unlike ESD (electrostatic discharge) diodes—which shunt brief, multi-kilovolt static zaps for nanoseconds—OVP is built to detect and disconnect sustained or millisecond-scale over-voltage conditions on power rails.

How does Over-Voltage Protection (OVP) differ from ESD protection on a USB-C port?

ESD protection clamps ultra-brief, high-voltage static shocks lasting nanoseconds, whereas OVP monitors power rails for sustained or charger-fault over-voltage and disconnects or clamps the supply before downstream components overheat or break down.

OVP stages typically use either a series pass-FET / eFuse disconnect—where a fast comparator compares the rail against a precision reference and drives back-to-back MOSFETs off within microseconds—or a shunt/crowbar clamp (TVS, Zener, or SCR) that shorts excess voltage to ground and trips an upstream fuse. Designers must balance trip threshold (V_ovp) and hysteresis against normal USB-PD voltage transitions and inductive cable ringing, ensure front-end switches carry sufficient breakdown rating (for example, 28 V–60 V on USB-PD sinks), and stay within the MOSFET’s Safe Operating Area (SOA) during fault turn-off.
