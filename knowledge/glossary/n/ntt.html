NTT (Number Theoretic Transform)

A fast mathematical transform over finite fields used to multiply large polynomials quickly in lattice-based cryptography.

Modern post-quantum encryption protects data using math problems built from long lists of numbers called polynomials. Multiplying two long lists directly takes a huge number of steps, which would make every secure connection feel sluggish. NTT is a clever shortcut—like sorting and pairing numbers before multiplying—that turns heavy work into microseconds.

By converting polynomials from coefficient form into point-value form modulo a prime q, NTT lets two degree-n polynomials be multiplied element-by-element in O(n log n) operations instead of O(n²). Unlike the standard Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), which uses complex floating-point numbers and suffers from rounding errors, NTT uses exact integer arithmetic modulo q—which is mandatory for cryptography where a single rounding bit would break decryption.

Why does lattice cryptography use NTT instead of a standard floating-point FFT?

Cryptographic operations must be exact and deterministic across all CPUs. A floating-point FFT introduces rounding errors that can corrupt key recovery or decryption, whereas NTT operates strictly on integers modulo a prime q with zero precision loss.

NTT computes the Discrete Fourier Transform over a finite polynomial ring such as Z_q[X]/(X^n + 1) (the negacyclic ring used in NIST FIPS 203 ML-KEM and FIPS 204 ML-DSA) by evaluating at primitive 2n-th roots of unity in Z_q (requiring q ≡ 1 mod 2n, or an incomplete NTT when q ≡ 1 mod n). Butterfly stages (Cooley-Tukey for forward, Gentleman-Sande for inverse) execute in-place with precomputed twiddle factors in Montgomery or Barrett form to avoid expensive integer division. Because secret-dependent branches or variable-time modular reductions would leak key bits via timing side channels, production implementations must be strictly constant-time and vectorize butterflies across AVX2 or NEON lanes.
