NOR (NOR gate / NOR flash)

A fundamental digital logic gate (NOT-OR) and a type of non-volatile flash memory commonly used to store motherboard firmware.

The word NOR refers to two related ideas in computing. As a building-block logic rule, NOR means “neither”: its output turns on only when every input is off. As a memory chip, NOR flash is the small, reliable chip on a computer’s motherboard that holds the startup instructions (UEFI or BIOS) so the machine knows how to wake up when you press the power button.

In hardware, NOR flash is very different from the NAND flash inside SSDs and USB drives. While NAND offers high capacity at low cost by reading whole pages or blocks, SPI NOR flash supports fast, byte-level random reads—letting the CPU fetch early boot instructions directly—at the cost of smaller capacity and slower writes. On Linux, you may see motherboard or embedded NOR chips exposed via mtd (/dev/mtd*) or read with flashrom.

Why do motherboards store UEFI/BIOS firmware on NOR flash instead of cheaper NAND flash?

NOR flash allows reliable random-access reads of individual bytes and execute-in-place (XIP) right after power-on, before RAM and complex storage controllers are initialized. NAND flash requires block-oriented controller logic and error correction just to read early code.

As a Boolean operator ~(A | B), NOR is functionally complete—any combinational or sequential logic circuit can be constructed solely from NOR gates. In memory architecture, NOR flash wires memory cells in parallel to bitlines, enabling random-access read latencies in tens of nanoseconds and XIP over parallel or Quad/Octal SPI buses, whereas NAND wires cells in series for higher density. NOR flash erases entire sectors (typically 4 KiB to 64 KiB) to 1s and programs bits from 1 to 0, so firmware updates require read-modify-erase-write cycles and carry bricking risk if power is lost mid-erase without dual-bank or A/B failover.
