NFC (Near Field Communication)

A very short-range wireless standard operating at 13.56 MHz, used for tap-to-pay cards, badges, and hardware security keys.

NFC lets two devices exchange small bits of data when you hold them just a couple of centimeters apart—like tapping a transit card on a turnstile or tapping a security key against the back of a phone. Because it only works at touch-level distance, someone across the room cannot accidentally trigger or intercept the tap.

On Linux and mobile devices, NFC is most commonly encountered with FIDO2/WebAuthn hardware security keys (which often support both USB-C and NFC) and smartcards. Unlike Bluetooth, NFC requires no pairing step and can power a passive tag or security key directly from the reader’s magnetic field, though data rates are low (typically 106 to 424 kbit/s) and range is under ~4 cm.

Why does an NFC security key work without a battery, unlike a Bluetooth key?

An NFC reader emits a 13.56 MHz magnetic field that induces electric current in the key’s coil antenna, powering the chip for the duration of the tap. Bluetooth devices must power their own radio transceiver.

NFC builds on ISO/IEC 14443 (Types A/B), JIS X 6319-4 (FeliCa), and ISO/IEC 18092 using inductive coupling at 13.56 MHz with ASK/BPSK modulation and load modulation for passive-target replies. For authentication, CTAP2 over NFC uses ISO-DEP (ISO 14443-4) APDUs with command chaining when payloads exceed the frame size. On Linux, NFC readers typically expose CCID over USB (pcscd) or kernel nfc subsystem sockets (AF_NFC), and timing out during multi-APDU cryptographic operations is a frequent failure mode if the user pulls the token away too quickly.
