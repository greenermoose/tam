NRZ (Non-Return-to-Zero)

A binary line coding method that represents 1s and 0s as two steady voltage levels held for the entire bit period without returning to zero between bits.

To send digital 1s and 0s over a wire, you have to turn them into electrical voltages. NRZ is the simplest rule: hold the voltage high for a 1, hold it low for a 0, and stay at that level for the whole duration of each bit. Because it never pauses at zero in between, it sends data cleanly—unless there is a long stretch of identical bits with no change at all.

Raw NRZ has a practical catch: if a transmitter sends a long string of 0s or 1s, the voltage stays flat with no edges. Without edges, the receiver’s clock recovery circuit can drift out of sync, and long-term DC voltage can build up on AC-coupled lines. Real-world links (like USB, SATA, or Ethernet) solve this by pairing NRZ with bit stuffing, scrambling, or block coding such as 8b/10b or 64b/66b to guarantee frequent transitions.

What goes wrong if you send a long stream of 1s over an AC-coupled link using raw NRZ?

Without voltage transitions, the receiver cannot resynchronize its clock (losing bit timing), and the sustained high voltage causes baseline wander across coupling capacitors, eventually causing bit errors.

NRZ (2-level pulse-amplitude modulation, PAM-2) maps each bit to one of two symbols (+V/-V in polar NRZ-L, or transition-on-1 in NRZ-I) at a symbol rate equal to the bit rate, yielding a Nyquist bandwidth of f_bit / 2. Its power spectral density has a sinc^2 envelope peaking at DC, causing severe DC wander over transformer- or capacitor-coupled channels and unbounded run lengths (k consecutive identical symbols). High-speed serial PHYs (PCIe, 10GbE, SATA) bound run length and flatten the spectrum using DC-balanced line codes (8b/10b with 20% overhead, or 64b/66b with 3.125% overhead plus a self-synchronizing scrambler), while newer PHYs (PCIe 6.0, 400GbE) move to PAM-4 to double bits per symbol when channel attenuation limits baud rate.
