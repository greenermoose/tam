VRM (Voltage Regulator Module)

A VRM (Voltage Regulator Module) is the power-delivery circuitry on a motherboard or graphics card that steps high supply voltage down to the low, steady voltage required by a processor.

A computer’s power supply delivers around 12 volts, which is far too high for a modern CPU that needs roughly 1 volt—just as household water mains must be regulated before reaching delicate plumbing. The VRM is the row of small blocks and metal cylinders next to the processor socket that converts 12 volts into clean, steady ~1-volt power, even when the CPU suddenly jumps from idle to full speed.

Most motherboard VRMs use a multi-phase design: several parallel stages take turns delivering current, which spreads heat and smooths out voltage ripples. Under heavy sustained loads, poorly cooled VRMs can overheat and force the CPU to throttle its clock speed even when CPU temperature itself looks fine. VRMs also coordinate with the CPU’s power-management firmware to raise or lower voltage dynamically as P-states change.

Why might a CPU drop to very low clock speeds under load even when its own core temperature is well below 70 °C?

If the motherboard’s VRM MOSFETs overheat due to weak heatsinks or poor airflow, the VRM controller asserts a thermal-throttling signal (such as PROCHOT) to protect its power stages, forcing the CPU to reduce frequency and current draw.

A processor VRM is a multi-phase synchronous DC-DC buck converter governed by a digital PWM controller communicating with the CPU over high-speed serial telemetry (Intel SVID or AMD SVI2/SVI3). Each phase comprises high-side and low-side MOSFETs (or integrated DrMOS smart power stages), an output inductor, and bulk/MLCC decoupling capacitors. Interleaving N phases at 360°/N phase shift reduces input/output ripple current and improves transient response to steep load steps (di/dt), while programmed load-line impedance (droop) intentionally lowers Vcore slightly with increasing current to absorb overshoot when load drops abruptly.
