Voltage

Voltage is the electrical pressure that pushes electric charges through a circuit.

Think of electricity flowing through a wire like water flowing through a garden hose. Voltage is the water pressure pushing the flow along: a 1.5-volt AA battery has gentle pressure, while a wall outlet has much higher pressure. Devices like laptops and CPUs are built for specific voltages, so too little voltage can make them unstable and too much can permanently damage their parts.

Measured in volts (V), voltage is always a potential difference between two points—such as a +12 V power rail and ground (0 V)—where 1 V equals 1 joule of energy per coulomb of charge. In PCs, power supplies deliver standard rails (+12 V, +5 V, +3.3 V), while motherboard regulators step voltage down to roughly 0.7–1.4 V for the CPU. Under Linux, tools like sensors (via lm-sensors) or upower let you monitor rail and battery voltages.

Why can a CPU run at a lower voltage when idle than when boosting to maximum frequency?

Higher clock frequencies require transistors to switch faster, which demands a higher supply voltage so signals cross logic thresholds within a shorter clock period. When idle at low frequency, lowering the voltage (DVFS) keeps switching reliable while cutting dynamic power, which scales with the square of voltage.

Formally, voltage is the line integral of the electric field between two points (in electrostatics, the negative gradient of scalar potential). In digital CMOS circuits, rail voltage (Vdd) sets noise margins, propagation delay, and power: dynamic switching power scales as C · Vdd² · f, while subthreshold leakage grows exponentially with temperature and threshold reduction. Modern processors use dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS) and load-line calibration (droop compensation) to keep Vcore within tight millivolt tolerances during transient current steps (di/dt) without violating minimum Vmin or maximum Vmax reliability limits.
