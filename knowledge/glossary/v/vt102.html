Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with V / VT102 (DEC Video Terminal)

The VT102 was a 1982 Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) hardware terminal whose text-control escape sequences became the baseline model for the Linux kernel console.

Before personal computers had graphical screens, people used dedicated desk machines called terminals—just a monitor and keyboard wired to a shared mainframe. The DEC VT102 was a popular 1982 terminal that understood special text codes for moving the cursor, clearing the screen, and highlighting words. Even today, Linux’s built-in text console still speaks the VT102’s language so old and new command-line tools work reliably.

On Linux, the kernel’s built-in virtual console (TERM=linux) implements a subset of VT102 and ECMA-48 (ANSI) control sequences rather than every feature of modern graphical terminal emulators. That is why full-screen programs like vim, less, or htop check the TERM variable and terminfo database: features that work in an xterm-256color window may fall back to simpler colors or attributes on a raw VT102-style console.

Why might a terminal program look different or lose 256-color styling on a raw Linux text console compared to a desktop terminal window?

The Linux text console emulates a VT102-era subset (TERM=linux) with a small hardware palette, whereas desktop terminal emulators advertise richer capabilities (like xterm-256color) in terminfo. Programs adapt their escape sequences to match what TERM reports.

Released in 1982 as a cost-reduced VT100 integrating the Advanced Video Option (AVO) and a bidirectional printer port, the DEC VT102 standardized ANSI/ECMA-48 CSI sequences (such as ESC [ H, ESC [ J, ESC [ K, and SGR attributes) for 80/132-column video terminals. The Linux kernel’s drivers/tty/vt/vt.c parser—documented in console_codes(4)—models VT102 state transitions (G0/G1 character sets, DEC private modes via CSI ? ... h/l, and scrolling margins) while adding Linux-specific private sequences (ESC [ ... ]) for palette and cursor shape.
