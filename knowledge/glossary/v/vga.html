Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with V / VGA - Video Graphics Array

VGA (Video Graphics Array) is an older analog display standard and connector for sending video signals from a computer to a monitor.

If you have ever plugged a thick cable with a blue, three-row, 15-pin plug into an older monitor or projector, that is a VGA cable. Unlike modern cables such as HDMI or DisplayPort, which send crisp digital numbers, VGA sends continuous electrical waves—like an old analog radio signal. Because those waves can pick up interference along the cable, a loose or cheap VGA connection can make a screen look slightly blurry, wavy, or tinted.

In Linux, you still encounter VGA in two places: on older hardware or server BMCs (Baseboard Management Controllers), and as a kernel/bootloader fallback mode (such as vga= or nomodeset/efifb before a KMS driver loads). Unlike DisplayPort or HDMI, VGA carries no audio and requires the monitor’s analog-to-digital converter to sample pixels to a clock, so misconfigured timings can leave an LCD image shifted or shimmering until auto-adjust runs.

Why can a VGA cable display a blurry or ghosted image at high resolutions while an HDMI cable does not?

VGA transmits continuous analog voltage waveforms for red, green, and blue rather than discrete digital packets. High pixel clocks require fast voltage transitions that attenuate, reflect, or pick up electromagnetic interference over long or poorly shielded cables, whereas HDMI carries digital symbols that either decode cleanly or drop out.

Introduced by IBM in 1987, VGA defines both a 640×480 16-color (and 320×200 256-color) register-compatible graphics architecture and an analog RGBHV electrical interface on a DE-15 connector. Video signals are 0.7 Vpp into 75 Ω with separate TTL horizontal and vertical sync pulses; VESA DDC2B adds an I2C bus (pins 12 and 15) so the GPU can read the monitor’s EDID. Without valid EDID or with a damaged DDC pin, the Linux DRM subsystem falls back to conservative DMT/GTF timings (typically 1024×768 or 800×600). Modern GPUs omit integrated RAMDACs, so VGA output today requires an active DisplayPort-to-VGA or HDMI-to-VGA protocol converter.
