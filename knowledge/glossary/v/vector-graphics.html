Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with V / Vector graphics

Vector graphics describe images as mathematical shapes, lines and curves rather than a fixed grid of colored dots.

Think of the difference between a mosaic of square tiles and a recipe for drawing a circle. Zoom into a photo and you see squares; a vector is the recipe, saying 'draw a blue circle of this size with a black outline'. The computer redraws it sharply at any size.

Common vector formats include SVG for web and icons, PDF and PostScript for documents, and TrueType or OpenType outlines for fonts. Desktop icons stay crisp at 100% and 200% display scales without separate image files. Photographs are poor vector candidates because millions of subtle color variations are costly to describe as shapes.

Why do UI icons and logos usually use SVG while camera photos stay raster?

Icons are clean geometric shapes that redraw sharply; photos have millions of irregular pixel-level colors that vectors cannot represent compactly.

Vector scenes use resolution-independent primitives: line segments, quadratic and cubic Bézier curves, transforms, stroke caps and joins, and fill rules such as non-zero winding or even-odd. These are rasterized only at display or print time, with coverage sampling or multisampling to prevent jagged edges. Font outlines are vectors until a rasterizer produces glyph bitmaps; SVG also brings an XML and scripting surface that static image formats do not have.
