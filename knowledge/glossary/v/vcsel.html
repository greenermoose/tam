Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with V / VCSEL (Vertical-Cavity Surface-Emitting Laser)

A VCSEL (Vertical-Cavity Surface-Emitting Laser) is a compact, low-cost semiconductor laser that shines light straight up from the surface of a chip, widely used in short-range fiber optics and optical sensors.

Most traditional laser chips shine light out of their cut edge, which makes them harder to test and align. A VCSEL shines light straight out of the top face of the silicon wafer, like a tiny upward-pointing flashlight. Because thousands can be tested right on the wafer before cutting them apart, they are inexpensive and power-efficient—making them common inside short data-center fiber cables, optical mice, and phone depth sensors.

In networking, 850 nm VCSELs are the standard light source inside short-reach multimode transceivers (such as 10GBASE-SR SFP+ and 25G/100G SR4 QSFP28 modules) paired with aqua OM3 or OM4 fiber over roughly 100–400 meters. Unlike long-reach 1310 nm or 1550 nm single-mode transceivers—which use edge-emitting lasers and cost much more—VCSELs cannot travel kilometers because modal dispersion in multimode fiber spreads the light pulses out. You can check transceiver type, wavelength, and optical transmit/receive power on Linux with ethtool -m eth0.

Why do data centers use 850 nm VCSEL multimode transceivers inside a rack or hall, but switch to edge-emitting single-mode optics between buildings?

VCSELs are cheap and low-power for short distances, but multimode fiber suffers from modal dispersion that limits reach to a few hundred meters; single-mode edge-emitting lasers avoid this dispersion and travel kilometers.

A VCSEL forms a vertical optical cavity perpendicular to the substrate between two epitaxially grown Distributed Bragg Reflectors (DBRs)—alternating quarter-wavelength layers of high/low refractive index semiconductors (typically AlGaAs/GaAs for 850 nm)—sandwiching a multi-quantum-well active region. Because the cavity length is on the order of one wavelength, it supports a single longitudinal mode with sub-milliampere threshold current and a circular, low-divergence beam that couples efficiently into 50 µm multimode fiber without anamorphic optics. Trade-offs versus edge-emitting DFB/EML lasers include limited single-mode output power, higher series resistance through the DBR stack, thermal rollover, and susceptibility to back-reflection noise or modal-noise penalties when paired with dirty or mismatched (OM1/OM2 vs. OM3/OM4) fiber.
