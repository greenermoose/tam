Ethernet

The standard wired local-area networking technology used to connect computers, switches, and routers over copper or fiber-optic cables.

Think of Ethernet as a dedicated highway for data inside your home or office, compared to Wi-Fi, which shares the air like people talking across a room. Plugging in an Ethernet cable gives your computer a steady, private lane to your router without wireless interference.

Ethernet operates at the physical and data-link layers, packaging data into frames addressed by 48-bit MAC addresses. On Linux, Ethernet ports appear as interfaces like eth0 or enp3s0 (viewable with ip link or ethtool) and auto-negotiate speed—commonly 1 Gbps or 10 Gbps—and full-duplex operation. Unlike Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11), which contends for shared radio spectrum, wired switched Ethernet gives each port its own dedicated link.

Why do devices plugged into a modern Ethernet switch rarely suffer packet collisions?

Modern twisted-pair and fiber Ethernet links run in full-duplex mode between each device and its switch port, using separate send and receive paths. Because no two devices share a single wire for transmitting, the switch buffers and forwards frames instead of relying on collision detection.

An IEEE 802.3 family spanning physical layers (10BASE-T, 1000BASE-T with 4D-PAM5, 10G/25G/100G/400G PHYs using PAM4 and Reed–Solomon FEC) and MAC framing: 7-byte preamble, 1-byte SFD, 6-byte destination and source MACs, optional 4-byte 802.1Q VLAN tag, 2-byte EtherType/length, 46–1500 byte payload (for standard 1500-byte MTU), 4-byte CRC-32 FCS, and 12-byte interframe gap. Legacy half-duplex coax/hubs required CSMA/CD and a 64-byte minimum frame to detect collisions within the slot time; modern full-duplex switched fabrics replace CSMA/CD with optional 802.3x PAUSE or 802.1Qbb PFC flow control, end-to-end jumbo frames (typically 9000-byte MTU), and NIC offloads (TSO, GSO, GRO, and hardware checksums).
