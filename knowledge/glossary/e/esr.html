ESR (Equivalent Series Resistance)

Equivalent Series Resistance: the small internal electrical resistance inside a real capacitor that limits filtering and turns ripple current into heat.

An ideal capacitor stores and releases electrical energy cleanly without wasting any, but a real-world capacitor has a tiny bit of built-in resistance called ESR. As old electrolytic capacitors dry out, their ESR climbs—causing them to heat up, bulge, and fail to smooth out power, which leads to random PC crashes or dead monitors.

In switch-mode power supplies (SMPS) and motherboard CPU voltage regulators (VRMs), high-frequency ripple current flows through output capacitors constantly. Low-ESR polymer and ceramic capacitors keep output voltage ripple small and run cool, whereas using a high-ESR general-purpose electrolytic capacitor in a high-frequency supply causes overheating and instability.

Why does rising ESR in an aging power-supply capacitor cause the capacitor to heat up and fail faster?

Ripple current passing through the capacitor’s ESR dissipates power as heat (I² × ESR), which accelerates electrolyte evaporation and drives ESR even higher in a runaway cycle.

In a lumped-element model, a real capacitor’s complex impedance is Z(ω) = ESR + j(ω·ESL − 1/(ω·C)), meaning ESR sets the minimum impedance floor at the self-resonant frequency (SRF = 1/(2π√(ESL·C))). Beyond I²·ESR thermal loss, output capacitor ESR creates a zero in the buck-converter control loop (ω_z = 1/(ESR·C)) that voltage-mode regulators sometimes rely on for phase margin—meaning swapping an electrolytic for an ultra-low-ESR MLCC without checking compensation can induce loop oscillation.
