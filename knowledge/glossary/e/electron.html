Electron

An electron is a tiny, negatively charged elementary particle whose motion and energy states govern electric current and semiconductor behavior.

Electrons are part of every atom in ordinary matter. In metals like copper, some electrons are free to drift along like water in a pipe; when you switch on a computer or plug in a cable, electric forces push those electrons through wires and silicon chips to deliver power and carry signals.

In a metallic conductor, many loosely bound electrons drift under an electric field to form current. In a semiconductor like silicon, very few electrons are free to conduct unless heat, an electric field, or chemical doping supplies them—and chip designers track both negatively charged electrons and positively charged “holes” (vacancies left behind when an electron moves).

Why does pure silicon conduct poorly compared with copper until it is doped or switched on by a transistor gate?

At room temperature, most electrons in pure silicon are locked in covalent bonds, whereas copper has a dense sea of free conduction electrons ready to move.
