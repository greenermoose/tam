Electromagnetic Wave

An electromagnetic wave is a propagating oscillation of coupled electric and magnetic fields that carries energy and information through space.

Electromagnetic waves are invisible ripples of energy traveling at the speed of light—like ripples spreading across a pond, except they move through air or empty space without needing a wire. Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, mobile phones, and FM radio all send information by shaping these waves with an antenna.

In wireless networking, high-frequency alternating current in a transmitting antenna accelerates electrons, radiating electromagnetic waves that induce a tiny matching current in a receiving antenna. Different bands trade off range and speed: 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi penetrates walls better than 5 GHz or 6 GHz, while higher bands offer wider, less crowded channels—and unlike guided optical waves in fiber, unguided radio waves spread out and weaken rapidly with distance.

Why does 5 GHz Wi-Fi usually have a shorter range through walls than 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi?

Higher-frequency waves suffer greater free-space path loss and are absorbed or reflected more strongly by building materials like drywall, brick, and concrete.

Governed by Maxwell’s curl equations in linear isotropic media, transverse orthogonal electric (E) and magnetic (B) fields propagate at c = 1 / sqrt(μ₀ε₀) in vacuum (slowed by refractive index n = sqrt(μ_r ε_r) in dielectrics) with free-space path loss scaling as (4πd / λ)². Digital wireless standards such as Wi-Fi and 5G impress symbols onto RF carriers using IQ modulation (QAM) across orthogonal subcarriers (OFDM) to counter multipath fading and inter-symbol interference.
