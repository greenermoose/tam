Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with E / ESD - Electrostatic Discharge

Electrostatic discharge: a sudden flow of static electricity between two objects at different electrical potentials, which can damage sensitive electronics.

Walking across a carpet and getting a tiny shock from a doorknob is ESD. A static discharge can reach thousands of volts and that can silently fry microscopic circuits inside a computer’s memory stick or processor.

When opening a PC or handling bare circuit boards, you prevent ESD by touching a grounded metal chassis before touching components, using an anti-static wrist strap, and storing spare parts in conductive anti-static bags (not plain plastic or carpet). Beware of latent damage: an ESD hit may weaken a chip so it fails weeks later rather than immediately.

Why can touching a bare RAM stick cause damage even if you didn’t feel a spark?

Humans typically only feel static discharges above ~3,000 volts, whereas modern CMOS transistors and gate oxides can be punctured or degraded by discharges under 100 volts.

ESD transients are characterized by standard test models—chiefly the Human Body Model (HBM: 100 pF discharged through 1.5 kΩ, ~10 ns rise time) and Charged Device Model (CDM: sub-nanosecond, multi-ampere spikes from the package’s own self-capacitance). IC pins rely on on-die clamping networks (steering diodes and rail clamps) tested using device-level JEDEC methods; equipment immunity may also be tested under IEC 61000-4-2, while board designs add low-capacitance TVS diode arrays on high-speed interfaces like USB and HDMI.
