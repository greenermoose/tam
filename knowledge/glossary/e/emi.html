EMI (Electromagnetic Interference)

Electromagnetic interference: unwanted electrical noise radiated through the air or conducted along wires between circuits and cables.

Just as a nearby vacuum cleaner can cause static on an old radio, motors, power bricks, and high-speed data lines give off invisible electrical noise. If that noise leaks into an unshielded audio or data cable, it can add hum to speakers or scramble digital signals so devices drop connections.

In practice, EMI shows up as audio buzz near power cables, flickering displays, or USB and Ethernet dropouts when cables run parallel to AC mains. Twisted-pair wiring, differential signaling, metal cable shields, and snap-on ferrite beads reduce both incoming pickup and outgoing emissions.

Why does twisting two signal wires together reduce EMI?

Twisting ensures both wires sit at nearly the same average distance from a noise source, so interference induces roughly equal voltage on both conductors—which a differential receiver cancels out.

EMI couples via four mechanisms—capacitive, inductive, conductive (common impedance), and far-field radiation—degrading signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and causing bit errors, clock jitter, or spurious hot-plug detect (HPD) glitches. Mitigation targets loop-area minimization, continuous return planes, common-mode chokes, and 360-degree shield termination to chassis ground, keeping radiated and conducted emissions within regulatory limits (such as FCC Part 15 and CISPR 32).
