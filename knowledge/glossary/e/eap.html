EAP (Extensible Authentication Protocol)

A flexible authentication framework used to verify users and devices on enterprise Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.1X / WPA-Enterprise), wired switch ports, and VPNs.

Home Wi-Fi uses one shared password that everyone types in, like a single house key. EAP is the login system behind company and university Wi-Fi, where the network asks for your own username, password, or digital certificate and checks it with a central server before letting you connect.

Unlike home WPA2/WPA3-Personal (PSK), WPA-Enterprise carries EAP over IEEE 802.1X between your device, the access point, and a RADIUS server. Common methods include EAP-TLS (mutual certificates, strongest), PEAP, and EAP-TTLS (an outer TLS tunnel protecting inner credentials). On Linux (NetworkManager or wpa_supplicant), configuring a trusted CA certificate is essential for tunneled methods.

What is the security risk of leaving "No CA certificate required" enabled when configuring PEAP or EAP-TTLS on Linux?

Without verifying the RADIUS server’s TLS certificate against a trusted CA, a rogue access point can impersonate the network, terminate the outer TLS tunnel, and capture your inner password or MSCHAPv2 challenge-response.

Defined in RFC 3748, EAP is a lock-step request/response framing layer (Code, Identifier, Length, Type) that decouples authentication methods from lower transports such as EAPOL (802.1X), IKEv2, PPP, and RADIUS/Diameter. Negotiation begins with EAP-Request/Identity and method proposals (with EAP-Nak fallback). Successful methods export an MSK/EMSK to seed 802.11 4-way handshake keys, and modern deployments require Channel Binding (RFC 6677) and TLS 1.3 protections (RFC 9190 for EAP-TLS 1.3) to prevent compound-authentication and MITM attacks.
