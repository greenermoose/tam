EIRP (Effective Isotropic Radiated Power)

EIRP (Effective Isotropic Radiated Power) is a measure of how much radio power a transmitter and its antenna radiate in the direction of strongest signal.

Imagine a bare light bulb versus a flashlight: both use the same bulb, but the flashlight’s reflector focuses the light into a much brighter beam in one direction. EIRP measures how strong a Wi-Fi or radio signal is in its brightest direction, which is why radio regulators limit EIRP by law rather than just the chip’s raw power.

EIRP is calculated in dBm as TX power (dBm) − cable loss (dB) + antenna gain (dBi), meaning a high-gain antenna raises EIRP even if the radio’s output stays the same. On Linux, the kernel’s cfg80211 regulatory database enforces regional EIRP caps per Wi-Fi band, which you can inspect with iw reg get and iw dev wlan0 info.

If a Wi-Fi card outputs 20 dBm into a 6 dBi antenna with 1 dB of cable loss, what is its EIRP—and what must you do if the local limit is 20 dBm?

The EIRP is 20 − 1 + 6 = 25 dBm. To stay legal under a 20 dBm cap, you must lower the card’s TX power to 15 dBm.

EIRP is the power a theoretical lossless isotropic radiator would need to emit to match the peak far-field power flux density S = P_EIRP / (4πr²), given linearly by P_EIRP = P_TX · G_ant / L_c or logarithmically by P_EIRP(dBm) = P_TX(dBm) − L_c(dB) + G_ant(dBi). Unlike ERP (referenced to a half-wave dipole, where EIRP = ERP + 2.15 dB), EIRP uses an isotropic reference; in 6 GHz Wi-Fi (LPI/VLP), regulators pair total EIRP ceilings with power spectral density (dBm/MHz) limits and Transmit Power Control (TPC).
