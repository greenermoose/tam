ECDLP (Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem)

The mathematical hardness assumption behind elliptic-curve cryptography, asking how many times a point on an elliptic curve was added to itself to reach a target point.

Imagine hopping around a giant clock face using a secret number of fixed jumps: it is easy to take 1,000 jumps forward and tell someone where you landed, but almost impossible for them to look at your landing spot and figure out how many jumps you took. ECDLP is that same one-way puzzle on special curves, letting modern security apps use much shorter keys than older systems like RSA.

Curves such as P-256 and Curve25519 (used in TLS, SSH, and WireGuard) rely on ECDLP because a 256-bit key gives roughly 128-bit classical security—matching a 3072-bit RSA key at a fraction of the size. Like standard discrete logarithms, however, ECDLP can be solved in polynomial time by a large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computer running Shor’s algorithm.

Why do Curve25519 and P-256 keys stay around 256 bits long when RSA keys need 2048–3072 bits for comparable classical security?

No sub-exponential classical algorithm is known for ECDLP over well-chosen curves (the best attacks scale roughly as the square root of the group order), whereas RSA and finite-field DLP fall to sub-exponential index-calculus attacks and need much larger keys.

Given an elliptic curve E over a finite field F_q, a base point P of prime order n, and Q = kP for secret scalar k in [1, n-1], ECDLP asks to recover k. Without a known sub-exponential index-calculus attack on generic curves, the best classical algorithms are square-root methods (Pollard’s rho, baby-step giant-step) in O(sqrt(n)) group operations. Curve selection must still avoid anomalous curves (#E(F_p) = p, broken by Smart’s attack), small embedding degrees (MOV/Frey–Rück reduction to F_p^k), and implementation pitfalls such as missing point-on-curve or small-subgroup validation.
