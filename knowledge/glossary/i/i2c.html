Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with I / I2C - Inter-Integrated Circuit

A two-wire serial bus used to connect low-speed chips and sensors to a processor.

I2C is like a shared two-wire intercom inside a computer or circuit board. One wire acts as a steady beat to keep everyone in sync, and the other carries messages. Every small chip—such as a temperature sensor, battery gauge, or laptop touchpad—has its own address number so only the right chip answers when spoken to.

Inter-Integrated Circuit uses a clock line (SCL) and a data line (SDA) with pull-up resistors so multiple chips can share the same two wires. Unlike SPI, which needs a separate chip-select wire for every device and runs much faster, I2C identifies targets by address. On Linux, kernel I2C adapters appear as /dev/i2c-* device nodes and can be inspected with tools like i2cdetect, though probing unknown addresses can occasionally confuse sensitive hardware.

How can many I2C sensors share just two wires without a separate select pin for each chip?

The controller starts each transfer by sending the target device’s unique address on the data line; all other devices on the bus ignore the rest of the transaction until a STOP condition frees the bus.

I2C is a synchronous, half-duplex, multi-master serial bus using open-drain SDA/SCL lines pulled high by external resistors, creating an RC rise-time constraint against bus capacitance (400 pF max in Standard/Fast mode). Frames use START/STOP transitions, 7-bit (112 usable) or 10-bit addresses, and per-byte ACK/NACK bits. Multi-master arbitration uses wired-AND collision detection without data loss; slow targets can hold SCL low via clock stretching. Standard speeds range from 100 kHz to 1 MHz (Fast-Mode Plus) and 3.4 MHz (High-Speed). Related SMBus/PMBus specifications tighten voltage thresholds and add clock-low timeouts to prevent hung devices from wedging the bus.
