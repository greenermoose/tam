IFP (Integer Factorization Problem)

The mathematical problem of splitting a large composite integer into its prime factors, which underpins RSA cryptography.

Multiplying two huge prime numbers together is quick for a computer, but working backward from the answer to discover the two original primes is extraordinarily slow. That one-way puzzle—easy to lock, hard to reverse—is what keeps classic RSA encryption and digital signatures safe.

RSA key sizes (2048 or 3072+ bits today; 1024-bit is no longer considered safe) are chosen so classical factoring takes infeasible time—much larger than a 128-bit AES key for comparable strength. However, a sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer running Shor’s algorithm could solve IFP quickly, which is why new systems increasingly use elliptic-curve or post-quantum cryptography.

Why does RSA need a 2048-bit or 3072-bit key to roughly match the security of a 128-bit AES key?

Because mathematical factoring algorithms can exploit the structure of integers to factor a modulus much faster than brute-forcing every possible number, whereas symmetric AES has no comparable shortcut.

Given a composite modulus N = pq for large primes p and q, recover p and q. The fastest known classical algorithm for general moduli is the General Number Field Sieve (GNFS), with subexponential heuristic complexity L_N[1/3, (64/9)^(1/3)]—giving roughly 112 bits of classical security for 2048-bit RSA and ~128 bits for 3072-bit RSA. Real-world RSA failures rarely factor random moduli directly; instead, they exploit weak RNG entropy producing shared prime factors across keys (batch GCD), primes too close together (Fermat factorization), or side-channel leakage of CRT exponents.
