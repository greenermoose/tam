Integrated circuit

A complete electronic circuit—combining millions or billions of transistors and microscopic wiring—manufactured onto a single piece of semiconductor material.

Before integrated circuits, engineers had to solder separate switches and wires onto large boards by hand, like building a city out of individual bricks. An integrated circuit shrinks an entire city of electronics onto a single flat fingernail-sized slice of silicon, making computers small, fast, and affordable enough to fit in a pocket.

Almost every active chip in a computer—the CPU, GPU, RAM chips, SSD controllers, and network adapters—is an integrated circuit (IC) housed in a protective package with pins or solder balls. On Linux, commands like lspci and lsusb list the controller ICs attached to your system buses. Most digital ICs use CMOS logic, drawing significant power mainly when transistors switch states.

Why did moving from discrete transistors to integrated circuits make processors dramatically faster?

Shrinking components onto one semiconductor die slashes the physical distance signals travel and reduces parasitic capacitance, enabling much higher clock speeds with far less power.

Monolithic ICs are fabricated on single-crystal silicon wafers via photolithography, ion implantation/diffusion, deposition, and multi-layer BEOL copper or cobalt metallization over FEOL FinFET or GAAFET transistors. Architects balance power, performance, and area (PPA) against wafer defect yield, clock-skew distribution, and electromigration limits; large designs increasingly split dies into chiplets or 2.5D multi-chip modules (MCMs) bonded on a silicon interposer.
