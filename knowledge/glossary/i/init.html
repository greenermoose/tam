Init

The first user-space program the operating system kernel starts at boot, responsible for bringing up the rest of the system and managing running services.

When you turn on a computer, the kernel wakes up the hardware first, like turning on a building’s electricity and plumbing. Init is the first manager who walks through the door: it starts background helpers like networking and login screens, and stays on duty until shutdown to keep everything orderly.

Init always runs as process ID 1 (PID 1). On most modern Linux distributions, systemd fills this role, though alternatives like OpenRC, runit, or s6 exist. When a parent program exits before its child processes finish, PID 1 adopts those orphans and cleans up their exit status so they do not pile up as zombie processes. You can check your active init with ps -p 1 -o comm=.

What happens to a Linux system if PID 1 (init) crashes or exits unexpectedly?

The kernel immediately panics and halts the system, because PID 1 is required to supervise user space and reap orphaned processes.

After mounting the root filesystem (or pivoting from initramfs), the kernel launches /sbin/init (or the binary specified by init=) via kernel_execve(). The kernel gives PID 1 special signal semantics: default fatal dispositions (such as SIGTERM or SIGINT) are ignored unless PID 1 explicitly installs a handler, and even SIGKILL cannot be delivered to it from user space. Beyond service and cgroup supervision, PID 1 must call waitpid(-1, ..., WNOHANG) on SIGCHLD to reap reparented children and invoke sync() and reboot(2) at shutdown.
