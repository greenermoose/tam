Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with I / IOPS (Input/Output Operations Per Second)

Storage benchmark measuring how many individual read or write requests a drive completes per second.

Imagine a busy post office clerk sorting individual letters versus loading one giant crate onto a truck. IOPS counts how many separate small requests—like opening an app icon, saving a settings file, or looking up a single record—a storage drive can finish in one second. When a computer feels snappy while starting up or switching programs, high IOPS is usually why, because operating systems read and write thousands of tiny files all the time.

In Linux storage testing (for example with fio), IOPS is usually measured with small 4 KiB random reads and writes rather than large sequential transfers. A spinning hard drive (HDD) must physically move its read/write head and wait for the platter to spin, limiting it to roughly 75–150 random IOPS, whereas an NVMe SSD handles tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of IOPS over parallel PCIe queues. Advertised peak IOPS assume high queue depths (many concurrent requests), while everyday desktop responsiveness depends heavily on low-queue-depth (QD1) random read IOPS and latency.

Why can a drive advertise 500 MB/s sequential speed yet still feel sluggish when booting Linux or compiling code?

Booting and compiling issue thousands of tiny, scattered read and write requests rather than one continuous stream. A drive with high sequential MB/s but low random 4 KiB IOPS spends most of its time waiting on seek or request-completion latency for each small operation.

IOPS and throughput are coupled by I/O size (Throughput = IOPS × BlockSize), so 4 KiB random IOPS primarily characterizes request-dispatch and media-access latency rather than bus bandwidth. Because NVMe exposes up to 64K submission/completion queue pairs with 64K entries each, saturating flash parallelism requires high queue depth (QD) and multi-threaded io_uring or libaio (O_DIRECT) workloads; at QD=1, latency L bounds IOPS to 1 / L regardless of controller parallelism. Real-world steady-state write IOPS also degrades below fresh-out-of-box (FOB) benchmarks once flash blocks fill and FTL garbage collection and wear leveling compete with host writes (write amplification).
