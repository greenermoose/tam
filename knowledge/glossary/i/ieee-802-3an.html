Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with I / IEEE 802.3an (10GBASE-T)

The IEEE 802.3an (10GBASE-T) standard for 10 Gigabit Ethernet over twisted-pair copper cabling.

IEEE 802.3an is the rulebook that lets familiar copper Ethernet cables carry 10 billion bits per second—ten times faster than standard gigabit Ethernet—over distances up to 100 meters when paired with higher-grade Cat 6A cabling.

10GBASE-T auto-negotiates down to 5G, 2.5G, or 1GBASE-T on older links and reaches 100 meters over Cat 6A twisted-pair cable (or shorter 37–55 meter runs on Cat 6 depending on crosstalk). Compared with SFP+ fiber or direct-attach copper (DAC) cables, 10GBASE-T uses familiar RJ45 jacks and existing building wiring, but the transceivers draw noticeably more power and run hotter.

Why do data centers often prefer SFP+ DAC or fiber over 10GBASE-T for short in-rack links?

10GBASE-T requires heavy signal processing to cancel noise across copper pairs, so each port consumes much more power, generates more heat, and adds slightly higher latency than SFP+ DAC or optical modules.

IEEE Std 802.3an-2006 (Clause 55) specifies a 10 Gbps full-duplex PHY operating simultaneously across all four twisted pairs (2.5 Gbps per pair) at 800 MBaud. It combines PAM-16 modulation mapped onto a DSQ128 two-dimensional constellation, Tomlinson-Harashima precoding (THP) to counter inter-symbol interference, and a (2048, 1723) LDPC forward error correction code. Continuous DSP for echo cancellation, NEXT/FEXT, and alien crosstalk (ANEXT) mitigation yields higher PHY latency (~1.5–2.5 µs vs. ~0.3 µs for SFP+) and 2–5 W per-port power draw, which also constrains Energy-Efficient Ethernet (EEE) wake-up times.
