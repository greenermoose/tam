ISO (International Organization for Standardization)

International federation of national standards bodies that publishes worldwide technical, industrial, and computing standards.

Novice definition

ISO is the worldwide organization where countries agree on shared rules—called standards—so products, software, and data work the same way everywhere. Without shared standards, files, dates, text encodings, and hardware from different makers would not fit together. The short name "ISO" is not an English acronym; its founders chose it from the Greek word isos ("equal") so it stays the same in every language. In Linux, an ".iso" installer file gets its name from the ISO 9660 standard for optical discs.

Learner definition

ISO brings together national standards bodies (such as ANSI in the United States) to publish voluntary, consensus-based specifications. In computing, ISO works jointly with the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) under Joint Technical Committee 1 (JTC 1). Standards matter because they give compiler writers, operating-system developers, and hardware vendors a neutral contract so code and data stay portable across platforms and decades. Examples in this glossary include ISO/IEC 9899 (C), ISO 8859-1 (Latin-1), ISO/IEC 21031 (Software Carbon Intensity), and PDF (ISO 32000).

Expert definition

Non-governmental federation founded in 1947 in Geneva, comprising over 160 national standards bodies. Drafts advance through structured ballots (WD, CD, DIS, FDIS) in technical committees and subcommittees; IT standards such as ISO/IEC 9899 (SC22/WG14) and ISO/IEC 8859-1 (SC2) are governed by ISO/IEC JTC 1. Unlike IETF RFCs, many ISO/IEC specifications are sold rather than freely distributed, though selected JTC 1 standards (such as ISO/IEC 21031) are available as free downloads.
