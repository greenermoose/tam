Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with I / ISI (Inter-Symbol Interference)

Signal distortion where one transmitted pulse smears out and overlaps neighboring bit slots.

Imagine speaking very fast in a tiled hallway with a strong echo: the end of one syllable blurs into the start of the next, making your words hard to tell apart. ISI is the electrical or optical version of that echo on a cable or circuit board. When data bits are sent billions of times per second, each pulse stretches slightly as it travels and smears into the next bit, making it harder for the receiver to tell a 1 from a 0.

ISI becomes a limiting factor on high-speed links such as PCIe, USB4, DisplayPort, HDMI, and 10G+ Ethernet as data rates rise or cables get longer. On an oscilloscope, engineers inspect ISI using an “eye diagram”—a clean signal shows a wide-open eye, while heavy ISI closes the eye vertically and horizontally. Hardware combats ISI using pre-emphasis or de-emphasis at the transmitter (boosting high-frequency edges) and equalizers such as CTLE (Continuous Time Linear Equalization) and DFE (Decision Feedback Equalization) at the receiver.

Why does shortening a high-speed DisplayPort or USB4 cable often fix flickering or link-training failures?

Shorter cables have lower high-frequency attenuation and fewer impedance discontinuities, reducing pulse smearing (ISI). With less ISI, the receiver’s eye diagram stays open enough to recover the clock and bits reliably without dropping link speed.

ISI arises from channel dispersion—frequency-dependent attenuation (skin effect and dielectric loss in copper, chromatic or modal dispersion in fiber) and impedance-mismatch reflections—which causes the channel impulse response h(t) to have non-zero tails at integer multiples of the symbol period T (h(kT) ≠ 0 for k ≠ 0), violating the Nyquist ISI criterion. In PAM4 signaling (used in PCIe 6.0 and 400GbE), having four amplitude levels shrinks noise margins by roughly 9.5 dB compared to NRZ, making links far more sensitive to residual ISI and requiring multi-tap FIR TX equalization plus CTLE, multi-tap DFE, or ADC/DSP-based FFE/MLSE receivers combined with Forward Error Correction (FEC) to meet target BER.
