Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with H / HBR (High Bit Rate)

HBR (High Bit Rate) is the original 2.7 gigabit-per-second per-lane speed standard used by DisplayPort connections to carry video and audio to a monitor.

Think of a DisplayPort cable like a four-lane highway carrying picture data from your computer to your screen. HBR is the classic speed limit for each lane—fast enough to comfortably run everyday 1080p or 1440p monitors at standard refresh rates, though newer high-resolution or high-refresh screens need faster lanes.

In DisplayPort link rates, a full 4-lane HBR link gives 10.8 Gbps raw signaling and 8.64 Gbps of usable video payload after encoding overhead. On Linux, you can check whether a connected monitor negotiated HBR (2.7 Gbps) rather than a higher rate by reading the DRM/KMS connector properties or kernel logs when diagnosing why a high-refresh mode is missing.

Why does a 4-lane HBR link at 2.7 Gbps per lane only deliver 8.64 Gbps of usable video data instead of 10.8 Gbps?

Because DisplayPort 1.0–1.4 uses 8b/10b line coding, which turns every 8 bits of video data into 10 bits on the wire for clock recovery and DC balance—a 20% overhead.

Defined in DisplayPort 1.0+, HBR operates at a 2.70 Gbps/lane symbol rate using ANSI 8b/10b encoding (2.16 Gbps/lane effective payload, or 8.64 Gbps across four lanes; DisplayPort also supports 1-lane and 2-lane fallback). During AUX-channel link training (Clock Recovery with TPS1 and Channel Equalization with TPS2), if signal integrity fails at higher rates such as HBR2/HBR3 due to long or passive cables, the source falls back to HBR or RBR (1.62 Gbps/lane), capping available pixel clock and bit depth (for example, 2560×1440 at 60 Hz 8 bpc fits easily, whereas 3840×2160 at 60 Hz 8 bpc exceeds 8.64 Gbps without chroma subsampling).
