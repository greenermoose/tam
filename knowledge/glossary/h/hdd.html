Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with H / HDD - Hard Disk Drive

An HDD (Hard Disk Drive) is a non-volatile storage device that stores data magnetically on spinning metal or glass platters.

Think of an HDD like a tiny, super-fast record player sealed inside a metal box. Instead of music grooves, spinning magnetic disks hold your files, and a moving arm reads and writes data as the disks spin. Because parts have to physically move, HDDs are slower than solid-state drives (SSDs), but they give you much more storage space for the price.

On Linux, HDDs appear as block devices such as /dev/sda (and partitions like /dev/sda1) and are well suited for bulk storage, backups, and media libraries rather than OS roots or databases with heavy random I/O. Because seek time dominates random reads and writes, schedulers like bfq or mq-deadline and SMART health monitoring (smartctl) matter more for HDDs than for NVMe SSDs.

Why does an HDD slow down dramatically on many small random reads compared to reading one large video file?

Reading one large sequential file lets the read/write head stay on adjacent tracks, whereas small random reads force the actuator arm to physically seek across tracks and wait for the platter to rotate under the head each time.

An HDD is an electromechanical block device combining a spindle motor (commonly 5,400 or 7,200 RPM, giving ~5.5 ms or ~4.2 ms average rotational latency), a voice-coil actuator positioning GMR/TMR heads to within nanometers of concentric tracks, and a controller exposing 512e or 4Kn logical block addressing (LBA) over SATA or SAS. High-capacity drives often use Shingled Magnetic Recording (SMR), where overlapping tracks boost areal density at the cost of write amplification during random updates—requiring either drive-managed read-modify-write caching or host-managed zoned block commands (ZBC/ZAC).
