HDR - High Dynamic Range

HDR (High Dynamic Range) is a display and video technology that preserves much brighter highlights, deeper shadows, and a wider range of colors than traditional standard dynamic range (SDR).

On a normal screen, bright sunlight in a scene often washes out to flat white while dark shadows turn into muddy gray. HDR lets a compatible monitor show both crisp detail in dark corners and dazzling bright highlights at the same time, making movies and games look closer to how your eyes see the real world.

HDR requires end-to-end support from the content, operating system, cable, and display: 10-bit (or higher) color depth, wide color gamut (such as Rec. 2020), and an HDR transfer function (PQ or HLG). On Linux, HDR output has matured in modern Wayland compositors and kernels (via DRM color-management properties), whereas X11 lacks native per-window HDR compositing and often leaves HDR either unsupported or washed out.

Why do HDR videos or desktops look washed out and gray when displayed in SDR mode without tone mapping?

HDR content is encoded with a much wider luminance curve (such as PQ up to 10,000 nits) and wider color primaries; interpreting those numbers directly as SDR (typically ~100 nits, Rec. 709) compresses midtones and desaturates colors.

HDR replaces the display-referred 2.2/2.4 gamma curve of SDR (Rec. 709) with either Perceptual Quantizer (PQ, SMPTE ST 2084—an absolute luminance transfer function from 0 to 10,000 cd/m²) or Hybrid Log-Gamma (HLG, backward-compatible relative scene-referred encoding), typically quantized at 10 or 12 bpc inside Rec. 2020 primaries. Static metadata (CTA-861.3 InfoFrames: MaxCLL, MaxFALL, mastering display primaries) or dynamic metadata (HDR10+, Dolby Vision) guides the display’s tone-mapping curve when panel peak luminance is below the mastered range. In Linux DRM/KMS, HDR_OUTPUT_METADATA and per-plane color pipelines handle blending SDR and HDR surfaces.
