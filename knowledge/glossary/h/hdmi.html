Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with H / HDMI (High-Definition Multimedia Interface)

HDMI (High-Definition Multimedia Interface) is a widely used standard for carrying digital video, multi-channel audio, and device-control signals over a single cable.

HDMI is the familiar flat, slightly tapered plug on TVs, monitors, laptops, and game consoles. One cable carries both the picture and the sound, so you do not need separate audio wires—making it the everyday standard for plugging a computer into a living-room TV or second screen.

HDMI combines video, digital audio, monitor identification (EDID), and optional remote-control commands (CEC) in one link, along with HDCP copy protection when required by media apps. On Linux, open-source GPU drivers support HDMI 2.0 well, while HDMI 2.1’s highest 48 Gbps FRL modes have faced licensing restrictions on open-source implementations (such as AMD’s Linux driver), sometimes requiring DisplayPort or a lower refresh rate/chroma format.

Why can a Linux system fail to output 4K at 120 Hz over HDMI 2.1 even when the hardware and cable support it?

The HDMI Forum has restricted public specification access for HDMI 2.1 FRL modes, which has blocked open-source Linux drivers (notably AMD’s) from enabling 48 Gbps FRL signaling, capping output at HDMI 2.0 TMDS speeds unless chroma subsampling or lower refresh rates are used.

HDMI 1.0–2.0 transmit serialized pixels and audio packets using Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) across three data channels and one clock channel (up to 6.0 Gbps/lane, 18 Gbps aggregate in HDMI 2.0, 14.4 Gbps payload with 8b/10b). HDMI 2.1 replaces TMDS clock-plus-data with Fixed Rate Link (FRL) packetized signaling across 3 or 4 lanes at up to 12 Gbps/lane (48 Gbps aggregate using 16b/18b coding) and optional DSC 1.2a. Sidebands include DDC (I2C for EDID/SCDC), CEC, HPD (Hot Plug Detect), and eARC for high-bitrate return audio.
