Photon

A photon is a discrete packet (quantum) of electromagnetic energy that carries light and other electromagnetic radiation.

Light behaves like a wave, but when it arrives at your eye, a camera sensor, or a solar panel, it lands in tiny individual packets called photons. Photons have no mass when at rest and always travel at the speed of light in a vacuum. Higher-frequency light—like blue or ultraviolet—packs more energy into each photon than lower-frequency light such as red or infrared.

Photons bridge optics and electronics in everyday hardware: LEDs and displays emit photons when electrons drop across an energy gap, while camera sensors, fiber-optic receivers, and solar cells absorb photons to free electrons. Because photon energy depends on frequency rather than brightness, a dim blue LED can trigger electronic transitions (or fade dyes) that a bright red LED cannot, because each red photon carries less energy than the material’s threshold.

Why can a single ultraviolet or blue photon trigger a semiconductor transition that many red photons cannot?

Each photon’s energy depends on its frequency, not the total brightness of the beam. A red photon carries less energy than the material’s threshold, whereas a blue or ultraviolet photon carries enough energy in one packet to cross it.

The gauge boson of the electromagnetic field with zero invariant mass, spin 1, energy E = hf = hc/λ, and momentum p = h/λ. In optoelectronics, interband absorption in photodiodes and CMOS sensors requires hf >= E_g (the semiconductor bandgap); in single-mode fiber links (such as 1310 nm or 1550 nm transceivers), quantum shot noise from discrete photon arrivals sets a fundamental signal-to-noise floor alongside thermal and laser RIN noise.
