PCIe - Peripheral Component Interconnect Express

PCIe is the high-speed internal expansion bus that connects components such as graphics cards, NVMe drives, and network adapters to the processor and memory.

Inside a computer, fast parts cannot share a slow cable or wait in a single line. PCIe acts like a dedicated multi-lane highway directly between the main processor and high-speed add-in parts—such as a graphics card or solid-state storage—so large amounts of data move quickly without slowing each other down.

PCI Express is a point-to-point serial fabric organized into lanes (such as x1, x4, or x16). Both physical slot size and electrical lane wiring matter: a physical x16 slot may only be wired for x4 or x8 lanes. On Linux, lspci -vv shows the currently negotiated link speed and width (LnkSta) alongside the hardware maximum (LnkCap), whereas buses like SATA or USB sit behind an extra controller.

Why might an NVMe SSD or GPU show a lower link speed in lspci when the system is idle?

PCIe Active State Power Management (ASPM) dynamically downshifts link speed and width when a device is idle to save power, then raises them under load. Comparing LnkSta during active I/O against LnkCap distinguishes power saving from a mis-seated card or lane-sharing limit.

PCIe uses a layered packet-based architecture—Transaction, Data Link, and Physical layers—with 8b/10b (Gen1/2), 128b/130b (Gen3–5), or PAM4 (Gen6) signaling and credit-based flow control. Transaction Layer Packets (TLPs) route via Bus/Device/Function (BDF) identifiers through the Root Complex and switches, while interrupts travel as MSI/MSI-X memory writes. Operational edge cases include IOMMU/ACS isolation groups for VFIO device passthrough, hot-plug surprises, and Advanced Error Reporting (AER) link-recovery events that silently cap throughput.
