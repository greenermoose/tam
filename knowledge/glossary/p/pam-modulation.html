PAM modulation (PAM-2, PAM-4, PAM-5)

Pulse-Amplitude Modulation (PAM) encodes digital data by varying the voltage height of each electrical pulse across two or more distinct levels.

Computers talk over wires using quick pulses of electricity. In simple signaling, a pulse is either high or low—like tapping Morse code with only two volumes. Pulse-Amplitude Modulation generalizes that idea by letting each pulse take one of several preset heights (such as 2, 4, or 5 levels), so a single pulse can carry more information at once when a cable or circuit board cannot switch any faster.

High-speed interfaces step up from PAM-2 (binary, 1 bit per symbol) to PAM-4 (2 bits per symbol, used in PCIe 6.0 and 400G Ethernet) and PAM-5 (used in 1000BASE-T Gigabit Ethernet). Adding voltage levels increases bits per symbol without raising the switching frequency, which helps when copper traces lose too much signal at higher frequencies—though closer voltage steps make the link more sensitive to noise.

What is the main trade-off when moving from PAM-2 to a higher-level scheme like PAM-4?

You double the bits carried per symbol without increasing the symbol rate, which reduces high-frequency cable/trace loss, but the voltage margins between levels shrink—making the link much more sensitive to noise and requiring error correction.

PAM-M represents M-ary baseband amplitude modulation (PAM-2, PAM-4, PAM-5) trading SNR margin for spectral efficiency (log2(M) gross bits/symbol) under Nyquist channel bandwidth constraints. Stepping from 2 to M levels reduces eye height by 1/(M − 1), imposing a 20·log10(M − 1) dB SNR penalty (9.54 dB for PAM-4) and tightening jitter, linearity, and crosstalk budgets. Modern SerDes PHYs compensate with multi-tap TX FFE, RX CTLE/DFE, ADC-based DSP receivers, and Gray-coded or trellis-coded FEC.
