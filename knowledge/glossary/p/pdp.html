PDP (Policy Decision Point)

A Policy Decision Point is the authorization component in an access-control system that evaluates rules and attributes to return a permit or deny decision.

Imagine a bouncer at a door holding a phone to a manager inside. The bouncer stops you and asks, “Can alex enter?”—and the manager checks the guest list, time of day, and rules before saying “yes” or “no.” In computer security, the PDP is that manager: it does not block the door itself, but makes the yes-or-no decision based on the rules.

In Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC) and engines like Open Policy Agent (OPA) or XACML, the PDP is separated from the Policy Enforcement Point (PEP). The PEP—such as an API gateway or reverse proxy—intercepts the request, asks the PDP, and enforces the returned decision. Decoupling them lets teams update authorization rules centrally without rewriting every service, though it adds a runtime dependency on the PDP.

What is the difference between a Policy Decision Point (PDP) and a Policy Enforcement Point (PEP)?

The PEP sits in the request path and actually allows or blocks the action, while the PDP evaluates the policy rules against the request’s attributes and returns the permit or deny answer for the PEP to enforce.

Defined in RFC 2904 and the XACML architecture, the PDP evaluates subject, resource, action, and environment attributes—often resolved via a Policy Information Point (PIP)—against policies authored in a Policy Administration Point (PAP). Architectural trade-offs center on co-locating the PDP as an in-process library or sidecar (sub-millisecond latency, eventual consistency of policy bundles) versus a remote PDP service (centralized state, network latency and availability risk). Edge cases include fail-open vs. fail-closed behavior during PDP outages, stale PIP caches, and rule-combining conflicts such as deny-overrides versus permit-overrides.
