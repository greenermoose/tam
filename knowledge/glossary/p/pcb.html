Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with P / PCB (Printed Circuit Board)

A PCB (Printed Circuit Board) is the flat rigid or flexible board that physically holds electronic components and connects them with copper pathways.

Open up almost any computer or gadget and you will see a green, blue, or black board covered in chips, resistors, and sockets—that is a PCB. Instead of a tangled bird’s nest of loose wires, thin copper lines printed onto the board carry power and signals neatly between every part.

A modern motherboard or SSD is a multi-layer PCB: alternating sheets of insulating fiberglass (FR-4) and etched copper foil laminated together. Internal layers often act as dedicated power and ground planes to keep high-speed signals clean, while tiny plated holes called vias connect traces between layers. When reading hardware schematics or diagnosing damaged boards, you will see surface-mount pads, through-hole pins, and silkscreen labels like R12 or U3.

Why do computer motherboards use multi-layer PCBs instead of putting every copper trace on one side of a single-layer board?

Modern chips have hundreds of pins and high-speed signals that cannot cross on a single plane without shorting. Multiple copper layers let traces route over and under one another via vias, while dedicated ground planes shield signals from interference.

A multi-layer PCB laminates prepreg and core dielectric sheets (commonly FR-4, or low-loss Rogers/Megtron laminates for multi-gigabit SerDes) with electrodeposited copper foil, interconnected via through-hole, blind, or buried microvias. High-speed digital design treats traces as controlled-impedance transmission lines (typically 50 Ω single-ended, 85–100 Ω differential) governed by trace width, dielectric thickness, and reference-plane continuity. Manufacturing relies on Gerber/ODB++ artwork, solder-mask dams, surface finishes (ENIG, HASL), and design-for-test (DFT) fiducials and ICT test points.
