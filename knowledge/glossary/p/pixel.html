Pixel (picture element)

One tiny dot of color in a digital image or on a screen.

Imagine a giant mosaic made of tiny square tiles, or a drawing colored in on graph paper. From normal viewing distance the squares blend into smooth photos and crisp letters, but if you zoom way in you can see each individual tile—that single square of color is a pixel. Packing more pixels into the same space makes images look sharper.

In raster images and framebuffers, each pixel stores color values—commonly red, green, and blue (RGB) channels, often plus an alpha channel for transparency (RGBA) at 8 bits per channel. On high-DPI displays, software often works in logical pixels that the compositor scales onto multiple physical hardware pixels (each made of even smaller red, green, and blue subpixels) so text stays readable rather than microscopic.

Why can a 100×100 pixel icon look crisp on a standard monitor yet blurry on a 4K laptop screen if it isn’t scaled properly?

On a high-DPI screen with 2× scaling, each logical pixel spans a 2×2 block of physical pixels; if no higher-resolution asset is available, the compositor stretches the original 100×100 grid, softening edges.

A pixel is a discrete point sample on a 2D spatial lattice rather than a physical rectangle; reconstructing or resampling it without aliasing requires band-limiting per the Nyquist–Shannon theorem. In memory, pixels are packed according to a pixel format (such as DRM/FourCC ARGB8888 or XRGB2101010) with row alignment governed by a byte stride (pitch), and alpha blending depends on whether components are straight or premultiplied and whether operations occur in sRGB-encoded or linear light space. Physical displays further map logical pixels through fractional scaling, subpixel layouts (RGB stripe, PenTile), and display-panel LUTs.
