PFC (Power Factor Correction)

Power Factor Correction is a circuit stage in modern power supplies that shapes alternating-current (AC) input draw into a smooth sine wave aligned with the wall voltage.

A basic power supply gulps electricity in short, sharp bursts at the peak of each power wave, which wastes capacity in home wiring and heats up transformers. A PFC circuit acts like a smooth regulator at the power supply’s front door, sipping electricity evenly across the entire wave so the wall outlet and power grid can deliver energy cleanly and efficiently.

Almost all modern desktop PC power supplies (PSUs) use Active PFC, achieving a power factor above 0.95 and supporting universal 100–240 V AC input without a manual voltage-selector switch. In practice, Active PFC interacts with uninterruptible power supplies (UPS): budget stepped/simulated sine-wave UPS units can trigger over-current faults or shutdowns on Active PFC PSUs during switchover, whereas pure sine-wave UPS units handle them cleanly.

Why do modern PC power supplies with Active PFC rarely have a manual 115V/230V switch on the back?

An Active PFC stage boosts the rectified AC input to a fixed high DC bus voltage (typically around 380–400 V DC) across the entire 100–240 V AC range automatically, eliminating the need for a manual voltage-doubler switch.

Usually implemented as a continuous-conduction-mode (CCM) or boundary-conduction-mode (BCM) boost converter immediately after the bridge rectifier and EMI filter, Active PFC forces input current to track the instantaneous rectified line voltage while regulating the bulk DC bus (~380–400 V DC) ahead of the isolated SMPS stage. This minimizes Total Harmonic Distortion (THD) to meet IEC 61000-3-2 limits and keeps displacement and distortion power factor near unity. Design trade-offs include cold-start inrush current (requiring NTC thermistors and bypass relays), bulk-capacitor hold-up time, and sensitivity to non-sinusoidal UPS transfer gaps.
