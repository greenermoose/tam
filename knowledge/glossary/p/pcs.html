PCS (Post-Compromise Security)

Post-Compromise Security is a property of secure messaging protocols that allows a conversation to regain confidentiality after a temporary leak of its current session keys.

If someone briefly copies the key to your locked diary today, you want future pages to become private again once you change the lock. Post-Compromise Security works the same way for encrypted chat: even if an attacker steals your current messaging key, the app keeps mixing in brand-new secret keys as you and your friend reply, locking the attacker back out of future messages.

PCS complements Forward Secrecy (FS) in opposite directions of time: FS protects messages sent before a key leak, whereas PCS restores privacy for messages sent after a leak once fresh key material is exchanged. Protocols like Signal’s Double Ratchet provide PCS by mixing new Diffie–Hellman outputs on alternating turns, unlike static PGP keys where one leak exposes all future traffic until manual rotation.

How does Post-Compromise Security differ from Forward Secrecy?

Forward Secrecy protects past messages if today’s key is stolen, while Post-Compromise Security heals the session so future messages become secret again after a key compromise—assuming the attacker stops actively intercepting the exchange.

PCS guarantees future secrecy after full session-state exposure once honest parties complete at least one uncompromised asymmetric ratchet step (such as a DH ratchet round-trip in the Double Ratchet or a TreeKEM epoch update in MLS). Symmetric KDF ratchets alone provide forward secrecy without PCS because a leaked chain key yields all future symmetric keys; injecting fresh ephemeral DH or post-quantum KEM entropy breaks that dependency. Its fundamental boundary is active impersonation: if an adversary races to inject forged ratchet keys before honest parties complete a clean round-trip, healing fails.
