PKE (Public-Key Encryption)

Asymmetric encryption in which a public key encrypts data and only the matching private key can decrypt it.

Think of public-key encryption like a padlock you leave unlocked for anyone to take. Anyone can put a message in a box and snap your padlock shut—even though they cannot open it again—because only you hold the single physical key that unlocks it. This lets strangers send you private messages without ever meeting beforehand to share a password.

Because asymmetric math is much slower than symmetric ciphers and has strict message-size limits, real systems like TLS and PGP use hybrid encryption: PKE protects a short, randomly generated symmetric key, and a fast cipher such as AES or ChaCha20 encrypts the actual data. Note the direction too: for encryption, you use the recipient’s public key; for digital signatures, you sign with your own private key so others can verify with your public key.

If you want to send an encrypted file to Bob, which key do you encrypt it with?

Bob’s public key, because only Bob holds the matching private key needed to decrypt it—encrypting with your own key would prevent Bob from reading it.

Formally a tuple (KeyGen, Enc, Dec) targeting IND-CPA or IND-CCA2 security; raw algebraic schemes such as unpadded RSA are homomorphic and malleable, requiring randomized padding like OAEP to resist chosen-ciphertext attacks. Modern protocols (TLS 1.3, WireGuard, post-quantum ML-KEM / FIPS 203) largely avoid direct PKE key transport in favor of ephemeral Diffie–Hellman or IND-CCA2 Key Encapsulation Mechanisms (KEMs) paired with AEAD, eliminating Bleichenbacher-style padding oracles and providing forward secrecy.
