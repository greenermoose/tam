PCAP (Projected Capacitive Touch)

PCAP (Projected Capacitive Touch) is the touchscreen technology that senses a finger’s electrical field through a protective glass layer.

If you have ever tapped or pinched-to-zoom on a smartphone or laptop screen, you were using PCAP. Behind the glass sits an invisible grid of tiny sensors that notices where your finger changes the screen’s electrical field—even without pressing hard—which lets the screen track several fingers at once.

PCAP screens support multi-touch gestures (pinch, rotate, two-finger scroll) and work behind durable cover glass, unlike older resistive screens that needed physical pressure and only tracked one point. On Linux, PCAP controllers typically appear as hid-multitouch or I2C HID input devices you can inspect with libinput list-devices. (Do not confuse this hardware term with .pcap packet-capture files used by tcpdump and Wireshark.)

Why does a standard PCAP touchscreen respond to a bare finger or conductive stylus, but not to a regular plastic pen or thick winter gloves?

PCAP detects changes in mutual capacitance caused by a conductive object near the electrode grid. Insulators like plastic or thick dry fabric do not couple the electric field enough to register a touch.

A PCAP sensor uses an orthogonal X/Y grid of transparent Indium Tin Oxide (ITO) or metal-mesh electrodes separated by a thin dielectric under cover glass. In mutual-capacitance mode, the controller drives sequential transmit (Tx) lines and measures charge coupling at every receive (Rx) intersection; a grounded finger shunts electric field lines away from the Rx node, reducing mutual capacitance C_m at that coordinate and enabling unambiguous multi-touch localization without ghosting. Firmware on the touch IC performs baseline tracking, water/palm rejection, and 50/60 Hz common-mode noise filtering before reporting HID multitouch contacts (ABS_MT_POSITION_X/Y) over I2C or USB.
