PVC (Polyvinyl Chloride)

PVC (Polyvinyl Chloride) is a durable, flexible plastic commonly used as the outer jacket and wire insulation on everyday electrical and network cables.

PVC is the familiar smooth plastic coating around most power cords, speaker wires, and standard Ethernet patch cables you use around a desk. It is inexpensive, bends easily, and protects the copper wires inside from scrapes and moisture.

In structured cabling, jacket ratings matter for fire safety: standard PVC jackets (often rated CM or CMR for riser shafts) are fine for open-room patch cords, whereas cables routed above drop ceilings or inside air-return ducts (plenum spaces) require plenum-rated (CMP) jackets—typically fluoropolymers like FEP—because burning PVC releases dense smoke and corrosive hydrogen chloride gas.

Check: why should you avoid running ordinary PVC-jacketed Ethernet cable inside an HVAC air-return ceiling?

In a fire, standard PVC emits heavy toxic smoke and acidic hydrogen chloride gas that an air-return plenum would spread throughout the building; building codes require low-smoke plenum-rated (CMP) cable there instead.

Plasticized PVC offers good mechanical abrasion resistance and UL 94 V-0 flame retardancy (via chlorinated polymer chemistry), but its relatively high dielectric constant (~3.0–3.5) and dissipation factor (~0.01–0.02) cause noticeable insertion loss and skew at high frequencies. Consequently, high-speed Category 6A/8 Ethernet, 10G+ twinax, and RF coaxial cables frequently use foamed polyethylene (PE) or PTFE/FEP for conductor dielectrics while reserving PVC solely for the outer jacket—or replace PVC entirely with LSZH (Low Smoke Zero Halogen, polyolefin-based) jackets in transit, marine, and data-center environments.
