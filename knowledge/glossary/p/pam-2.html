Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with P / PAM-2 (NRZ / 2-level Pulse-Amplitude Modulation)

PAM-2 (also called NRZ) is a two-level electrical signaling method that sends one bit of data per symbol using a high or low voltage.

Imagine flipping a light switch either all the way up for “1” or all the way down for “0.” That is how PAM-2 works on a wire: every beat of the signal uses one of just two voltage levels, so the receiver only has to tell high from low. Because there are only two choices, it is simple, rugged, and resists electrical noise well.

Part of PAM modulation, PAM-2 (Non-Return-to-Zero, or NRZ) is the classic binary signaling used across SATA, USB 3.x, and PCIe 1.0 through 5.0. Because each symbol carries 1 bit, doubling data rate requires doubling the symbol rate (baud), which pushes signal frequencies higher until copper traces and cables attenuate the signal too heavily to recover cleanly.

Why did PCIe 6.0 move away from PAM-2 after PCIe 5.0?

At 64 GT/s, doubling the PAM-2 clock/symbol rate again would push the fundamental Nyquist frequency past what standard PCB copper traces can carry without excessive loss. Switching to 4-level signaling doubles bits per symbol instead of doubling frequency.

PAM-2 encodes 1 bit per unit interval (UI) as a two-level baseband waveform with a Nyquist frequency equal to half the baud rate (e.g., 16 GHz at 32 GT/s for PCIe 5.0). Compared to multi-level schemes, PAM-2 maximizes vertical eye opening and linearity tolerance, allowing simpler slicers and lower-overhead line coding (8b/10b or 128b/130b) without mandatory forward error correction (FEC). Its ceiling appears when channel insertion loss exceeds ~30–36 dB at Nyquist, where CTLE and DFE equalization can no longer close the loss budget without prohibitive power or reach penalties.
