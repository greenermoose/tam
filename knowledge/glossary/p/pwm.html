PWM - Pulse-Width Modulation

PWM (Pulse-Width Modulation) is a technique for controlling average electrical power or encoding a signal by switching a digital output on and off rapidly and varying how long it stays on in each cycle.

Imagine flickering a light switch on and off hundreds of times a second: your eyes cannot see the individual flashes, so if the switch is on half the time, the bulb looks half as bright. PWM works the same way for LEDs, laptop screen backlights, and PC cooling fans—instead of lowering the voltage directly, the computer pulses full power on and off faster than you can notice to smoothly control brightness or speed.

In PWM, the switching frequency stays fixed while the duty cycle (percentage of each cycle spent “on”) varies from 0% to 100%. On Linux hardware, 4-pin PC fans use a 25 kHz PWM control wire (exposed under /sys/class/hwmon/) so the fan motor runs quietly without audible whine, whereas low-frequency PWM (under a few hundred Hz) on cheap laptop displays can cause visible flicker or eye strain at low brightness.

Check: if a 12 V fan receives a 50% duty-cycle PWM signal, what is the average voltage across its control/power waveform—and why doesn’t it stutter?

The average is 6 V (half of 12 V). It spins smoothly rather than stuttering because the switching happens thousands of times per second, and the rotor’s mechanical inertia (or a circuit’s inductance/capacitance) averages out the rapid pulses.

A PWM waveform of period T and pulse width t_on has duty cycle D = t_on / T and DC component V_avg = D × V_high, plus harmonics at multiples of f_sw = 1/T whose amplitudes depend on D (peaking at D = 0.5 for odd harmonics). Effective filtering requires either a physical load time constant much larger than T (motor inductance/inertia, thermal mass) or an LC low-pass filter; otherwise ripple current, EMI, or audible magnetostriction/coil whine occurs. In embedded and Linux systems (/sys/class/pwm/), timer-counter resolution limits step granularity at high f_sw (e.g., a 100 MHz clock at 25 kHz yields 4000 steps ≈ 12 bits), creating a direct trade-off between switching frequency, resolution, and MOSFET switching losses.
