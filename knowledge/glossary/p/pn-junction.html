P-N Junction

The boundary inside a semiconductor where a positive-charge (P-type) region meets a negative-charge (N-type) region.

When silicon with extra electrons (N-type) is joined to silicon with missing-electron spaces (P-type), their meeting point acts like a one-way turnstile for electricity. Push current from one side and the gate opens easily; push from the opposite side and the middle zone widens into a barrier that blocks flow. This simple one-way behavior is the building block behind diodes, LEDs, solar cells, and transistors.

At the interface between P-type and N-type layers, mobile carriers diffuse across and recombine, leaving behind fixed ions that form a carrier-free depletion region with a built-in electric field. Applying a forward voltage (above roughly 0.7 V for silicon diodes, or 1.8–3.3 V for LEDs) collapses the barrier so current surges, whereas a reverse voltage widens the depletion zone and blocks current until breakdown.

What happens to the depletion region when you reverse-bias a P-N junction?

The external voltage pulls electrons and holes further away from the boundary, widening the insulating depletion region and preventing current from flowing (until reverse breakdown voltage is reached).

An anisotropic semiconductor interface governed by the Shockley ideal diode equation, I = Is(exp(qV / nkT) − 1), with built-in potential Vbi = (kT/q) ln(NAND / ni2). Its voltage-dependent depletion capacitance Cj underpins varactors, while minority-carrier storage limits reverse-recovery switching speed. In direct-bandgap III–V materials, forward-bias radiative recombination emits photons (LEDs, laser diodes), whereas reverse-bias photon absorption sweeps out electron-hole pairs as photocurrent (photodiodes).
