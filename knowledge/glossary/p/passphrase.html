Passphrase

A passphrase is a secret made from a sequence of words rather than a single short string, used to prove identity or unlock encrypted data.

Instead of trying to memorize a jumble like Tr0ub4dor&3, a passphrase strings together several unrelated words—like correct-horse-battery-staple. Because it is much longer, it is far harder for an attacker’s computer to guess, yet it is still easy for a person to remember and type when unlocking a laptop or an SSH key.

In Linux and security tooling, passphrases commonly protect LUKS full-disk encryption volumes, SSH private keys (ssh-keygen), and GPG keys. Unlike a short password with forced symbols, a passphrase gets its strength from length and randomness across a large word list (such as Diceware), making it resistant to brute-force guessing while remaining practical to enter at boot. You should still avoid famous quotes or song lyrics, which attackers include in cracking dictionaries.

Why are four or five randomly chosen common words usually stronger than an 8-character password with symbols?

Guesses scale exponentially with length and vocabulary size. An 8-character password has relatively few total combinations, whereas five random words from a 7,776-word list yield over 10^19 possibilities—making brute-force guessing vastly slower.

A passphrase provides high Shannon entropy (typically 50–80+ bits when sampled uniformly from a large wordlist, e.g., ~12.9 bits per Diceware word) in a form that avoids human-predictable substitution patterns. Because passphrases are still low-entropy compared to 128- or 256-bit symmetric keys, systems never use them directly as cipher keys; instead, they feed the passphrase and a random salt into a slow, memory-hard Key Derivation Function (such as Argon2id in LUKS2 or OpenSSH’s bcrypt_pbkdf) to derive a Key Encryption Key (KEK) that unwraps the randomly generated volume or private key.
