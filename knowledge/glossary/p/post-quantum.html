Post-quantum cryptography

Classical algorithms designed to resist attacks by both ordinary and quantum computers.

Today’s online locks rely on math puzzles that are hard for normal computers, but a large future quantum computer could solve them quickly. Post-quantum (or quantum-safe) cryptography swaps in new puzzles that run on your everyday laptop or phone today, protecting secrets recorded now from being decrypted years later.

Post-quantum algorithms such as ML-KEM (key exchange) and ML-DSA (signatures) replace RSA and elliptic-curve primitives, often deployed in hybrid modes like X25519MLKEM768 during migration. Expect larger keys and signatures than traditional elliptic-curve schemes.

Why do modern TLS connections often combine X25519 with ML-KEM instead of switching to ML-KEM alone?

A hybrid key exchange pairs a well-tested classical algorithm (X25519) with a newer post-quantum one (ML-KEM). An attacker must break both to recover the session key, protecting against both future quantum computers and any unforeseen flaw in the newer scheme.

Cryptographic primitives executing on classical hardware whose hardness assumptions—such as Module-LWE (ML-KEM, ML-DSA), stateless hash-based Merkle trees (SLH-DSA), or syndrome decoding—have no known polynomial-time quantum algorithm (Shor’s algorithm breaks factoring and ECDLP, while Grover’s algorithm yields at most a quadratic speedup countered by doubling symmetric key lengths). Operational trade-offs include kilobyte-scale keys and signatures that can trigger UDP fragmentation in DNSSEC or IKEv2 and exceed legacy handshake buffer limits.
