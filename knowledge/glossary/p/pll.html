PLL (Phase-Locked Loop)

A feedback circuit that locks an oscillator’s frequency and phase to a steady reference clock.

Think of a drummer keeping a band locked onto a steady metronome: whenever the drummer drifts slightly fast or slow, they hear the click and nudge their tempo back in line. Inside a computer, a single slow quartz crystal sets that steady beat, and a PLL lets chips run billions of times faster while staying tightly synchronized instead of drifting out of step.

CPUs, GPUs, and memory controllers use PLLs to multiply a low-frequency board crystal (often 24–100 MHz) up to multi-gigahertz operating clocks and to step frequencies up or down dynamically for power management. High-speed serial links such as PCIe, DisplayPort, USB, and SATA also rely on PLLs inside clock-data recovery (CDR) circuits to extract timing directly from an incoming data stream.

Why do modern processors use a PLL instead of putting a 5 GHz crystal directly on the motherboard?

Quartz crystals cannot vibrate reliably at multi-gigahertz frequencies and high-speed clock traces across a board would suffer severe loss and interference; a PLL safely multiplies a clean, low-frequency reference right inside the chip.

A closed-loop control system comprising a phase-frequency detector (PFD), charge pump, low-pass loop filter, voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO), and a feedback divider (integer-N or sigma-delta fractional-N). Loop-filter bandwidth governs the core trade-off between settling time, reference-jitter attenuation (low-pass), and VCO phase-noise suppression (high-pass), while fractional-N spurs and supply-induced jitter must be bounded to meet eye-diagram and setup/hold budgets during DVFS transitions.
