PPT (Probabilistic Polynomial-Time)

Standard theoretical model of an efficient attacker with polynomial runtime and random coins.

When cryptographers say a lock is secure, they do not mean it can withstand infinite time—anyone could guess a password given trillions of years. Instead, PPT describes a realistic attacker using ordinary computers who can make smart random guesses within a practical amount of time.

Classical security definitions prove that no PPT adversary can break a scheme with non-negligible probability (roughly, noticeably better than blind guessing). Contrast PPT with unbounded adversaries (information-theoretic security, like a one-time pad) and quantum adversaries.

Why does practical cryptography define security against PPT attackers rather than attackers with unlimited time?

Almost all practical encryption and signatures can be broken by brute-force search given infinite time. Bounding the attacker to polynomial time captures what is feasible in reality while enabling rigorous mathematical proofs.

Formally, a randomized Turing machine (or non-uniform circuit family in P/poly) running in time poly(lambda) in the security parameter lambda, with success probability bounded by a negligible function negl(lambda). In post-quantum security models, PPT is replaced by QPT (quantum polynomial-time), where classical rewinding proof techniques often fail in the Quantum Random Oracle Model because quantum states cannot be cloned or trivially rewound after measurement.
