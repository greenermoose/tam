Scaling

Resizing an image, video, or user interface so that its dimensions or visual elements fit a target display or resolution.

Scaling makes text, icons, and windows bigger or smaller on your screen. On a very sharp, high-resolution laptop display, everything would look tiny at its original size, so scaling enlarges the interface to keep it comfortable to read.

Desktop environments offer integer scaling (such as 100% or 200%, where each logical pixel maps cleanly to a 2×2 block of physical pixels) and fractional scaling (such as 125% or 150%). Integer scaling stays crisp, while fractional scaling often requires the compositor to render at a larger size and downsample, which can slightly soften older apps—especially X11 applications running through XWayland on a Wayland desktop.

Why does 200% display scaling usually look sharper than 150% scaling?

At 200%, one logical pixel maps evenly onto an exact 2×2 grid of physical pixels with no rounding. At 150%, edges fall between physical pixel boundaries, requiring interpolation or downsampling that can introduce slight blur.

Display and image scaling operate in distinct domains: raster resampling maps discrete samples between grids using kernels (nearest-neighbor, bilinear, bicubic, or Lanczos) with trade-offs between aliasing, ringing, and sharpness, whereas UI scaling transforms logical coordinates to device pixels. Under Wayland, wl_surface.set_buffer_scale supports integer factors natively, while wp_fractional_scale_v1 lets clients render directly at the exact fractional pixel grid rather than forcing the compositor to downscale an oversampled buffer. Legacy X11 clients lacking HiDPI awareness are either upscaled by the compositor (blurry) or rendered unscaled when XWayland scaling is disabled.
