Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with S / Slab serif

Slab serif fonts have heavy, rectangular serifs that look like small blocks at the ends of strokes.

Some serif fonts have bold, square feet rather than delicate hooks. That blocky style is called slab serif. It looks sturdy and is common in posters, newspapers and logos, where strong shapes survive distance and rough printing.

Slab serifs became popular in the nineteenth century for advertising and display printing; examples include Roboto Slab and Merriweather. They may be bracketed, joining the stroke with a curve, or unbracketed, meeting at a right angle. Heavy slabs work well in headings but can crowd small text.

Slab serifs are a classification within serif type rather than a technical feature: the serifs are contours in the outlines and can be monolinear or contrast-heavy. In variable fonts a weight axis can thicken stems faster than serifs, changing the visual effect. Dense slab serifs reduce counter space at small sizes, so screen designs often open apertures.
