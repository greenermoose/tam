Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with S / Semiconductor

A semiconductor is a material whose electrical conductivity sits between a conductor and an insulator and can be switched on or off by tiny electrical signals.

Copper wires always let electricity flow, and rubber coatings always block it. A semiconductor—most famously silicon—is special because it can act like either one depending on a tiny control signal. By shrinking billions of these controllable switches onto a fingernail-sized silicon wafer, engineers build the processors and memory chips inside computers and phones.

In pure silicon, electrons are mostly locked in a crystal lattice, separated from the conduction band by a moderate energy bandgap. Adding trace impurities (doping)—such as phosphorus for extra electrons (N-type) or boron for electron-deficient “holes” (P-type)—lets engineers build P-N junctions (diodes) and metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs). Unlike metals, whose resistance rises when heated, a semiconductor’s conductivity increases with temperature because heat frees more charge carriers, which is why overheating chips can suffer thermal runaway if uncooled.

Why does doping pure silicon with trace impurities make it useful for building transistors?

Pure silicon has too few free charge carriers at room temperature to carry strong currents. Doping introduces controlled densities of electrons (N-type) or holes (P-type), enabling P-N junctions and electric fields to switch current cleanly between conducting and blocking states.

Semiconductor behavior is governed by Fermi–Dirac carrier statistics across an indirect or direct bandgap (1.12 eV in crystalline Si at 300 K; 1.42 eV in GaAs; ~3.26 eV in 4H-SiC). Under the drift–diffusion model, carrier transport combines electric-field drift and concentration-gradient diffusion, while Poisson’s equation couples space-charge regions in depletion zones. Modern CMOS logic scales MOSFETs down to FinFET and gate-all-around (GAA) nanosheet geometries, where short-channel effects—drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL), subthreshold leakage, and gate-oxide tunneling—trade off dynamic switching power against static leakage.
