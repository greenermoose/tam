Suspend

Suspend is a low-power sleep state that pauses your computer while keeping your open programs and data alive in memory.

Closing your laptop lid or choosing “Suspend” is like putting a bookmark in a book and turning off the lamp. Your screen, fans, and hard drive power down to save battery, but everything you were working on stays waiting in memory. When you open the lid or press a key, the computer wakes up in a second or two right where you left off—unlike a full shutdown, which closes everything.

Because suspend keeps your session in RAM rather than saving it to disk (which is called hibernate), it wakes almost instantly but still sips a small amount of battery power. On Linux, you can trigger it from the desktop menu or with systemctl suspend. If a laptop’s battery drains completely while suspended, unsaved work in RAM is lost—which is why some setups use suspend-then-hibernate.

Why does suspend wake up much faster than hibernate, and what is the trade-off?

Suspend keeps system state in fast RAM, so resuming only needs to power devices back on; hibernate writes RAM to disk and powers off completely, taking longer to resume but using zero battery while asleep.

On x86/ACPI Linux systems, suspend typically targets either deep (ACPI S3, Suspend-to-RAM, where the CPU powers off and DRAM enters self-refresh) or s2idle (ACPI S0ix, Modern Standby, a software-driven low-power idle state required on many modern laptops that lack S3 firmware support). The kernel writes the chosen mode to /sys/power/mem_sleep and initiates freeze-tasks, device suspend() callbacks, and non-boot CPU offlining via /sys/power/state (mem). Wakeup events are governed by ACPI GPEs and /proc/acpi/wakeup or power/wakeup sysfs attributes; misconfigured USB/PCIe wake sources or buggy GPU/Wi-Fi driver resume hooks are the most common causes of spurious wakes or battery drain in s2idle.
