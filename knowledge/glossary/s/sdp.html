SDP (Service Discovery Protocol / Session Description Protocol)

SDP refers to two unrelated networking protocols depending on context: Bluetooth’s Service Discovery Protocol for finding device capabilities, and the Session Description Protocol for negotiating audio and video streams.

Imagine walking into a restaurant and asking for a menu before ordering. When your laptop pairs with wireless headphones over Bluetooth, it uses Bluetooth’s SDP to ask, “What can you do—play music, act as a headset microphone, or both?” Separately, in internet video calls, the other SDP is the “menu” two computers trade at the start of a call so they agree on audio and video formats before the call begins.

Because the acronym is shared, check the protocol stack to tell them apart: Bluetooth SDP runs over L2CAP when pairing or inspecting devices (for example, with sdptool or bluetoothctl info), whereas streaming SDP appears as plain-text lines (v=0, m=audio ..., a=rtpmap:...) inside SIP invites or WebRTC offers. In Bluetooth, missing or misadvertised SDP records explain why a device pairs successfully yet lacks an expected profile such as A2DP stereo audio or HFP calling.

If a Bluetooth headset pairs but only shows low-quality call audio instead of stereo music, how does SDP help diagnose it?

SDP exposes the device’s advertised service UUIDs and profile records. Inspecting the SDP records shows whether the headset actually advertised the A2DP Sink UUID or only HFP/HSP, separating a device capability issue from a PipeWire/PulseAudio routing problem.

Bluetooth SDP (Core Specification Vol. 3, Part B) operates over L2CAP PSM 0x0001 using request/response PDUs (ServiceSearchRequest, ServiceAttributeRequest, ServiceSearchAttributeRequest) to query attribute-value records keyed by 16-bit or 128-bit UUIDs and protocol descriptor lists; BlueZ exposes these profiles over D-Bus rather than raw L2CAP to unprivileged clients. By contrast, IETF RFC 8866 Session Description Protocol is a transport-independent UTF-8 text format describing multimedia session parameters (c= connection address, m= media lines, a= codec/ICE/DTLS attributes) carried inside signaling protocols such as SIP or WebRTC’s offer/answer model (RFC 8829). A key edge case in WebRTC is that mismatched a=fmtp or a=setup roles in SDP cause ICE/DTLS negotiation to fail silently at media startup even when signaling succeeds.
