STX (Start of Text, 0x02)

STX (Start of Text) is the ASCII control character with byte value 0x02, sent in terminals when you press Ctrl+B.

In the early days of teletypes and serial cables, machines needed invisible “traffic signals” inside a message to know where the header stopped and the actual text began. STX was the signal meaning “the real message starts now.” Today, you rarely think about those old serial messages, yet pressing Ctrl+B on your keyboard still sends that exact same byte (0x02) to terminal programs.

STX belongs to the C0 control codes (bytes 0x00–0x1F). Because holding Ctrl clears bit 6 (and bit 5) of an ASCII letter, Ctrl+B maps from 'B' (0x42) to 0x02. In modern Linux workflows, you most often encounter STX as the default prefix key in tmux: pressing Ctrl+B tells tmux to intercept the next keystroke as a multiplexer command rather than forwarding it to the running shell.

If Ctrl+B is intercepted by tmux as its prefix, how do you send an actual Ctrl+B (STX) to a program inside tmux?

Press Ctrl+B twice. The first Ctrl+B enters tmux’s command mode, and the second Ctrl+B invokes send-prefix, forwarding one literal 0x02 byte to the active pane.

Defined in ANSI X3.4 / ISO/IEC 646 and ECMA-48 as 0x02 (^B), STX was designed as a transmission-control delimiter paired with SOH (0x01, Start of Heading) and ETX (0x03, End of Text) for framing bisync/serial blocks. Under standard POSIX termios canonical or raw input, 0x02 has no special kernel line-discipline meaning (unlike 0x03, which defaults to VINTR and raises SIGINT), so it passes straight to the reading process. Applications therefore repurpose it freely: tmux binds it as prefix, while Readline/Emacs-mode shells bind ^B to backward-char, which is why tmux users often rebind the prefix or use send-prefix to avoid shadowing cursor movement.
