SVID (Serial Voltage Identification)

SVID (Serial Voltage Identification) is the dedicated high-speed serial bus that an Intel processor uses to tell its motherboard’s voltage regulator module (VRM) what voltage to supply.

A processor does not run at one fixed voltage all the time. When you are reading a document it sips power, and when you launch a heavy program it needs a sudden boost. SVID is like a private intercom between the processor and the power circuitry on the motherboard, letting the chip ask for just enough electrical pressure from moment to moment without wasting energy or overheating.

In Linux, power-management governors (such as intel_pstate) and idle drivers request performance states (P-states) and deep sleep states (C-states). The processor’s internal power control unit (PCU) translates those requests into VID codes sent over SVID to the VRM, stepping core and uncore rails up for turbo frequencies or down toward zero in package C10. Unlike older parallel VID pins that required many motherboard traces for each rail, SVID carries commands over a compact serial link.

Why can’t the Linux kernel set CPU core voltage directly over SVID?

The SVID bus connects the CPU’s internal power control unit directly to the VRM, not to operating-system software. Linux requests frequency or power-saving targets through ACPI or MSRs, and the CPU firmware decides the safe voltage step to command over SVID.

Intel’s SVID specification defines a 3-wire serial interface—clock (SVC), bidirectional data (SVD), and an active-low alert (ALERT#)—running at tens of MHz between the processor’s integrated VR controller (master) and external PWM controllers (slaves) across multiple rails (VR0, VR1, etc.). Commands include SetVID_Fast, SetVID_Slow, SetVID_Decay, SetPS (power-state and phase shedding), and telemetry reads (GetReg for current, temperature, and power). A key failure mode is SVID signal-integrity degradation or a misconfigured VRM AC/DC loadline, which causes Vdroop overshoot or undershoot during transient current steps (di/dt) and can trigger machine-check exceptions or VR-HOT throttling even when average temperatures look normal.
