SMPS (Switched-Mode Power Supply)

A power converter that efficiently steps electrical voltage up or down by switching current on and off rapidly rather than burning off excess energy as heat.

Think of filling a bucket with a fast-tapping faucet rather than letting a full hose run while draining the extra water on the ground. Older power bricks wasted extra voltage as heat, which made them heavy and hot. A switched-mode power supply (SMPS) flips electricity on and off thousands of times a second and smooths the pulses out, so laptop chargers and PC power supplies stay small, cool, and energy-efficient.

In Linux laptops and desktops, the AC adapter and motherboard VRMs (voltage regulator modules) are SMPS designs. Unlike linear regulators—which drop excess voltage as heat and suit low-noise, low-current analog rails—SMPS converters routinely reach 85–95%+ efficiency, at the cost of high-frequency switching ripple and electromagnetic interference (EMI) that require inductor-capacitor filtering. On Linux, you can inspect power-supply telemetry under /sys/class/power_supply/ or VRM and fan sensors via lm-sensors.

Why does an SMPS stay much cooler than a linear regulator when dropping 12 V down to 1.2 V at high current?

A linear regulator acts like a variable resistor, dissipating the voltage difference times current as heat (nearly 90% wasted here). An SMPS switches its transistor fully on (near-zero voltage drop) or fully off (zero current) and stores energy in an inductor, so far less power turns into heat.

An SMPS regulates output voltage via pulse-width modulation (PWM) or pulse-frequency modulation (PFM) of MOSFET or GaN switches operating from tens of kHz to several MHz across topologies such as buck, boost, flyback, and LLC resonant converters. Key failure modes and design trade-offs include inductor saturation, switching and gate-drive losses, output-capacitor ESR ripple, control-loop phase-margin instability under transient loads (such as CPU turbo steps), and conducted or radiated EMI requiring snubbers and spread-spectrum clocking.
