Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with S / SCP (Short-Circuit Protection)

Short-Circuit Protection (SCP) is a fast-acting safety cutoff in a power supply that shuts down output power when electrical resistance drops near zero.

If two bare power wires accidentally touch, electricity rushes through the shortcut in a dangerous surge that can melt cables or start a fire. SCP acts like an instant reflex that cuts the power off in a fraction of a second before heat or sparks can damage your computer or charger.

Unlike a traditional glass fuse that takes milliseconds or seconds to melt, electronic SCP inside ATX power supplies and USB-C PD chargers senses the sudden voltage collapse and trips in microseconds. Depending on the design, the supply either latches off until you unplug and reset it, or enters "hiccup mode," briefly retrying every second until the short is removed. Note that in Linux networking, "scp" also refers to the Secure Copy command—a completely unrelated term.

Why does a power supply often need to be unplugged for several seconds after SCP trips?

Many power supplies use a latched SCP design that keeps the output disabled until the primary bulk capacitors discharge and the controller resets, preventing repeated arcing into a persistent fault.

SCP detects near-zero load impedance by monitoring current-sense shunts, MOSFET R_DS(on) drops, or output rail undervoltage (UVP) coupled with current-limit saturation (OCP). In ATX12V/EPS12V PSUs and USB-PD controllers, the protection loop blanks brief capacitive inrush transients (hundreds of microseconds) before disabling PWM gate drives within 10–100 µs. Failure modes include high-resistance "soft shorts" (e.g., damaged 12VHPWR contacts) that draw high current without collapsing rail voltage below the SCP threshold, causing thermal damage before OCP or OTP engages.
