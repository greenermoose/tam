Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with S / Serif

A serif is the small foot or hook at the end of a letter stroke, and serif fonts include these finishing details.

Look at the letters in a printed book: many have tiny feet and hooks at the ends of their strokes. Those little marks are serifs. Fonts with them are called serif fonts; fonts without them are sans-serif.

Serifs originated in carved and written letterforms and became a defining feature of Roman and Renaissance type. Families such as Liberation Serif, Georgia and Times suit long passages because the details guide the eye along the line. Screen versions often use slightly sturdier serifs to survive rasterization.

Serifs are part of the glyph outline, not separate marks, and come in bracketed, hairline, slab and wedge forms. Classification distinguishes old-style, transitional, Didone and slab serif families by stroke contrast and shape. At small pixel sizes thin hairline serifs can disappear under hinting, so screen-oriented designs thicken them; the trait is not encoded in SFNT tables.
