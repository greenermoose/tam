Serial Communication

Serial communication is a method of sending data one bit at a time in sequence over a single wire or differential pair.

Imagine moving a crowd through a hallway: parallel communication uses eight lanes side by side, while serial communication uses a single-file line. Although single-file sounds slower, modern electronics can send bits down one line billions of times per second without the wires interfering with each other or arriving out of step. That is why almost every modern cable and slot—including USB, SATA, and PCIe—is serial.

On Linux, you encounter classic asynchronous serial ports as character devices like /dev/ttyS0 or /dev/ttyUSB0 (UARTs configured with baud rate, data bits, parity, and stop bits such as 115200 8N1), while high-speed internal buses (PCIe, SATA, USB, Ethernet) use differential serial lanes. Parallel buses fell out of favor at high frequencies because tiny length differences between traces cause clock skew—bits from the same byte arrive at different times—whereas serial lanes can be clocked far faster per wire.

Why did high-speed computer buses like PCIe and SATA switch from parallel cables to serial lanes?

At high frequencies, parallel wires suffer from clock skew and crosstalk between adjacent traces, limiting how fast a shared clock can run. Serial links avoid inter-wire skew and use differential signaling, allowing much higher per-lane bit rates.

High-speed serial links replace a forwarded parallel clock with Serializer/Deserializer (SerDes) transceivers and Clock Data Recovery (CDR). To guarantee enough edge transitions for CDR phase locking and maintain DC balance across AC-coupled differential pairs, links apply line codes such as 8b/10b, 64b/66b, or 128b/130b (PCIe Gen3–5) and PAM4 with forward error correction (PCIe Gen6). At multi-gigabit rates, skin effect and dielectric loss attenuate high frequencies, requiring transmitter pre-emphasis/de-emphasis and receiver CTLE/DFE equalization to keep the eye diagram open.
