SVG (Scalable Vector Graphics)

SVG (Scalable Vector Graphics) is an XML-based image format that describes shapes with math rather than pixels, so graphics stay sharp at any size.

Most photos are made of tiny colored squares called pixels—if you zoom way in, they turn blocky and blurry. SVG works differently: it stores instructions like “draw a blue circle here with a smooth curve” instead of a grid of dots. Because your computer redraws those instructions fresh at whatever size you need, SVG icons and logos look crisp on both a small laptop screen and a giant 4K monitor.

Because SVG files are plain XML text, you can open one in a text editor, inspect or tweak colors directly, and compress it as .svgz. On Linux desktops, icon themes and UI widgets rely heavily on SVG so HiDPI and fractional scaling stay razor-sharp without shipping dozens of pre-rendered PNG sizes. Unlike photos (where JPEG or WebP is better), SVG shines for logos, diagrams, and interface icons.

Why are SVG files great for desktop icons and diagrams, but a poor fit for camera photographs?

Photographs have millions of subtle color variations that would take an enormous number of vector shapes to describe, making an SVG huge and slow; pixel-based formats like JPEG or WebP compress natural photos much better.

Standardized by the W3C (SVG 1.1 / SVG 2), SVG encodes a retained-mode scene graph of geometric primitives, cubic/quadratic Bézier <path> data, affine transforms, gradients, and CSS styling inside XML. On Linux, desktop toolkits rasterize SVGs at target DPI via librsvg (GTK/GNOME, Cairo/Pango-backed) or QtSvg (Qt/KDE). Important caveats: QtSvg historically targets the SVG 1.2 Tiny profile and drops advanced SVG filters or embedded CSS features, and untrusted SVGs pose security/DoS risks (XXE in naive XML parsers, billion-laughs entity expansion, or expensive Gaussian-blur filter chains) unless sanitized or rendered in a restricted parser.
