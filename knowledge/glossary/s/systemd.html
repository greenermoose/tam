Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with S / systemd

systemd is the program that starts and supervises most services after the Linux kernel finishes early boot on current Tamlinux systems.

When you turn on your computer, the Linux kernel wakes up the hardware first, and then hands the baton to an “init” program—on current Arch-based Tamlinux systems, that program is systemd. Think of it as the building manager that turns on the lights, starts networking, launches the login screen, and keeps background services running or restarts them if they crash. (A future Void rootstock may use runit instead.)

systemd organizes work into units—such as .service (daemons), .socket (on-demand activation), .timer (scheduled jobs), and .target (synchronization milestones similar to traditional runlevels). You inspect and control units with systemctl (for example, systemctl status or systemctl --user) and read structured logs from the journal with journalctl (journalctl -b -p err). When troubleshooting a boot or desktop problem, checking systemd units and the journal helps separate a failed service from a kernel or compositor failure.

Why does systemd run as PID 1, and why does that matter for troubleshooting?

The kernel spawns the init process as PID 1, making systemd the ancestor of all other user-space services and the reaper of orphaned processes. Because it starts and tracks every unit, its state and journal are the first place to check whether a service never started, crashed, or timed out.

On the current Arch-based Tamlinux rootstock, systemd acts as PID 1 (and per-user systemd --user manager) using cgroups v2 (systemd.slice) to isolate, resource-limit, and reliably tear down entire process trees without PID-file races. Activation relies on dependency graphs (Wants=, Requires=, Before=, After=) combined with socket and D-Bus activation so independent services start in parallel. For forensics, systemd-journald indexes structured binary logs (_SYSTEMD_UNIT, _BOOT_ID, monotonic and wall timestamps) alongside kernel kmsg, making unit exit codes, cgroup OOM kills, and boot-order stalls directly queryable.
