SATA - Serial ATA

A standard hardware interface and serial protocol used to connect storage drives—such as hard disks, SSDs, and optical drives—to a computer’s motherboard.

SATA is the flat cable and connector inside many desktop computers and older laptops that links hard drives and 2.5-inch SSDs to the main board. It carries both commands ("read this file") and the actual data back and forth.

Unlike older wide ribbon cables (PATA/IDE) that sent data across many parallel wires at once, SATA uses a thin point-to-point serial link that allows better airflow and hot-plugging. SATA III tops out at 6 Gbit/s (roughly 550–600 MB/s in real transfers), which is plenty for spinning hard drives but much slower than modern NVMe SSDs connected directly over PCIe. On Linux, SATA drives usually appear as /dev/sda, /dev/sdb, and so on.

Why are modern M.2 NVMe SSDs several times faster than SATA SSDs even though both use flash memory?

A SATA SSD is bottlenecked by the 6 Gbit/s SATA bus and the older AHCI command protocol (1 queue of 32 commands), whereas NVMe connects directly over PCIe lanes and supports tens of thousands of parallel queues.

Serial ATA (SATA 1.0/2.0/3.0 at 1.5, 3.0, and 6.0 Gbit/s using 8b/10b encoding, yielding 150/300/600 MB/s raw payload) transports ATA commands encapsulated in Frame Information Structures (FIS) over differential TX/RX pairs. Most controllers expose AHCI (Advanced Host Controller Interface) to the OS, enabling Native Command Queuing (NCQ, up to 32 outstanding commands) and hot-plug via staggered spin-up and link-state transitions (PHYRDY). In Linux, libata and ahci manage link negotiation, error recovery (soft/hard resets on CRC or 8b/10b disparity errors from marginal cabling), and expose devices through the SCSI subsystem (sd).
