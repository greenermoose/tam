SIDH (Supersingular Isogeny Diffie-Hellman)

SIDH (Supersingular Isogeny Diffie–Hellman) was a proposed post-quantum key-exchange protocol that was completely broken by classical mathematical attacks in 2022.

SIDH was a candidate encryption method designed to stay safe even against future quantum computers, while keeping the keys much smaller than most alternatives. However, in 2022 researchers discovered a clever shortcut that lets an ordinary laptop break SIDH in minutes. Its story shows why new security tools go through years of public testing before anyone relies on them in real software.

SIDH—and its NIST submission SIKE—looked attractive because its public keys were only a few hundred bytes, far smaller than lattice-based schemes like ML-KEM (Kyber). Yet in July 2022, Wouter Castryck and Thomas Decru published an attack breaking SIDH in about an hour on a single CPU core, without needing a quantum computer at all. The key takeaway for practitioners is never to deploy unstandardized or experimental cryptography in production, and to prefer vetted post-quantum standards such as ML-KEM.

Why was the 2022 break of SIDH/SIKE especially surprising to the cryptography community?

SIDH was built to resist quantum computers, yet it was broken by a classical algorithm running on an ordinary computer in minutes, exploiting extra information the protocol published alongside its keys.

SIDH exchanged keys by walking secret isogeny paths in the supersingular elliptic-curve graph over F_(p^2), which required publishing auxiliary torsion-point images (φ(P_B), φ(Q_B)) so both parties could complete the commutative diagram. Castryck and Decru (2022), followed by Maino–Martindale and Robert, showed that these torsion-point images—combined with Kani’s theorem gluing two elliptic curves into a principally polarized abelian surface (and higher-dimensional abelian varieties via Zarhin’s trick)—allow recovering the secret isogeny in polynomial time classically, completely breaking SIDH and SIKE. (Note that related isogeny schemes without exposed torsion-point images, such as CSIDH or SQIsign, are not directly broken by this attack.)
