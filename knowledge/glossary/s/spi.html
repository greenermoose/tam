SPI - Serial Peripheral Interface

A fast, short-distance serial communication bus used to connect a processor or microcontroller to chips like flash memory, sensors, and small displays.

Imagine a teacher (the main chip) talking to several students (peripherals) using four shared wires: a clock beat to keep everyone in step, one wire for the teacher to speak, one wire for the student to reply, and a separate “tap on the shoulder” wire for each student so only the chosen one answers. Because it is simple and has a dedicated clock wire, SPI is much faster than many other small-board connections.

A standard 4-wire SPI bus uses SCLK (clock), MOSI (controller out, peripheral in), MISO (controller in, peripheral out), and CS/SS (chip select, active-low per device). Unlike I2C, SPI has no built-in device addresses—each peripheral needs its own CS line—but it supports full-duplex transfers at tens of MHz. On Linux, SPI controllers and devices appear under /sys/bus/spi/ and optional userspace nodes /dev/spidevX.Y (used by tools like flashrom for BIOS/UEFI SPI NOR flash).

If you connect three SPI sensors to one controller, why do you need three separate chip-select (CS) wires instead of just two shared data wires?

SPI has no addressing bytes in its protocol. All devices share SCLK, MOSI, and MISO, so each peripheral requires its own CS line to know when to listen and when to tri-state its MISO output.

SPI is a synchronous, full-duplex shift-register bus configured by four clock modes (CPOL ∈ {0,1} for idle polarity, CPHA ∈ {0,1} for sample-on-leading vs trailing edge) with no hardware flow control, ACK bit, or framing standard beyond CS assertion. Variants include Dual, Quad, and Octal SPI (repurposing MOSI/MISO and extra pins for half-duplex 2–8-bit parallel transfers in NOR flash XIP). In Linux (drivers/spi/), spi_sync() and spi_async() serialize spi_message transfers per bus controller, with DMA alignment and CS setup/hold timing constrained by trace capacitance above 25–50 MHz.
