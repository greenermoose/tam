SIGKILL (Kill signal)

SIGKILL (signal 9) is an uncatchable signal that immediately ends a process inside the kernel.

If normal quit requests are like asking a guest to leave, SIGKILL is the operating system’s bouncer escorting them straight out the door. You use it only as a last resort when a frozen program refuses to respond, because it stops the program instantly without giving it a chance to save open files.

Part of Unix signals, SIGKILL (9) cannot be caught, blocked, or ignored by the process, making kill -9 reliable for unresponsive processes. The trade-off is that cleanup handlers never run—temporary lock files, open sockets, or unsaved buffers are left behind. Always try SIGTERM (plain kill) first, and reserve SIGKILL for processes that fail to exit.

Why can a process still appear as a zombie (Z) in ps even after receiving kill -9?

Because SIGKILL terminates the process immediately, but its parent still has to call wait() to read its exit status and reap the process table entry; until then, or if the process is stuck in uninterruptible sleep (D state), it remains visible.

POSIX signal 9 (SIGKILL) bypasses userspace signal handlers: the kernel marks the thread group for immediate termination, and SIGKILL cannot appear in a signal mask or have a custom sigaction. However, signal delivery only occurs at kernel-to-userspace boundaries or when waking interruptible sleeps; a task blocked in uninterruptible kernel I/O (TASK_UNINTERRUPTIBLE, state D, such as a hung NFS mount or failing block device) cannot act on pending SIGKILL until the blocking kernel call returns. Killing a process with SIGKILL also leaves SysV IPC objects, PID/lock files, and half-written non-atomic files uncleaned, though the kernel still closes file descriptors and reclaims virtual memory.
