Symmetric encryption

Symmetric encryption scrambles and unscrambles data using the exact same secret key on both ends.

Imagine locking a diary with a padlock and giving an identical copy of that key to a trusted friend. Anyone who has the key can both lock new pages and unlock old ones, while anyone without the key sees only scrambled gibberish. The catch is that you and your friend must find a safe way to share that key beforehand without a stranger copying it.

In everyday Linux tools—such as LUKS disk encryption, WireGuard tunnels, and TLS data transfer—symmetric ciphers like AES-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305 do the heavy lifting because they are orders of magnitude faster than public-key (asymmetric) algorithms. In practice, systems pair the two: asymmetric cryptography exchanges or derives a temporary session key, and symmetric encryption protects the actual bulk data. Notice also the difference between confidentiality alone and authenticated encryption (AEAD), which also detects tampering.

Why do HTTPS and SSH use asymmetric cryptography during setup and symmetric encryption for the rest of the session?

Asymmetric cryptography lets two parties agree on a shared secret over an untrusted network without meeting first, but its math is slow. Once they have a shared key, switching to a symmetric cipher encrypts gigabytes of data at near-wire speed.

Symmetric encryption schemes are built from pseudorandom permutations (block ciphers such as AES, 128-bit blocks with 128/192/256-bit keys) or pseudorandom functions (stream ciphers such as ChaCha20), composed with a universal hash or MAC (GHASH in AES-GCM, Poly1305 in ChaCha20-Poly1305) to form an AEAD (Authenticated Encryption with Associated Data) construction providing IND-CPA and INT-CTXT security. The most common catastrophic failure mode is nonce/IV reuse under the same key: in CTR-based modes (including AES-GCM and ChaCha20), reusing a 96-bit nonce XORs two plaintexts together and, in AES-GCM, leaks the GHASH authentication key, enabling arbitrary ciphertext forgery. Hardware acceleration (AES-NI, ARMv8 Crypto) makes AES-GCM fastest on modern CPUs and eliminates timing side channels from table lookups, whereas ChaCha20-Poly1305 offers constant-time software performance without dedicated AES instructions.
