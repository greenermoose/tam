Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with S / SIKE (Supersingular Isogeny Key Encapsulation)

SIKE (Supersingular Isogeny Key Encapsulation) is a broken post-quantum key-encapsulation mechanism built on supersingular isogeny Diffie–Hellman (SIDH).

SIKE was an experimental way for two computers to agree on a shared secret key that was designed to resist future quantum computers. Researchers liked how tiny its keys were, but in 2022 mathematicians found a fast shortcut to crack it on an ordinary laptop, so it is no longer safe to use.

In NIST’s post-quantum cryptography standardization process, SIKE reached Round 4 as an alternate candidate because its public keys were much smaller than lattice schemes like ML-KEM (Kyber). However, in mid-2022, classical attacks broke the underlying SIDH problem in minutes on a single PC—demonstrating that “quantum-resistant” candidates must still survive classical cryptanalysis.

Why was SIKE eliminated even though no large quantum computer was used to break it?

The 2022 attacks used purely classical mathematics on ordinary PCs to recover secret keys in minutes, breaking SIKE’s security against today’s computers as well as quantum ones.

CCA2-secure KEM constructed via the Fujisaki–Okamoto transform over SIDH (random walks in supersingular elliptic-curve isogeny graphs over GF(p²)). Crucially, SIDH published auxiliary torsion-point images alongside the public curve so both parties could complete the non-commutative diagram. In 2022, Castryck–Decru, Maino–Martindale, and Robert exploited those auxiliary points via Kani’s theorem and higher-dimensional (2,2)-isogenies between products of elliptic curves to recover the secret isogeny in polynomial time classically. Isogeny schemes without exposed torsion-point images (such as CSIDH or SQIsign) are not broken by this attack.
