SMD (Surface-Mount Device)

SMD (Surface-Mount Device) refers to an electronic component designed to be soldered flat onto copper pads on a circuit board’s surface rather than through drilled holes.

If older electronic parts are like pegs pushed through holes in a pegboard, SMDs are like tiny flat tiles glued directly onto the top of the board. Because they are small and have no long wire legs, modern phones and laptops can pack thousands of parts onto both sides of a compact board.

Most components on modern PCBs—tiny rectangular resistors and capacitors, voltage regulators, and SOIC-8 or QFN chips on USB-UART adapters—are SMDs, built using Surface-Mount Technology (SMT). Compared to through-hole parts, SMDs allow automated pick-and-place assembly and shorter signal paths, though tiny packages without exposed leads (like QFN or BGA) are harder to hand-solder or probe with a multimeter.

Why are high-speed digital circuits almost always built with SMDs instead of through-hole components?

SMDs eliminate long wire leads and large drilled pads, reducing parasitic inductance and capacitance while allowing much denser component placement on both sides of the PCB.

Components packaged for Surface-Mount Technology (SMT): passive imperial/metric chip sizes (0201, 0402, 0603, 0805) and active IC packages (SOIC, TSSOP, QFP, QFN, BGA) soldered to land-pattern footprints via solder paste stencil printing and reflow ovens (or hot-air rework). Key failure modes and design constraints include tombstoning during reflow from asymmetric thermal relief, solder bridging on fine-pitch leads (0.4–0.5 mm), and mechanical or thermal fatigue on leadless BGA/QFN joints—whereas high-stress connectors (USB, barrel jacks) often retain through-hole mechanical anchor tabs.
