SHA-3

SHA-3 is a family of cryptographic hash functions standardized by NIST using a sponge design that is completely different from SHA-2.

After older hash functions like SHA-1 showed weaknesses, cryptographers wanted a backup standard built on a totally different mathematical idea. If a surprise flaw were ever discovered in SHA-2, SHA-3 provides an independent alternative. It absorbs data like a sponge soaking up water and then squeezes out a fixed-size fingerprint, giving software and hardware systems a diverse second option for verifying data.

Part of SHA algorithms, SHA-3 (standardized from the Keccak competition winner) sits alongside SHA-2 rather than replacing it—SHA-2 remains secure and is still more common in TLS and Linux package tooling. SHA-3 includes fixed-length hashes (SHA3-256, SHA3-512) and extendable-output functions called SHAKE128 and SHAKE256, which can emit digests of any requested length. Because of its sponge design, SHA-3 is naturally immune to the length-extension attacks that affect unkeyed SHA-256.

Why did NIST standardize SHA-3 even though SHA-2 had not been broken?

SHA-3 uses the Keccak sponge construction instead of SHA-2’s Merkle–Damgård design, providing algorithmic diversity so a breakthrough against SHA-2 would not leave systems without a drop-in cryptographic hash.

Standardized in NIST FIPS 202 (2015), SHA-3 uses the Keccak-f[1600] 24-round permutation over a 1600-bit state arranged as a 5×5×64 bit array (theta, rho, pi, chi, iota steps). Data is XORed into the outer r-bit rate portion during the absorbing phase while an inner c-bit capacity remains untouched; for SHA3-n, capacity is c = 2n, bounding generic collision and preimage security to the indifferentiability bound min(2^(c/2), 2^n). Domain-separation suffixes (01 for SHA3-*, 1111 for SHAKE*) prevent cross-mode collisions. Hardware implementations of Keccak are notably compact and energy-efficient, though software throughput without dedicated instructions lags behind SHA-NI-accelerated SHA-256 and BLAKE3.
