SHA-384

SHA-384 is a 384-bit cryptographic hash function in the SHA-2 family, built by running SHA-512 with distinct initial values and truncating the result.

SHA-384 is a stronger, 384-bit cousin of SHA-256. You will often see it in secure web connections and government or enterprise security profiles that require extra safety margin against future attackers, without needing the full 512-bit output size.

Part of SHA algorithms, SHA-384 uses the 64-bit internal engine of SHA-512 and discards the final 128 bits of the digest. On 64-bit Linux systems, sha384sum and TLS cipher suites using SHA-384 often run faster than SHA-256 because 64-bit CPUs process 64-bit words natively. It is commonly paired with P-384 elliptic-curve certificates in TLS 1.3 and CNSA-aligned configurations to match 192-bit security levels.

Why does truncating SHA-512 to 384 bits not reduce security below what SHA-384 promises?

The internal state remains 512 bits wide, and discarding 128 bits still leaves 384 bits of output—providing 192-bit collision resistance while hiding part of the state, which also blocks length-extension attacks.

Defined in FIPS 180-4, SHA-384 executes the 80-round, 1024-bit-block SHA-512 compression function over eight 64-bit state words initialized with distinct IVs (derived from the fractional parts of the square roots of the ninth through sixteenth primes) so its digests are domain-separated from SHA-512, then outputs only the first six words (384 bits). Retaining 128 unexposed internal state bits defeats length-extension attacks on unkeyed SHA-384(k || m), and its 192-bit collision / 384-bit preimage resistance aligns with AES-192/256 and P-384 suites.
