SONOS (Silicon-Oxide-Nitride-Oxide-Silicon)

SONOS is a non-volatile memory cell structure that stores electric charge in a nitride layer between oxide layers, used in some EEPROM and flash devices.

Instead of storing charge in one tiny floating bucket, a SONOS cell traps charge in a special layer inside the chip, like dust caught in a filter. Because the charge stays trapped after power is removed, the cell can remember whether it holds a 0 or 1.

SONOS stands for the layers in the cell: Silicon, Oxide, Nitride, Oxide, Silicon. During programming or erasing, electrons tunnel through a thin oxide and become trapped in the nitride layer. This trapped charge changes how easily the transistor turns on, which is how the device reads stored data. SONOS and related charge-trap designs can offer good retention and endurance characteristics, and they are used in some embedded non-volatile memories.

How is SONOS different from a floating-gate cell?

A floating-gate cell stores charge on a conductive floating gate, while SONOS traps charge in a nitride layer. Trapping charge in discrete sites can make the cell less vulnerable to losing all stored charge through a single defect in the tunnel oxide.

SONOS charge-trap cells store charge in localized states within a silicon nitride layer, with tunnel and blocking oxides controlling charge injection and retention. Program/erase can use Fowler–Nordheim tunneling or hot-carrier mechanisms depending on device design. Compared with continuous floating-gate storage, charge trapping can improve tolerance to localized tunnel-oxide defects, but retention and endurance still depend on oxide quality, electric-field stress, temperature, and disturb behavior. SONOS derivatives (such as TANOS/BE-SONOS) are used in some NOR/NAND and embedded NVM technologies.
