SNR - Signal-to-Noise Ratio

A measure comparing the strength of a desired signal to the level of background noise in a communication channel or recording.

Imagine trying to hear a friend speak in a quiet library versus a crowded, loud café. Your friend’s voice is the signal, and the background chatter is the noise. A high SNR means the voice stands out clearly above the background; a low SNR means the noise drowns it out and words get misheard.

SNR is usually expressed in decibels (dB) because signal-to-noise power ratios span huge ranges: 10 * log10(P_signal / P_noise). Every +3 dB doubles the signal-to-noise power ratio, and +10 dB multiplies it by 10. On Linux, you can see SNR in wireless diagnostics (iw dev wlan0 link, comparing signal dBm against noise floor dBm) or audio interfaces, where low SNR causes Wi-Fi rate drops, packet retries, or audible hiss.

If a Wi-Fi link has a signal of -60 dBm and a noise floor of -90 dBm, what is the SNR and why does it matter?

The SNR is 30 dB (-60 minus -90). A 30 dB SNR is strong enough for high-order modulation (like 64-QAM or 256-QAM) and fast throughput, whereas an SNR below roughly 10 dB forces slow, heavily error-corrected rates or drops.

Formally, SNR = P_signal / P_noise (or 10 log10(P_signal / P_noise) dB) at a defined bandwidth and reference point; in digital links it relates directly to Eb/N0 (energy per bit to noise power spectral density) and Shannon–Hartley channel capacity C = B log2(1 + SNR). Real receivers are bounded by thermal noise (-174 dBm/Hz at 290 K plus noise figure and 10 log10(B)), adjacent-channel interference (SINR), and ADC quantization noise (~6.02N + 1.76 dB for an ideal N-bit converter).
