Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with W / WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines)

WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) is a set of W3C standards for making digital text, controls and interfaces readable and usable by everyone, including people with disabilities.

Just as curb cuts and clear signs help people navigate a city, WCAG gives software and web designers rules such as keeping text distinct from its background and making buttons work from a keyboard. People with low vision, color blindness or motor differences benefit directly.

For typography, the most cited rules are contrast: at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text at level AA, with 7:1 for normal text at AAA. Large text is commonly 18 point or 14 point bold. WCAG also asks that text be resizable to 200% without loss of content, that line height be adjustable to at least 1.5 times the font size, and that focus indicators remain visible.

Why does WCAG require higher contrast for normal text than for large headings?

Thin strokes in small text are harder to resolve with reduced acuity, so small text needs a stronger luminance difference.

WCAG 2.1 and 2.2 organize testable success criteria under four principles—Perceivable, Operable, Understandable and Robust—with levels A, AA and AAA. Contrast uses sRGB relative luminance L = 0.2126R + 0.7152G + 0.0722B after gamma linearization, then (L1 + 0.05)/(L2 + 0.05). The 2.x formula treats light-on-dark and dark-on-light symmetrically and ignores stroke weight and spatial frequency, limitations that perceptual models attempt to address.
