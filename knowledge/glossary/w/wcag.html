WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines)

WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) is a set of W3C standards for making digital text, controls, and interfaces readable and usable for everyone, including people with disabilities.

Just as curb cuts and clear street signs help people navigate a city, WCAG gives software and web designers practical rules—such as keeping text sharp against its background and making buttons work from a keyboard—so people with low vision, color blindness, or motor differences can use interfaces comfortably.

When configuring Linux terminal themes, desktop palettes, or web pages, WCAG is most often referenced for contrast ratios: at least 4.5:1 for normal text at level AA, 7:1 at level AAA, and 3:1 for large text or UI icons. Meeting AA keeps text legible in bright rooms and on varied displays without forcing harsh black-and-white-only themes.

Why does WCAG require a higher contrast ratio (4.5:1) for normal body text than for large headings or UI icons (3:1)?

Thin strokes in small text are harder to resolve with reduced visual acuity, so normal text needs a stronger luminance difference between foreground and background to remain legible.

WCAG 2.1 and 2.2 organize testable success criteria across four principles (Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust) and three conformance levels (A, AA, AAA). Contrast is computed from sRGB relative luminance L = 0.2126 * R + 0.7152 * G + 0.0722 * B (after piecewise gamma linearization) using (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05). While widely adopted in accessibility audits and theme linters, WCAG 2.x contrast math treats light-on-dark and dark-on-light symmetrically and ignores spatial font weight—known edge cases that perceptual models like APCA aim to address in WCAG 3 drafts.
