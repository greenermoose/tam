Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with W / Wi-Fi

Wi-Fi is the standard wireless networking technology that connects laptops, phones, and other devices to a local network and the internet using radio waves.

Wi-Fi replaces the network cable between your computer and router with two-way radio signals. Just like people in a busy room take turns speaking so they do not talk over each other, nearby Wi-Fi devices share radio channels to send and receive data.

Wi-Fi implements the IEEE 802.11 family across bands such as 2.4 GHz (longer range, more interference), 5 GHz (higher speeds, shorter range), and 6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E/7). On Linux, utilities like iw and nmcli show which band, channel width, signal strength, and WPA2/WPA3 security mode a connection uses.

Why does a 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi signal often reach farther through walls than 5 GHz, even though 5 GHz is usually faster?

Lower-frequency 2.4 GHz radio waves attenuate less through walls, while 5 GHz offers wider, less crowded channels that carry more data per second at shorter range.

Wi-Fi comprises IEEE 802.11 PHY and MAC specifications: CSMA/CA medium access with DCF/EDCA contention and optional RTS/CTS for hidden-node mitigation, OFDM/OFDMA modulation with MU-MIMO and high-order QAM (up to 4096-QAM in 802.11be), and management/control frames handling beacon discovery, association, roaming, and WPA2/WPA3-SAE 4-way key handshakes. In the Linux kernel, cfg80211 and mac80211 separate regulatory/configuration interfaces and softMAC frame scheduling from userspace supplicants (wpa_supplicant, iwd).
