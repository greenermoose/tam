WEP (Wired Equivalent Privacy)

WEP (Wired Equivalent Privacy) is an obsolete, cryptographically broken 1997 Wi-Fi security protocol that was replaced by WPA, WPA2, and WPA3.

WEP was the earliest lock put on home and office Wi-Fi networks in the late 1990s so wireless traffic would not be as easy to eavesdrop on as open radio. Because of fundamental design flaws, nearby attackers can crack a WEP password in minutes, so modern routers and devices avoid it completely.

On modern Linux systems (using NetworkManager, wpa_supplicant, or iwd), WEP is disabled or deprecated because it offers no real confidentiality. Unlike WPA2 and WPA3, which negotiate fresh per-session keys and strong integrity checks, WEP relies on a shared static key; if you ever encounter a WEP access point today, treat it as effectively unencrypted.

Why is upgrading from WEP to WPA2 or WPA3 essential even on a small home network?

WEP keys can be recovered passively in minutes just by capturing normal network traffic, whereas WPA2/WPA3 derive unique session keys per device and protect packet integrity.

Defined in IEEE 802.11-1997, WEP concatenates a 24-bit initialization vector (IV) with a static 40-bit or 104-bit root key to seed the RC4 stream cipher, and appends a linear CRC-32 Integrity Check Value (ICV) before XORing with the keystream. Because a 24-bit IV space collides quickly (birthday bound around 5,000 frames) and RC4 leaks key bytes from weak IVs, Fluhrer–Mantin–Shamir (FMS) and PTW attacks recover the root key from tens of thousands of captured frames, while CRC-32 linearity enables unauthenticated bit-flipping packet injection.
