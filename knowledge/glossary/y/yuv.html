YUV (luma and chroma)

A family of color representations that separate image brightness (luma) from color information (chroma).

Human eyes usually notice fine light-and-shadow detail more readily than equally fine color detail. YUV separates brightness from color information, letting some video formats store less color detail. See light and the living eye for how we see color.

Digital video tools such as FFmpeg often use YCbCr, which separates luma from two chroma components. In 4:2:0 chroma subsampling, a pair of color-difference samples ordinarily corresponds to each two-by-two group of luma samples. The yuv420p pixel format stores 8-bit samples in separate planes.

For an even-sized unpadded image, 8-bit 4:2:0 uses 12 sample bits per pixel, half the 24 bits in 8-bit RGB before compression. Actual compressed file sizes also depend on the encoder and image. A color-range mismatch can make blacks look gray or hide shadow detail.

Why do many H.264 and AV1 videos use 4:2:0?

It reduces raw color sample data while preserving the full luma sample grid. Those video codecs and the pixel format are separate choices.

Traditional YUV is associated with analog PAL signals; digital video commonly uses Y′CbCr. Y′ is nonlinear luma, distinct from linear-light luminance. Gamma, color matrices, quantization and range are distinct properties. BT.601, BT.709 and BT.2020 specify television parameters associated respectively with SD, HD and UHD; BT.2020 alone does not imply HDR.

Memory layouts differ: I420 has three planes; NV12 is semi-planar; and YUYV is packed 4:2:2. Hardware decoding on a GPU can use interfaces such as VA-API or V4L2, depending on the system. Incorrect metadata or conversion can change hue or clip values.
