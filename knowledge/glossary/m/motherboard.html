Motherboard

The main printed circuit board in a computer that connects the processor, memory, storage, and expansion hardware together.

If the processor is the brain of a computer, the motherboard is its nervous system and skeleton. It is the large flat board inside the case where the CPU, memory sticks, storage drives, and power cables all plug in so they can share power and talk to one another.

A motherboard provides the CPU socket, DIMM memory slots, PCIe expansion slots, M.2 or SATA storage connectors, a chipset (or Platform Controller Hub) for peripheral I/O, and the non-volatile flash chip that holds the UEFI/BIOS firmware. When troubleshooting hardware or checking Linux compatibility, the motherboard determines which CPUs and RAM speeds are supported, how many PCIe lanes reach each slot, and which onboard network, audio, and USB controllers appear in lspci or lsusb.

Why can two computers with the same CPU model have different numbers of USB ports or M.2 slots?

Because peripheral controllers, M.2 wiring, and expansion slots are determined by the motherboard’s chipset and board layout rather than the CPU alone.

Modern motherboards are multi-layer PCBs engineered for high-speed differential signaling (PCIe, DDR5, USB4/DisplayPort) with impedance-controlled traces, length-matched memory channels, and a multi-phase VRM (voltage regulator module) stepping 12 V ATX rail power down to sub-1 V core rails. At boot, the board’s SPI NOR flash supplies the firmware (UEFI/coreboot) and Embedded Controller (EC) / Super I/O logic that sequence power rails, train DRAM timings, expose ACPI tables and SMBIOS/DMI metadata to the kernel, and configure IOMMU/PCIe root complex topology.
