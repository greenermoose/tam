MOSFET - Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor

A microscopic electronic switch whose ability to conduct current is controlled by voltage applied to an insulated gate.

Computers represent ones and zeros using billions of tiny electronic switches inside a processor chip. A MOSFET is the most common kind of switch: imagine a water pipe with a flexible valve you can pinch shut or open wide just by pressing on the outside without touching the water. Applying a small voltage to the control pin opens or closes the path for electricity, using almost no power just to hold its state.

A MOSFET has three main signal terminals—source, gate, and drain—plus a body (substrate) connection. Because a thin insulating oxide layer separates the gate from the semiconductor channel, steady-state gate current is nearly zero; instead, the electric field from the gate voltage attracts or repels charge carriers to form or pinch off a conducting channel between source and drain. Digital chips pair n-channel and p-channel MOSFETs in complementary (CMOS) logic so that almost all power is consumed only while switching, though MOSFETs are also widely used as high-current power switches in VRMs and motor controllers.

Why does a CMOS circuit built from MOSFETs draw very little current when it is idle?

Because the gate is electrically insulated by an oxide layer and complementary n-channel/p-channel pairs ensure no direct path exists between supply and ground when logic states are static.

In an enhancement-mode n-MOSFET, inversion occurs once gate-to-source voltage exceeds the threshold voltage (Vth), creating a conducting channel whose drain current transitions from the triode/linear region (proportional to Vds when Vds < Vgs − Vth) into saturation (governed roughly by (Vgs − Vth)² with channel-length modulation). Key failure modes and trade-offs include gate-oxide breakdown under overvoltage, switching losses from gate capacitance (Qg) and Miller plateau charge during transitions, Rds(on) conduction losses in power stages, and subthreshold leakage plus short-channel effects (DIBL) as CMOS process nodes scale down—often mitigated with high-k metal gates and FinFET or Gate-All-Around (GAA) 3D geometries.
