MITM (Man-in-the-Middle)

A Man-in-the-Middle (MITM) attack occurs when an adversary secretly sits between two communicating parties to eavesdrop on or alter their traffic.

Imagine passing notes to a friend through a messenger who opens every envelope, reads or rewrites what you wrote, and reseals it before handing it over—while both you and your friend think you are talking directly. On an untrusted network like public Wi-Fi, an attacker can play that hidden messenger unless the connection is properly verified.

Encryption alone does not stop a MITM attack if you do not verify who holds the key on the other end, because an attacker can run a fake server and encrypt separately with you and the real site. That is why browsers check TLS certificates against trusted Certificate Authorities, and why ssh warns you loudly when a server’s host key unexpectedly changes.

Why does encryption without identity verification fail to stop a MITM attack?

Without verification, an attacker can pretend to be the server to you and pretend to be you to the real server, establishing two separate encrypted connections and reading or modifying everything in between.

Active MITM attacks operate via ARP spoofing, rogue DHCP/DNS, BGP hijacking, or SSL-stripping proxies. Mitigation requires authenticated key exchange (AKE): X.509 PKI binds ephemeral ECDHE keyshares to server identities via CA signatures (hardened by Certificate Transparency and HSTS), whereas SSH relies on Trust On First Use (TOFU) or SSHFP/CA-signed host keys. Authorized corporate TLS inspection proxies operate as explicit MITMs by installing a local root CA into client trust stores.
