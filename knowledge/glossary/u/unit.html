Unit

A named resource—such as a background service, scheduled timer, or mounted filesystem—that systemd knows how to start, stop, and monitor.

When your computer starts up, dozens of background jobs need to happen in the right order: the network has to come up, disks have to be attached, and background programs need to keep running. Rather than using one giant startup script, systemd breaks all of those pieces into individual building blocks called units. Each unit is like a small instruction card saying what the piece does and what needs to be ready before it starts.

In systemd, every unit has a name and a type suffix—for example sshd.service (a daemon), home.mount (a mount point), fstrim.timer (a schedule), or multi-user.target (a synchronization milestone). You manage them with systemctl status, start, stop, and enable, and inspect their definitions with systemctl cat. Locally overridden or drop-in unit files in /etc/systemd/system/ take precedence over packaged units in /usr/lib/systemd/system/.

Why is creating a drop-in override with systemctl edit safer than editing a unit file under /usr/lib/systemd/system/ directly?

Files under /usr/lib/systemd/system/ are owned by packages and will be overwritten on the next update. A drop-in snippet in /etc/systemd/system/name.service.d/ persists across upgrades and only changes the specific directives you override.

A systemd unit is a state-machine object parsed from INI-style configuration ([Unit], type-specific sections such as [Service] or [Socket], and [Install]), backed by a dedicated cgroup v2 slice/scope when running processes. Importantly, requirement dependencies (Requires=, Wants=, BindsTo=) are orthogonal to ordering dependencies (Before=, After=): omitting ordering lets systemd activate both units in parallel. Unit generators (such as systemd-fstab-generator) synthesize transient units into /run/systemd/generator/ at boot or daemon-reload, and template units (foo@.service) instantiate parameterized instances via %i/%I specifiers.
