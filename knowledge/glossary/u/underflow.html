Underflow

Underflow happens when a result is closer to zero than the format can normally represent.

A scale that only reads whole grams shows zero for a grain of sand. Floating point can also lose a value that is too tiny, replacing it with zero or with a special tiny form.

Binary64 can represent ordinary values down to about 2.2 times 10^-308. Below that, results may become subnormal and then zero. Underflow is the opposite of overflow, which concerns values too large to hold. It usually matters in probabilities, physics simulations and products of many small numbers.

Is underflow always a bug?

No. If a value is negligible for the task, flushing it to zero is harmless. It becomes a bug when later division, logarithms or accumulation depend on the tiny value.

IEEE 754 distinguishes tininess before and after rounding and can raise an underflow flag. Gradual underflow uses subnormal values to preserve some bits; flush-to-zero modes replace them with zero for speed. Underflow also occurs in fixed-point arithmetic when a product's fractional bits are discarded, and in integer arithmetic when a result is smaller than the least representable step. Scaling computations into a friendlier range is often better than relying on extreme exponents.
