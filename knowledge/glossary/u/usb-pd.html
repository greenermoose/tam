USB-PD (USB Power Delivery)

A smart charging protocol over USB-C that lets a charger and device negotiate higher voltages and currents—up to 240 W—safely.

Old USB ports always supplied a fixed 5 volts, which was fine for phones and mice but far too slow for laptops. USB Power Delivery lets the charger and your device have a quick digital handshake as soon as you plug in the cable. They agree on the highest voltage and wattage both can handle safely—so the same charger can gently top up wireless earbuds at low power or fast-charge a workstation laptop.

Instead of assuming a fixed 5 V, USB-PD negotiates Power Data Objects (PDOs) across standard voltages (5 V, 9 V, 15 V, 20 V up to 100 W, and Extended Power Range 28 V, 36 V, or 48 V up to 240 W) as well as Programmable Power Supply (PPS) fine-grained voltage steps. Reaching above 3 A (60 W at 20 V) or entering EPR requires an electronically marked (“e-marker”) cable that reports its current and voltage rating. On Linux, negotiated PD status and power roles appear under /sys/class/power_supply/ and /sys/class/typec/.

Why does a 100 W USB-PD charger sometimes charge a laptop at only 60 W when used with a generic USB-C cable?

Without an e-marker chip in the cable confirming 5 A capability, the USB-PD protocol caps current at 3 A (60 W at 20 V) to prevent overheating the cable’s wires.

USB Power Delivery 3.0/3.1 communicates using 300 kbps Biphase Mark Coding (BMC) with 4b5b line encoding over the active CC wire (CC1 or CC2), keeping VBUS at safe 5 V until an explicit Request/Accept/PS_RDY handshake completes. Standard Power Range (SPR) covers up to 100 W (20 V / 5 A), while PD 3.1 Extended Power Range (EPR) adds 28 V, 36 V, and 48 V fixed PDOs plus Adjustable Voltage Supply (AVS) up to 240 W (48 V / 5 A), requiring EPR-rated e-marked cables and stricter keep-alive/hard-reset timing. Role swaps (PR_Swap, DR_Swap, VCONN_Swap) and Vendor Defined Messages (VDMs) for Alt Mode entry also travel over the same PD state machine.
