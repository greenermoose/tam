USB4

USB4 is a high-speed USB standard (40 to 80 Gbps) built on Thunderbolt 3 that carries USB data, display video, and PCIe over a single USB-C cable.

Earlier USB cables were mostly for data and charging, while monitors and fast external hardware often needed separate ports. USB4 acts like a multi-lane highway inside a single USB-C cable: one cable from a laptop to a dock can simultaneously charge the laptop, drive one or two crisp external monitors, and talk to fast external drives. Instead of needing a different port for each job, one port and cable handle them all together.

Unlike USB 3.2—which only sends USB packets—USB4 dynamically tunnels USB 3.2, DisplayPort, and PCIe traffic as packets over the same dual-lane link. On Linux, the kernel’s thunderbolt subsystem acts as the USB4 connection manager, exposing authorized devices and link speeds under /sys/bus/thunderbolt/devices/ and boltctl. Note that PCIe tunneling and 40 Gbps speeds are optional on some USB4 hosts (20 Gbps is the minimum), and passive 40 Gbps cables are typically limited to 0.8–1 m unless they use active electronics.

Why can a single USB4 dock run a 4K monitor, Gigabit Ethernet, and an NVMe SSD at once without dropping to USB 2.0 speeds?

USB4’s connection manager allocates link bandwidth dynamically across tunneled DisplayPort, PCIe, and USB 3.2 streams, rather than permanently splitting the cable’s physical lanes in half as older DisplayPort Alt Mode setups often do.

USB4 (v1.0 at 20/40 Gbps; v2.0 adding 80 Gbps symmetric and 120/40 Gbps asymmetric PAM3 signaling) replaces fixed physical-pin Alt Mode partitioning with a packetized fabric managed by a host-side Connection Manager (CM). Transport adapters encapsulate PCIe TLPs, DisplayPort main-link/AUX streams, and USB3 enhanced SuperSpeed packets into fixed-size transport packets routed across router hop paths with credit-based flow control. Security and interoperability edge cases include IOMMU/DMA protection (kernel iommu=pt or strict DMA remapping for untrusted PCIe tunneling, pre-boot DMA attacks), DisplayPort bandwidth reservation starving PCIe throughput when high-refresh monitors connect, and cable e-marker negotiation falling back to USB 3.2 or USB 2.0 if a 40 Gbps passive cable exceeds signal-integrity limits.
