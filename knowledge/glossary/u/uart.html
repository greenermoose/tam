UART - Universal Asynchronous Receiver-Transmitter

A hardware circuit that converts parallel bytes inside a chip into a timed, single-wire-per-direction serial stream of bits and back again.

Inside a computer, data moves around in whole bytes at a time across multiple parallel wires. A UART is a small translator circuit that takes each byte, lines its bits up single-file onto one wire to send out, and reassembles incoming single-file bits back into bytes on the other side. Because it does not need a separate clock wire, it is one of the simplest ways to connect a PC to a microcontroller or read startup logs from an embedded board.

A UART frames each byte asynchronously with a start bit, 5–8 data bits, an optional parity bit, and 1–2 stop bits (commonly written 115200 8N1 for 115,200 baud, 8 data bits, no parity, 1 stop bit). On Linux, onboard UARTs appear as /dev/ttyS* or /dev/ttyAMA* and USB-UART bridges as /dev/ttyUSB*; if both ends do not match on baud rate and framing, you get garbled characters instead of readable text.

What happens when you open a serial console at 9600 baud when the device is transmitting at 115200 baud?

The receiver samples the line at the wrong bit intervals, misreading start, data, and stop bits and displaying garbled characters or framing errors.

A UART (such as a 16550A-compatible controller) combines Tx/Rx shift registers, a programmable baud-rate divisor (typically oversampling the Rx line at 16× the baud clock to detect the falling start-bit edge and sample near bit centers), hardware FIFOs, and Line Status Register flags for overrun (OE), parity (PE), and framing (FE) errors. Because transmitter and receiver clocks are independent, cumulative clock drift across a 10-bit frame must stay within roughly ±2–3% combined tolerance. Without hardware flow control (RTS/CTS) or XON/XOFF, bursty writes at high baud rates can overflow the Rx FIFO during interrupt latency spikes.
