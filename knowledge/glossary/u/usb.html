Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with U / USB - Universal Serial Bus

USB (Universal Serial Bus) is a standard for connecting and powering external devices—such as keyboards, mice, drives, and phones—to a computer.

Think of USB as a universal wall outlet and translator rolled into one. Before USB, printers, mice, and cameras each needed their own bulky connector and often had to be plugged in before turning the computer on. With USB, one familiar family of plugs carries both electricity and data, and your computer automatically recognizes what you plugged in—so you can connect or unplug a flash drive or keyboard at any time without restarting.

On Linux, USB devices are organized in a tree starting from a host controller (root hub) down through external hubs to individual devices. Because USB defines standard device classes—such as HID for keyboards/mice, Mass Storage for flash drives, and UVC for webcams—built-in kernel drivers usually work without vendor software. You can inspect connected devices and their negotiated speed with lsusb and lsusb -t, and watch hot-plug events in dmesg or udevadm monitor. Notice that connector shape (like Type-A or Type-C) is separate from protocol speed (USB 2.0, 3.x, or USB4).

Why can a USB-C cable charge a phone quickly yet transfer files at only USB 2.0 speed?

USB-C describes the physical reversible connector, not the data wires inside the cable. Many charging cables only wire the USB 2.0 data pins and omit the high-speed SuperSpeed pairs, so power negotiation works while data tops out at 480 Mbps.

USB is a host-polled, tiered-star serial bus: devices never initiate transfers unprompted; the host controller schedules frames (1 ms in Full-Speed) or microframes (125 µs in High-Speed and SuperSpeed) and polls device endpoints. During enumeration, the host assigns a 7-bit address and reads device, configuration, interface, and endpoint descriptors to bind class or vendor drivers. Endpoints declare one of four transfer types—Control (enumeration and commands), Interrupt (bounded-latency HID polling), Bulk (error-checked storage with no latency guarantee), or Isochronous (reserved bandwidth without retries for audio/video). Common pitfalls include USB 3.x 2.4 GHz RF interference near unshielded ports, host controller xHCI endpoint limits when chaining hubs, and USB Power Delivery (PD) role/voltage mismatches causing brownouts or fallback to 5 V.
