UHBR (Ultra High Bit Rate)

DisplayPort 2.0 and 2.1 ultra-high-speed link rates (10, 13.5, and 20 Gbps per lane) that drive high-refresh 4K, 5K, and 8K displays.

Think of a monitor cable like a four-lane highway carrying picture data from your computer to your screen. As monitors get sharper and smoother (such as 4K at 240 Hz or dual 8K screens), older cable speeds run out of room. UHBR widens each lane so far more visual data fits through at once without needing extra cables or heavy picture compression.

Part of DisplayPort link rates, UHBR10, UHBR13.5, and UHBR20 operate over four lanes for raw totals of 40, 54, and 80 Gbps. Unlike older HBR rates, UHBR requires certified DP40 or DP80 cables (or full-featured USB4/Thunderbolt cables) because signal loss increases sharply at these frequencies. On Linux, negotiated link rates and lane counts appear in DRM/KMS connector properties and kernel debugfs under /sys/kernel/debug/dri/.

Why can a monitor fall back to a lower refresh rate even when both the GPU and display advertise UHBR20?

Link training tests signal integrity across the physical cable and any dock or adapter. If the cable is only rated for HBR3 or DP40, or has excessive attenuation, the DisplayPort transmitter steps down to a slower rate such as UHBR10 or HBR3, forcing DSC or a lower refresh rate.

Introduced in VESA DisplayPort 2.0/2.1, UHBR replaces 8b/10b channel coding (20% overhead) with 128b/132b coding (~3.03% overhead) plus mandatory Reed–Solomon Forward Error Correction (FEC), yielding effective payload rates of 38.69 Gbps (UHBR10), 52.22 Gbps (UHBR13.5), and 77.37 Gbps (UHBR20) across 4 lanes. Link training relies on DFE/FFE equalization and Link-Training Tunable PHY Repeaters (LTTPRs) in active cables or docks. DP 2.1 tightens DP40/DP80 cable insertion-loss budgets and aligns PHY signaling with USB4 DisplayPort Alt Mode tunneling, where UHBR13.5 is optional on some USB4 hosts that only route UHBR10 and UHBR20.
