Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with U / USB-C (USB Type-C)

A small, reversible 24-pin physical connector used across modern computers and devices for USB data, power delivery, and video output.

USB-C is the slim, rounded oval plug you can insert either way up without guessing. One port on a laptop can charge the battery, transfer files from a flash drive, and drive an external monitor—though not every cable or port supports all of those features at the same speed, so matching the cable to the job still matters.

Crucially, USB-C defines only the physical connector shape, not the protocol or speed inside: a USB-C port or cable might carry only USB 2.0 (480 Mbps) and 60 W charging, or full USB4 (40–80 Gbps), DisplayPort Alt Mode, and 240 W power. On Linux, you can inspect what a connected USB-C port and partner negotiated under /sys/class/typec/, and check whether DisplayPort Alt Mode or Thunderbolt/USB4 tunneling is active.

Why might two identical-looking USB-C cables behave differently when plugged into a 4K monitor?

A charging-only or USB 2.0 USB-C cable lacks the high-speed SuperSpeed wire pairs needed for DisplayPort Alt Mode or USB4 video tunneling. Only full-featured USB-C cables with all high-speed lanes wired (and an e-marker chip for higher speeds or wattages) can carry video.

Specified by the USB Type-C Cable and Connector Specification, the 24-pin receptacle duplicates USB 2.0 (D+/D−), VBUS, and GND symmetrically while routing four high-speed differential pairs (TX1/RX1, TX2/RX2), sideband use (SBU1/SBU2), and configuration channels (CC1/CC2). Rp/Rd pull-up/pull-down resistors on CC1/CC2 establish plug orientation, host/device role (DFP/UFP), and default current advertisement before USB-PD BMC negotiation takes over. Reconfiguring the four high-speed lanes for DisplayPort Alt Mode (2 or 4 lanes) or USB4/Thunderbolt 3 tunneling is coordinated by the port’s Type-C Port Controller (TCPC) and PHY mux, managed in Linux by the typec subsystem and UCSI/TCPM drivers.
