Journal

A journal is an ordered record of events or pending changes kept by a system so it can diagnose problems or recover cleanly.

Think of a journal like a ship’s logbook or a receipt tape before a cash register updates its total. If something goes wrong or the power suddenly cuts out, you can read the logbook to see what happened or finish the half-completed step without losing everything. It matters because computers do many things at once, and a reliable record turns mysterious crashes into solvable problems.

In Linux, the word usually refers to two different tools: the systemd journal (read with journalctl), which stores structured system and service logs, and a filesystem journal (such as in ext4 or XFS), which records metadata updates before writing them to their final disk locations. Both are append-oriented, but one helps humans debug failures while the other lets the kernel replay or discard incomplete disk operations after a crash.

Why does a journaling filesystem like ext4 recover faster after a power loss than an unjournaled one?

Because instead of scanning the entire disk with fsck to find inconsistencies, the kernel only replays or rolls back the small set of recent transactions recorded in the journal.

Both logging journals and write-ahead logs (WAL) rely on sequential, append-only writes ordered with barriers or fsync/cache-flush primitives, though their consistency guarantees differ. In ext4 (jbd2), data=ordered writes file data blocks before committing metadata to the journal to prevent stale-data exposure, whereas data=journal double-writes all data at a throughput cost. For systemd-journald, binary journal files (.journal) index structured key-value fields with optional forward-secure sealing (FSS), and rotate or vacuum independently of plain-text syslog.
