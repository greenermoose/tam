Job (shell)

A job is a unit of work—one command or a connected chain of commands—started and tracked by your shell.

Think of a shell job like a task you hand to an assistant: you can watch it run right in front of you, pause it for a moment, or let it work quietly in the background while you start something else. Keeping track of jobs matters because it lets one terminal window handle several tasks at once without losing track of which one is waiting for your keyboard.

In Bash and Zsh, a pipeline such as grep error log.txt | less counts as a single job, even though it runs multiple processes. You launch a job in the background with a trailing &, pause a foreground job with Ctrl-Z, list active jobs with jobs, and resume them with fg %1 or bg %1. Unlike system-wide process IDs (PIDs), job numbers like [1] exist only inside that specific shell session.

Why does pressing Ctrl-C stop only the foreground job instead of background jobs in the same shell?

Because the terminal driver sends SIGINT only to the foreground process group, leaving background process groups undisturbed until they try to read from the terminal or you bring them to the foreground.

POSIX job control maps each shell job to a distinct process group (pgid) within the shell’s session, using tcsetpgrp(3) to assign the controlling terminal’s foreground process group. When a background job attempts a terminal read (or write when TOSTOP is set in termios), the kernel delivers SIGTTIN or SIGTTOU, stopping the group until the shell reattaches it. Subshells and non-interactive scripts normally disable job control (set +m), and closing a login shell sends SIGHUP to remaining jobs unless detached with disown or nohup.
