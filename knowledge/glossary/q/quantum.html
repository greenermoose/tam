Quantum

A physical principle stating that certain properties in nature—such as energy and light—come in tiny discrete steps rather than smooth continuous amounts.

At everyday sizes, pouring water feels smooth and continuous. At the scale of atoms and electrons, nature behaves more like climbing stairs than walking up a ramp: electrons can only stand on specific energy steps, called quanta. Computer chips work because those fixed steps create gaps that transistors can switch across.

In solid-state electronics, quantized energy levels explain why materials act as conductors, insulators, or semiconductors: electrons occupy allowed energy bands separated by bandgaps, and jumping between bands involves absorbing or emitting a discrete packet of energy (a photon or phonon). Classical CPUs rely on these quantum rules to make transistors switch cleanly, whereas quantum computers use coherent quantum states directly for computation.

Why do semiconductors need a quantum bandgap to act as digital switches?

Without a forbidden energy gap between the valence and conduction bands, electrons would flow freely at all times like in a metal; the bandgap lets an applied voltage switch a transistor cleanly between non-conducting (off) and conducting (on) states.

Quantization arises when boundary conditions or canonical commutation relations restrict an observable’s spectrum to discrete eigenvalues—such as Bloch wavefunctions in a periodic crystal lattice forming Brillouin-zone energy bands. In nanoscale CMOS (sub-5 nm nodes), quantum tunneling through thin gate oxides and channel barriers becomes a primary leakage failure mode, whereas quantum computing exploits coherent superposition and entanglement of two-level systems constrained by decoherence and gate error rates.
