QFT (Quantum Fourier Transform)

QFT (Quantum Fourier Transform) is the quantum-computing analogue of the discrete Fourier transform, used to extract hidden periodic patterns from quantum states.

Just as a prism splits white light into a rainbow of frequencies, or an audio analyzer picks out musical notes from a sound wave, a Fourier transform reveals repeating patterns hidden inside data. QFT is the quantum version of that tool: it lets a quantum computer spot repeating cycles across huge numbers of possibilities at once, which is the trick behind Shor’s famous code-breaking algorithm.

In Shor’s algorithm, a quantum circuit first computes a periodic function into a superposition of states, and QFT then concentrates probability amplitude onto multiples of the inverse period so a measurement reveals it. Because factoring large integers and computing discrete logarithms reduce to period finding, a sufficiently large, fault-tolerant quantum computer running QFT would break RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC)—driving the transition to post-quantum cryptography.

Check: why doesn’t QFT give an exponential speedup for every problem that uses a classical Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)?

QFT transforms the quantum state’s amplitudes, not a readable list in RAM; measuring the state yields only a single sample from the distribution, so QFT only helps when the problem reduces to sampling dominant frequencies (like period or phase estimation).

Acting on an n-qubit register of dimension N = 2^n, QFT implements the unitary map |x⟩ → (1/√N) ∑_{k=0}^{N−1} exp(2πi·xk/N) |k⟩ using O(n^2) elementary gates (n Hadamard gates and n(n−1)/2 controlled phase-rotation gates R_m = diag(1, exp(2πi/2^m)), plus bit-reversal swaps), or O(n log n) when truncating rotations smaller than O(1/n)—exponentially fewer gates than the O(n·2^n) classical FFT. QFT is the core subroutine of Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE), which underpins Shor’s order-finding algorithm and HHL-style linear solvers, though physical execution on noisy hardware is constrained by circuit depth and fine-angle rotation fidelity.
