Quoting

Quoting is the way you tell a shell to treat text as one piece instead of splitting it or changing its special characters.

Think of quotes as a box you put words in. A shell normally uses spaces to decide where one word ends and the next begins. Text inside quotes stays in the box, so 'My notes.txt' names one file even though it has a space. You can also put a backslash before a single character to protect just that character.

Three forms are common. Single quotes keep every character exactly as typed, so '$name' prints the characters dollar sign and name. Double quotes keep spaces together but still let the shell expand variables and commands, so "$name" uses the value while keeping it as one field. A backslash protects the next character, as in My\ notes.txt. The choice matters because word splitting acts on unquoted results; see keeping words together for a worked example.

Why is "$name" usually the right form for a filename stored in a variable?

Double quotes let the variable expand to its value but stop word splitting and filename matching on that value, so spaces in the filename stay inside one argument.

Quoting is a token-recognition property, not a later string operation. Single quotes preserve every character literally; double quotes preserve all characters except dollar sign, backquote and backslash, which keep their special roles; a backslash preserves the next character except a newline, where it becomes line continuation. POSIX also defines dollar-single-quotes for escape sequences and here-documents as another quoting form. Quote characters are removed only in the final quote-removal step, after field splitting and pathname expansion, which is why quoted expansion results are protected from both. See POSIX Shell Command Language sections 2.2 and 2.6.7, and Bash manual.
