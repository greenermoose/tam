QWERTY

The standard Latin-script keyboard layout, named after the first six letter keys on its top alphabetic row.

Most English keyboards arrange their letters in the same familiar pattern starting with Q, W, E, R, T, and Y at the top left. Learning where keys sit on QWERTY helps because shortcuts, games, and typing guides almost always assume this layout.

On Linux, key presses start as physical hardware scancodes and are mapped by XKB into logical keysyms according to your active layout (such as us QWERTY, Dvorak, or Colemak). Tamlinux bindings use logical keysyms or keycodes so home-row navigation works cleanly on QWERTY, though switching layouts can move letter-based shortcuts unless your compositor binds physical keycodes.

Why do some keyboard shortcuts stay in the same physical spot when you switch away from QWERTY while others move?

Shortcuts bound to hardware keycodes follow physical key positions regardless of layout, whereas shortcuts bound to keysyms (like j or k) follow whichever key produces that letter in the active XKB map.

Originating from the 1870s Sholes and Glidden typewriter and standardized in ANSI/ISO 9995 variants (differing mainly in the Return key shape and the ISO 102nd key beside Left Shift), QWERTY maps Linux evdev keycodes (offset by +8 in X11/XWayland) through libxkbcommon rulesets to keysyms and modifiers. Edge cases include dead-key composition in international variants (us-intl) and hardware rollover limits on membrane matrices.
