Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with Q / QAM (Quadrature Amplitude Modulation)

QAM (Quadrature Amplitude Modulation) is a radio and signal-modulation method that packs multiple bits into every transmitted symbol by varying both the signal’s amplitude (strength) and its phase (timing shift).

If you only send messages by turning a flashlight on or off, each flash carries just one yes-or-no bit. QAM is like using both brightness levels and distinct color/timing combinations at the same time so a single flash can represent a whole group of bits—letting Wi-Fi and cable modems push much more data through the air or over a wire without needing a wider channel.

In Wi-Fi (and cellular or cable networks), QAM order is written as 16-QAM, 64-QAM, 256-QAM, 1024-QAM (Wi-Fi 6), or 4096-QAM (Wi-Fi 7). Higher QAM carries more bits per symbol—1024-QAM carries 10 bits vs. 6 bits for 64-QAM—but the distinct signal states sit much closer together, so your device automatically steps down to a lower QAM (and slower link speed) as you walk farther from the router and noise increases.

Check: why does moving behind a thick wall cause Wi-Fi to drop from 1024-QAM to 64-QAM or 16-QAM?

The wall attenuates the signal and lowers the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). With more noise relative to signal strength, the receiver can no longer reliably distinguish 1,024 tightly packed constellation points, so rate control falls back to a sparser QAM order.

Square M-QAM modulates two orthogonal carriers—in-phase (I = cos ωt) and quadrature (Q = −sin ωt)—onto a 2D lattice of M = 2^k constellation points, encoding k = log2(M) bits per symbol (commonly Gray-coded so adjacent-point errors flip only 1 bit). Doubling bits per symbol (e.g., quadrupling M) shrinks the minimum Euclidean distance d_min between points for a fixed average symbol energy, demanding roughly +6 dB additional SNR per +2 bits/symbol to maintain the same uncoded symbol error rate. Practical OFDM transceivers (802.11ax/be, DOCSIS 3.1) pair high-order QAM with LDPC forward error correction and tight EVM (Error Vector Magnitude) budgets—for instance, IEEE 802.11ax requires transceiver EVM better than −35 dB for 1024-QAM.
