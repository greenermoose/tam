Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with Q / QR code (Quick Response code)

A two-dimensional matrix barcode that stores text, links, or binary data and includes built-in error correction so scanners can still read damaged codes.

Think of a QR code as a square black-and-white pattern that a phone camera can read in an instant. Even if part of the square is smudged, folded, or covered by a small logo, the pattern carries spare backup clues so the scanner can still recover the exact link or Wi-Fi password.

On Linux desktops and terminals, tools like qrencode generate QR codes for sharing URLs, TOTP secrets, or Wi-Fi credentials, while zbarimg decodes them. Encoders offer four error-correction levels—L (7%), M (15%), Q (25%), and H (30%)—trading higher data capacity for stronger damage tolerance.

Why does choosing a higher error-correction level make a QR code denser for the same text?

Higher error-correction levels append more Reed–Solomon parity codewords alongside the payload, forcing the encoder to step up to a larger module grid (higher QR version) to fit both data and recovery bytes.

Standardized in ISO/IEC 18004, QR symbols span Version 1 (21×21 modules) to Version 40 (177×177) with finder, alignment, and timing patterns plus a format-selected XOR mask to avoid large monochrome regions. Payloads encode in numeric, alphanumeric, byte, or Kanji modes and use Reed–Solomon forward error correction over GF(2⁸) (primitive polynomial x⁸ + x⁴ + x³ + x² + 1) to correct erasures and unknown symbol errors up to half the parity budget.
