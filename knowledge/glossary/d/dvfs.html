DVFS (Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling)

A power-management technique that lowers a processor’s clock speed and supply voltage together when demand is light, and raises them under heavy load.

Driving a car gently at 30 mph in a neighborhood uses far less fuel than flooring it at 90 mph on the highway. DVFS lets a CPU or GPU cruise at a slower pace when you are only reading text—keeping your device cooler, quieter, and longer-lasting on battery—then speed right back up when you open a heavy app.

Because dynamic CMOS power scales roughly with C × V² × f, lowering voltage alongside frequency yields near-cubic power savings. On Linux, cpufreq governors (schedutil, powersave, performance) and drivers such as amd-pstate or intel_pstate coordinate P-states, which you can inspect under /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpufreq/.

Why does lowering voltage alongside frequency save much more power than lowering frequency alone?

Dynamic power scales linearly with frequency (f) but quadratically with voltage (V²), so reducing both together produces roughly cubic power savings rather than a simple linear drop.

DVFS sequences PLL/clock-divider transitions with voltage regulator module (VRM) VID steps over SVID/I2C/PMBus: frequency must step down before voltage drops, and voltage must settle before frequency rises, to prevent timing violations. Modern x86/ARM SoCs delegate fine-grained transitions to hardware autonomous loops (Intel HWP, AMD CPPC/amd-pstate active mode) informed by OS schedutil PELT utilization hints and thermal/PPT budgets. Failure modes include Vdroop undershoot on sudden load spikes, PLL relock latency stalls, and race-to-idle trade-offs where running slowly delays entry into deep C-states and increases total static leakage energy.
