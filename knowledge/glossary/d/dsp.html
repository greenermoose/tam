Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with D / DSP (Digital Signal Processor / Processing)

The mathematical processing of continuous signals in digital form—or a specialized chip built to run those operations in real time.

Imagine listening to a friend on a noisy train: your brain filters out the clatter and focuses on their voice. DSP does that same kind of instant cleanup for audio, radio, and network cables using fast math, turning noisy or distorted waves back into clear sound or reliable data.

DSP appears both as dedicated silicon (audio DSPs in laptops and phones, Wi-Fi/5G baseband chips, and SerDes DSPs in 100G+ NICs) and as software pipelines (PipeWire echo cancellation or SDR demodulation in GNU Radio). Unlike general-purpose CPUs built for branching logic, DSPs are tuned for predictable, streaming math with strict real-time deadlines.

Why do modern 100G and 400G Ethernet transceivers embed dedicated DSPs instead of relying only on simpler analog amplifiers?

At multi-gigabit PAM-4 symbol rates, copper and optical channels introduce severe inter-symbol interference, dispersion, and crosstalk that analog equalizers alone cannot invert; DSPs run adaptive digital filters to recover clean symbols at low bit-error rates.

DSP processor architectures combine modified-Harvard memory buses, circular/bit-reversed addressing, zero-overhead hardware loops, and wide SIMD or VLIW single-cycle multiply-accumulate (MAC) units to execute FIR/IIR filters, FFTs, and adaptive LMS/RLS equalizers deterministically. Key engineering trade-offs balance fixed-point vs. floating-point arithmetic (quantization noise and saturation behavior vs. silicon area and power) and pipeline group delay—especially critical in active noise cancellation or SerDes CDR/DFE feedback loops where latency directly constrains loop stability.
