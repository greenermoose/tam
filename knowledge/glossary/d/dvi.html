DVI - Digital Visual Interface

An older digital and analog video connector standard that bridged the transition from VGA to HDMI and DisplayPort.

Before HDMI became the standard plug on TVs and monitors, DVI was the wide white connector with rows of small pins that carried sharp digital picture from a PC to a flat-screen monitor—though without sound.

DVI comes in three variants: DVI-D (digital only), DVI-A (analog VGA-compatible only), and DVI-I (both digital and analog, with four extra pins around the flat blade). Single-link DVI reaches 1920×1200 at 60 Hz, while dual-link doubles the data pairs to reach 2560×1600 at 60 Hz. Passive HDMI-to-DVI adapters work because both use compatible digital signaling, whereas DVI-D cannot drive an analog VGA monitor passively.

Why does a simple passive plug adapter work between HDMI and single-link DVI-D, while a passive adapter cannot connect a DVI-D port to an analog VGA monitor?

Single-link DVI-D and HDMI both use compatible TMDS digital video signaling, so a passive adapter only remaps pins. DVI-D lacks the analog RGB pins found on DVI-I, so driving VGA requires an active digital-to-analog converter.

DVI 1.0 transmits video over Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) using DC-balanced 10-bit symbol encoding across three RGB data lanes plus one pixel clock lane (165 MHz max per link, 1.65 Gbps/lane). Dual-link adds a second trio of TMDS data pairs sharing the same clock for up to 330 MHz effective pixel clock—a frequent failure mode is "dual-link" cables missing the middle pins or passive HDMI dongles capping output at single-link rates. Display identification (EDID) runs over I2C Display Data Channel (DDC); unlike HDMI and DisplayPort, the DVI specification omits audio and InfoFrame packetization.
