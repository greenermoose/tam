Differential Signaling

Transmitting an electrical signal over a pair of wires using equal and opposite voltages.

Instead of sending a signal down a single wire and measuring it against a shared ground, differential signaling sends the signal down two twisted wires at once—one going up while the other goes down. Any outside electrical static hits both wires equally, so when the receiver subtracts one wire from the other, the static cancels out and the message comes through cleanly.

Because external electromagnetic interference couples almost identically into closely twisted conductors, the receiver’s differential subtraction (V+ - V-) rejects common-mode noise. This lets interfaces like USB, Ethernet, PCIe, and RS-485 run reliably over long cables or at multi-gigabit speeds using small voltage swings.

Why are the two wires in a differential pair twisted together?

Twisting keeps both wires in nearly the same electromagnetic environment so outside noise affects both equally and cancels out when subtracted at the receiver.

Balanced transmission over a controlled-impedance pair (typically 85–100 ohms differential) whose noise immunity is quantified by Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR). By eliminating reliance on a quiet shared ground reference, it suppresses ground-loop currents and far-field EMI; however, intra-pair length skew converts differential energy into common-mode noise (mode conversion) and closes the eye diagram at high frequencies.
