Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with D / DDH (Decisional Diffie-Hellman)

DDH (Decisional Diffie-Hellman) is a cryptographic hardness assumption stating that a Diffie-Hellman shared secret looks indistinguishable from a purely random value to any eavesdropper.

Imagine two people mixing secret paint colors with a shared base color in public, then combining what they receive to make a final secret shade. Even if an eavesdropper watches the public exchange, DDH is the mathematical guarantee that the final shared color looks completely random—not even a tiny hint or pattern leaks out.

In public-key cryptography, DDH is stronger than Computational Diffie-Hellman (CDH). CDH only says an attacker cannot compute the full shared secret; DDH says an attacker cannot even tell the real shared secret apart from random noise. Protocols like TLS and WireGuard rely on DDH-like assumptions so they can feed the shared secret through a key derivation function directly into symmetric encryption keys.

Why is CDH alone not enough when building a key exchange for encryption?

CDH only guarantees an attacker cannot compute the entire shared secret, which could still leave individual bits biased or predictable. DDH guarantees the shared secret is indistinguishable from random, making it safe to derive symmetric keys from it.

In a cyclic group G of prime order q with generator g, DDH asserts that the distributions (g, ga, gb, gab) and (g, ga, gb, gc) for uniform a, b, c in Zq are computationally indistinguishable. DDH implies CDH, which implies Discrete Log, but the converse fails in pairing-friendly (Gap-DH) groups where an efficiently computable bilinear map e(ga, gb) = e(g, gc) decides DDH in polynomial time. In Zp*, DDH also fails unless restricted to a prime-order subgroup because the Legendre symbol leaks parity; modern X25519 pairs Curve25519 with clamping and HKDF (relying on Hash-Diffie-Hellman).
