Digital-to-analog converter (DAC)

A circuit that turns a digital code into an analog voltage or current.

After sound has been stored as numbers, a player must turn those numbers back into a changing voltage to drive a speaker. The DAC does that job. It is the opposite of an analog-to-digital converter.

Many audio DACs produce a voltage for each sample and hold it until the next sample, making a staircase. An output filter smooths the staircase into a continuous waveform. The sample rate and bit depth set what the original recording could contain; the DAC cannot restore detail that was lost earlier.

Why does a DAC need an output filter?

Holding each value produces sharp steps and images of the signal at multiples of the sample rate. A filter removes those images so the output is the intended smooth waveform.

Common types include resistor-string, R-2R ladder, current-steering and delta-sigma DACs. Specifications include differential and integral non-linearity, offset and gain error, settling time, glitch energy, output impedance and total harmonic distortion plus noise. Clock jitter at the sample instant matters because a wrong time produces a wrong voltage. The reconstruction filter trades passband flatness, phase response and image rejection.
