DisplayPort link rates (RBR, HBR, HBR2, HBR3, UHBR)

DisplayPort link rates are standardized per-lane transmission speeds—from 1.62 Gbps (RBR) up to 20 Gbps (UHBR20)—that determine how much video data a DisplayPort cable can carry.

Think of DisplayPort link rates like speed limits on a four-lane highway. When you plug in a monitor, your computer and screen test the cable and agree on the fastest speed it can carry cleanly. If a cable cannot handle the top speed, the system drops to a slower rate, which is why a poor cable can limit a 4K monitor to a lower refresh rate or cause flickering.

A DisplayPort connection uses 1, 2, or 4 data lanes, so total link bandwidth equals the per-lane rate multiplied by active lanes minus encoding overhead. During link training, the GPU and display step through RBR, HBR, HBR2, HBR3, and UHBR until the link stabilizes.

Why does a 4-lane HBR3 link (8.1 Gbps raw per lane, or 32.4 Gbps total) deliver only about 25.92 Gbps of usable video data?

HBR3 uses 8b/10b line encoding, which transmits 10 bits on the wire for every 8 bits of payload data—spending 20% of raw bandwidth on clocking and DC balance.

VESA DisplayPort raw per-lane bit rates split into two channel-coding regimes: RBR (1.62 Gbps), HBR (2.7 Gbps), HBR2 (5.4 Gbps), and HBR3 (8.1 Gbps) use 8b/10b coding (80% payload efficiency), whereas UHBR 10, 13.5, and 20 Gbps (DP 2.0/2.1) adopt 128b/132b coding with Reed–Solomon FEC (~96.7% net efficiency after framing and FEC overhead). Link training negotiates voltage swing and pre-emphasis via DPCD registers over AUX, falling back in rate or lane count on bit errors; USB-C DP Alt Mode with concurrent USB3 SuperSpeed restricts the link to 2 lanes.
