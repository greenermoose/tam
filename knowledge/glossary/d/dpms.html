DPMS - Display Power Management Signaling

A VESA standard that lets a computer put a monitor into low-power standby, suspend, or off states when idle.

When you step away from your desk and your monitor turns black to save electricity—even though the computer itself is still running—DPMS is usually what made that happen. It is a simple signal from the graphics card telling the screen “no one is using the computer right now, go to sleep,” and waking it back up as soon as you move the mouse or press a key.

VESA DPMS originally worked over analog VGA by toggling horizontal and vertical sync pulses to select four power states: On, Standby, Suspend, and Off. On modern Linux desktops using DisplayPort or HDMI, the concept still exists under the DPMS name—visible in tools like xset dpms on X11 or idle-daemon settings in Wayland compositors—though the underlying mechanism uses digital connector power states rather than analog sync lines. Note that DPMS only turns off the display; the CPU, disks, and network stay awake unless system suspend is also configured.

If DPMS turns your monitor off after 10 minutes of idle time, will background downloads stop?

No. DPMS only puts the display into a low-power state; the rest of the computer keeps running normally unless a separate system sleep or suspend timer triggers.

Defined by the VESA DPMS standard (1993) via HSYNC/VSYNC combinations, DPMS is exposed in Linux DRM/KMS through the connector’s DPMS property (DRM_MODE_DPMS_ON, STANDBY, SUSPEND, OFF). Under atomic modesetting, compositors typically transition a CRTC/connector’s ACTIVE property to 0 rather than calling legacy drmModeConnectorSetProperty DPMS ioctls, which disables the scanout pipeline, tears down the DisplayPort/HDMI link (or enters Panel Self Refresh / D3 power states), and lets the panel enter deep sleep. Wake-up latency depends on link retraining and panel backlight ramp-up.
