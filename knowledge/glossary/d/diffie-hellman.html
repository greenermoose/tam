Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with D / Diffie-Hellman

A cryptographic method that lets two parties agree on a shared secret over a public channel.

Imagine two people who want to agree on a secret paint color while everyone in the room is watching. They start with a shared public color, each mixes in their own secret color, and they swap the mixtures openly. When each mixes their own secret color into the other person’s blend, both arrive at the exact same final shade—even though onlookers cannot unmix the swapped paints to find it.

In practice, Diffie-Hellman (most often elliptic-curve ECDH such as X25519 in SSH, WireGuard, and TLS) lets a client and server derive a shared symmetric key without ever sending that key across the network. On its own, plain Diffie-Hellman does not prove who you are talking to, so real protocols pair it with digital signatures or certificates to prevent man-in-the-middle attacks.

Why must Diffie-Hellman be combined with authentication (such as certificates) on the internet?

Without authentication, an active attacker can run separate Diffie-Hellman exchanges with both sides and read or alter everything in the middle.

A non-interactive key-agreement protocol in a cyclic group G of prime order q (a multiplicative subgroup of integers mod p or an elliptic-curve group), where security rests on the Computational and Decisional Diffie-Hellman assumptions (CDH/DDH) and discrete-logarithm hardness. Deployments must validate peer public keys against small-subgroup or invalid-curve points (or use Montgomery curves like X25519) and bind transcripts to identities to defeat active MitM.
