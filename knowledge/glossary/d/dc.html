DC - Direct Current

Electric current that flows steadily in one direction with constant polarity.

Think of a river flowing steadily downstream in one direction, rather than ocean waves sloshing back and forth. DC (direct current) is electricity that flows one way—like the power from a battery, a USB phone charger, or a laptop power brick.

Wall outlets deliver AC (alternating current, which reverses direction 50 or 60 times per second), whereas computer chips, SSDs, and LEDs require steady DC at tightly regulated low voltages (such as 12 V, 5 V, 3.3 V, and roughly 1 V for CPU cores). A PC power supply unit (PSU) or laptop adapter converts wall AC into DC, and motherboard regulators step it down further.

Why does a desktop PC need a power supply unit (PSU) if wall outlets already provide electricity?

Wall outlets supply high-voltage AC (120 V or 230 V reversing direction continuously), which would destroy computer chips. The PSU converts that AC into low-voltage, regulated DC rails (such as +12 V, +5 V, and +3.3 V) that digital circuits can safely use.

Unidirectional electric current with zero nominal frequency, characterized by DC voltage/current level, load regulation, and residual AC ripple/noise. In computer power delivery, front-end AC-DC rectification with power-factor correction feeds an intermediate DC bus (12 V ATX or 5–48 V USB-C PD), which multi-phase synchronous buck voltage regulator modules (VRMs) step down to sub-volt processor rails capable of hundreds of amps with microsecond transient response. Engineering trade-offs: unlike AC, DC cannot be stepped up or down with passive transformers and relies on switching regulators (introducing switching ripple and EMI), yet DC avoids skin-effect AC resistance and reactive power losses, making high-voltage DC buses attractive in data centers.
