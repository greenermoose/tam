Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with D / DB-9 (D-subminiature 9-pin connector)

A 9-pin D-shaped connector commonly used for serial RS-232 connections.

A DB-9 connector has nine metal pins arranged in a D-shaped shell. You often see it on older computers, networking equipment, industrial gear, and serial console cables. The D shape helps align the plug, and screws on the sides can hold it firmly in place.

Although people often say "DB-9", the 9-pin serial connector is formally a DE-9 in the D-subminiature family. In PC serial ports, it commonly carries RS-232 signals such as TX, RX, RTS, CTS, DTR, DSR, DCD, GND, and RI. Not every DB-9 carries RS-232; the same connector shape has been used for other interfaces, so you should check the device documentation before connecting equipment.

Why can a loose DB-9 cable cause strange intermittent problems?

Serial links depend on stable electrical contact. If the connector backs out slightly, pins can make and break contact, causing corrupted characters, framing errors, or dropped connections.

The DB-9 name persists colloquially, but the E-size shell designation (DE-9) is the precise D-sub form factor. RS-232 on DE-9 uses single-ended voltage signaling with defined pin assignments (TIA/EIA-232), unlike differential serial standards such as RS-485. Practical failure modes include ground/reference issues, incorrect null-modem assumptions, handshaking mismatch (RTS/CTS or DTR/DSR), and connector retention. For permanent equipment, jack screws prevent vibration-induced intermittent faults that can look like software or baud-rate problems.
