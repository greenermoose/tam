Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with D / DisplayPort

DisplayPort is an open, royalty-free digital interface standard used to transmit high-resolution video, audio, and control signals from computers to displays.

You can spot a standard DisplayPort plug by its rectangular shape with one angled corner, common on desktop graphics cards, docking stations, and monitors. It clicks securely into place, supports high refresh rates for smooth motion, and can also run through a USB-C port or link multiple monitors from a single cable.

Created by VESA, DisplayPort breaks video and audio into data packets—more like Ethernet or PCIe than older continuous pixel-clock standards such as VGA or DVI. That packet design enables Multi-Stream Transport (MST) daisy-chaining of several independent monitors on one port, which shows up as separate DRM connectors on Linux.

Why can a single DisplayPort output drive multiple independent monitors via daisy-chaining (MST), while standard HDMI cannot?

DisplayPort packetizes video streams and multiplexes virtual channels over the same physical lanes, whereas standard HDMI carries a single continuous video stream per cable.

A micro-packet protocol pairing 1 to 4 unidirectional AC-coupled Main Link differential lanes (8b/10b up to HBR3, 128b/132b in UHBR) with a bidirectional half-duplex AUX channel (1 Mbps Manchester-II, or higher in FAUX modes) for EDID/DDC polling, DPCD register access, and link training. Key features include MST virtual-channel allocation, VESA Display Stream Compression (DSC 1.2, visually lossless ~3:1), Adaptive-Sync (VRR), and USB-C Alternate Mode (pin assignments C/D/E muxing 2 or 4 high-speed pairs).
