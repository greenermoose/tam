DLP (Discrete Logarithm Problem)

DLP (Discrete Logarithm Problem) is the mathematical problem of finding the exponent x given g^x mod p, whose difficulty underpins many classical public-key cryptosystems.

Mixing two paint colors is quick and easy, but looking at the finished bucket and figuring out the exact drops that made it is painfully slow. The Discrete Logarithm Problem works like a one-way math puzzle: multiplying powers forward is fast, while reversing the steps to recover the secret exponent takes far too long for ordinary computers—though future quantum computers could solve it quickly.

Classical Diffie–Hellman key exchange, DSA, and ElGamal—as well as their elliptic-curve counterparts ECDH and ECDSA—rely on DLP being hard to invert. Because a large quantum computer running Shor’s algorithm can solve DLP efficiently, modern TLS and SSH implementations are adding post-quantum algorithms such as ML-KEM.

Why does switching from classical Diffie–Hellman to Elliptic-Curve Diffie–Hellman (ECDH) shrink key sizes, yet still leave connections vulnerable to a large quantum computer?

Elliptic-curve groups resist classical subexponential attacks, allowing smaller 256-bit keys, but Shor’s quantum period-finding algorithm breaks both finite-field DLP and ECDLP in polynomial time.

Given a generator g and element h = g^x in a finite cyclic group G, recover x. In prime-field multiplicative groups (𝔽_p^*), the Number Field Sieve solves DLP in subexponential time L_p[1/3], requiring 2048–3072-bit moduli and exposing shared 1024-bit primes to precomputation attacks (Logjam). In generic elliptic-curve groups E(𝔽_q), the best classical algorithms (Pollard’s rho) run in O(√n) time, permitting 256-bit keys. Shor’s quantum algorithm solves both variants in polynomial time O((log n)^3), driving hybrid post-quantum key-encapsulation deployments.
