Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with D / DPI (Dots Per Inch)

Dots per inch, a measurement of spatial density used for printer output, mouse sensitivity and, informally, display sharpness.

DPI counts how many tiny dots fit along a one-inch line. On a printer, higher DPI means sharper text and smoother pictures because ink marks are smaller and closer together. On a mouse it means the pointer travels farther for the same hand movement.

The word is overloaded: printer ink density, mouse sensor counts per inch (often called CPI), and interface scaling. For screens the accurate unit is PPI, because pixels are picture elements, not ink dots; desktop toolkits and display servers still say 'DPI' for scaling, with 96 DPI as the traditional 1× baseline. Font sizes in points are converted to pixels using DPI.

Why is a 1200 DPI printer not the same thing as a 1200 PPI monitor?

Printers use many ink dots, often with halftoning, to approximate one tone; a monitor's pixels directly emit full color, so the two densities are not comparable.

Strictly, DPI measures marks placed on a medium, PPI is the panel's pixel density, and CPI is sensor counts per inch of travel. Display servers historically reported physical dimensions from EDID to compute logical DPI, often clamped to 96 to avoid broken layouts; newer compositors decouple logical coordinates from physical pixels with integer buffer scales and fractional-scale protocols. Font rasterizers use the effective DPI to choose pixels per em.
