Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with D / DPI - Dots Per Inch

Dots per inch, a measurement of spatial density used for printer output, mouse sensor sensitivity, and—informally—display sharpness.

DPI counts how many tiny dots fit along a one-inch line. On a printer, higher DPI means sharper text and smoother photos because the ink droplets are smaller and packed closer together. On a computer mouse, higher DPI means the pointer moves farther across your screen for the same physical hand movement.

In computing, DPI is overloaded across three contexts: printer ink density, mouse sensor counts per inch (often called CPI by hardware makers), and screen scaling. For monitors, the accurate term is PPI (pixels per inch), because pixels are square picture elements rather than printer ink dots; however, Linux toolkits, X11 (Xft.dpi), and Wayland fractional scaling still use “DPI” as a shorthand for interface scaling (where 96 DPI is the traditional 1× baseline).

Why is a 1200 DPI printer not the same thing as a 1200 PPI monitor?

Printers use multiple tiny ink dots (halftoning) to approximate a single color tone, so printer DPI is much higher than the effective image resolution; a monitor’s pixels each emit full red, green, and blue directly, measured in PPI.

Strictly, DPI measures physical halftone/ink placement on media, whereas PPI equals sqrt(w_px^2 + h_px^2) / diag_in for a display panel and CPI measures optical sensor counts per inch of surface travel. In Linux graphics stacks, X11 historically reported physical dimensions from EDID to compute logical DPI, often clamped to 96 DPI by toolkits to avoid broken layouts, while Wayland compositors decouple logical coordinate spaces from physical pixel grids via integer buffer scales and the wp_fractional_scale_v1 protocol (expressing scale in 1/120ths).
