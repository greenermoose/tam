Daemon

A background program on Unix-like systems that runs continuously without an interactive terminal.

Think of the utility workers who keep electricity, water, and mail running quietly behind the scenes while you go about your day. A daemon works the same way on your computer: it runs quietly in the background without opening a window or waiting for you to type commands, handling ongoing jobs like printing, networking, or system logging.

By Unix convention, many daemon names end in d—such as sshd (SSH server), cupsd (printing), and systemd-journald (logging). Unlike a foreground command that exits when finished or stops if you close its terminal, a daemon detaches from any terminal and is typically started, monitored, and restarted by an init system such as systemd (systemctl status sshd).

Why do daemons detach from a controlling terminal (or run under an init supervisor)?

A normal terminal program receives a hangup signal (SIGHUP) and exits when you log out or close the terminal window. Detaching from the terminal—or running under a service supervisor—ensures the background service stays alive across user logins and logouts.

Traditionally, a Unix process daemonized via double-fork()—the first child calls setsid() to create a new session and shed its controlling TTY, and the second fork() exits the session leader so the grandchild cannot reacquire a controlling terminal on open()—followed by chdir("/"), umask(0), and redirecting FDs 0/1/2 to /dev/null. Modern supervisors (systemd, runit, s6) prefer foreground execution (Type=simple or Type=notify via sd_notify()), which avoids PID-file races, tracks all child processes reliably via cgroups, captures stdout/stderr directly into logs, and enables on-demand socket activation (LISTEN_FDS).
