Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with D / DWDM (Dense Wavelength-Division Multiplexing)

A fiber-optic transmission technique that sends dozens of closely spaced laser wavelengths down a single strand of glass fiber at the same time.

Think of a single optical fiber as a multi-lane highway where each lane is a slightly different color of laser light. Because those colors do not mix or interfere inside the glass, one thin strand can carry dozens of separate high-speed internet streams at once.

Unlike Coarse WDM (CWDM), which spaces channels 20 nm apart for short metro links, DWDM packs channels tightly inside the optical C-band (around 1530–1565 nm) where optical amplifiers can boost every channel at once. Each wavelength carries an independent 10G, 100G, or 400G+ data stream over long-haul single-mode fiber.

Why do long-haul backbone and subsea links use DWDM in the C-band instead of CWDM?

Erbium-doped fiber amplifiers (EDFAs) boost light directly across the C-band without converting signals back to electricity, and DWDM’s tight spacing fits 40–96+ high-capacity channels inside that single amplifier window.

Standardized by ITU-T G.694.1 on 100 GHz (~0.8 nm) or 50 GHz (~0.4 nm) grids—plus 12.5 GHz flex-grid slices for superchannels—DWDM operates over single-mode fiber (SMF). Coherent transceivers (such as 400ZR) pair dual-polarization QAM with high-speed DSPs to compensate chromatic and polarization-mode dispersion electronically. Optical nonlinearities—Four-Wave Mixing (FWM), Cross-Phase Modulation (XPM), and Stimulated Raman Scattering—limit per-channel launch power and minimum spacing, especially on low-dispersion fibers.
