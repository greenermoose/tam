Diode

A diode is a two-terminal electronic component that allows electric current to flow easily in one direction while blocking it in the other.

Think of a diode as a one-way check valve for electricity: current pushes through in the forward direction, while reverse flow is blocked. Inside computers and chargers, diodes turn alternating wall power (AC) into steady direct current (DC) and protect USB or display ports from static electricity shocks.

Built around a semiconductor P–N junction, a standard silicon diode conducts in forward bias once voltage exceeds roughly 0.6–0.7 V, with a stripe marking the cathode (negative) end. Different designs specialize the behavior: Schottky diodes drop less voltage (~0.2–0.3 V) and switch faster, Zener diodes hold a steady voltage in reverse breakdown, TVS diodes clamp sudden static spikes, and LEDs emit light when current flows.

Why is a Schottky diode often preferred over a standard silicon P–N diode in a low-voltage power rail or switching regulator?

Its lower forward voltage drop (~0.2–0.3 V vs. ~0.7 V) wastes less power as heat, and its fast switching reduces reverse-recovery losses at high frequencies.

A non-linear two-terminal semiconductor element whose asymmetric I–V curve follows the Shockley diode equation. In P–N junctions, minority-carrier storage introduces a reverse-recovery time (t_rr) that limits high-frequency switching, whereas majority-carrier metal–semiconductor Schottky barriers eliminate stored-charge recovery at the cost of higher reverse leakage. In hardware engineering, diodes serve in bridge rectifiers, inductive flyback clamping (dissipating L·dI/dt transients), and ultra-low-capacitance TVS arrays protecting multi-gigabit differential pairs (USB4, PCIe, DisplayPort) against ESD without attenuating high-frequency edges.
