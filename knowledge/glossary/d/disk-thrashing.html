Disk thrashing

Disk thrashing occurs when a computer runs short on RAM and spends most of its time moving memory pages back and forth to storage instead of doing useful work.

Imagine cooking a meal on a tiny counter where you constantly have to carry ingredients back and forth to a distant pantry for every step. You stay busy and exhausted, yet dinner barely progresses. Disk thrashing feels the same on a computer: the drive light stays busy, fans spin up, and the screen freezes for seconds at a time even though nothing has crashed.

Thrashing happens when running programs actively need more memory (their working set) than physical RAM can hold, forcing the kernel to evict pages to swap and immediately read them back on the next access. On Linux, it shows up in vmstat as sustained swap-in/swap-out activity (si/so) and high I/O wait (wa), unlike normal swapping where idle background pages are moved to disk once and left alone.

Why can a system become nearly unusable during disk thrashing even when the CPU is mostly idle?

Tasks spend most of their time blocked waiting on slow storage I/O to fault pages back into RAM, so the CPU sits idle in I/O wait instead of executing application work.

A pathological page-reclaim state (violating Denning’s working-set criterion) in which major page faults (pgmajfault) and writeback saturate storage IOPS and latency, collapsing useful throughput. Under Linux, global reclaim can also evict clean page-cache pages of active executables, causing instruction-fetch thrashing before the OOM killer ever triggers. Mitigations include monitoring PSI (/proc/pressure/memory), using compressed RAM swap (zram/zswap) to absorb hot faults without hitting block devices, running userspace OOM daemons (systemd-oomd), and setting cgroup v2 memory.low/memory.min floors for critical services.
