Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with K / Kerberos

Kerberos is a network authentication protocol that lets you log in once and prove your identity to multiple services using encrypted tickets instead of resending your password.

Think of Kerberos like a theme-park wristband system: you show your ID at the front gate once, get a timed pass, and then show that pass at every ride without handing over your wallet or ID again. It matters on shared networks because even if someone watches the traffic between your computer and a file server, they never see your password.

In Kerberos, you authenticate with your password (for example via kinit) to get a TGT from the KDC, then exchange that TGT for service tickets over an untrusted network—checking your active tickets with klist. Accurate NTP clock synchronization is required because tickets carry short validity windows; if your clock drifts by more than a few minutes, authentication fails.

Why does Kerberos reject logins when a client’s system clock is several minutes out of sync with the KDC?

Because Kerberos embeds timestamps inside encrypted authenticators to stop attackers from replaying captured tickets; clocks outside the allowed skew window (typically 5 minutes) are treated as potential replay attempts.

Symmetric-key authentication protocol (Kerberos v5, RFC 4120) derived from Needham–Schroeder with timestamps: the Authentication Server (AS) issues a TGT, the Ticket-Granting Server (TGS) issues service tickets encrypted with CTS-HMAC (aes256-cts-hmac-sha384-192) or legacy keys, and replay caches cover the clock-skew window. Unlike TLS, Kerberos provides mutual authentication without a public-key PKI (unless PKINIT is used), but requires strict DNS/SPN canonicalization and keytab hygiene on service hosts.
