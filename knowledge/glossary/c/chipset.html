Chipset

The motherboard support chip (or set of chips) that connects the CPU to storage, USB, networking, and other peripheral devices.

If the CPU is the brain of a computer, the chipset is the nervous system’s main switchboard. The processor talks directly to high-speed parts like memory and graphics, while the chipset routes traffic from slower everyday devices—such as USB ports, SATA drives, audio, and Ethernet—so everything can share the highway cleanly.

On modern PCs, functions that once lived in a separate “northbridge” (memory controller and primary PCIe graphics lanes) have moved onto the CPU die itself, leaving the chipset—called the Platform Controller Hub (PCH) on Intel or FCH on AMD—to handle extra PCIe lanes, USB, SATA, and low-speed buses like SPI/I2C. On Linux, you can inspect what is attached to the chipset versus the CPU root complex using lspci -t.

Why does an NVMe SSD wired directly to the CPU typically have lower latency than one wired through the motherboard chipset?

Traffic through the chipset must cross the CPU-to-chipset interconnect (such as DMI or PCIe uplink) and share bandwidth with USB, SATA, and other chipset-attached devices.

Contemporary x86 platforms connect the CPU SoC to the PCH/FCH over a dedicated high-speed link (Intel DMI 4.0, electrically PCIe x8 Gen4, or AMD x4 Gen4 PCIe uplink), making that uplink a shared bottleneck when multiple chipset-attached NVMe drives, 10GbE NICs, and USB4 controllers saturate concurrently. The chipset also houses the Platform Security Processor / Management Engine, SPI flash controller for UEFI firmware, RTC, eSPI/LPC bridge for Super I/O, and root ports whose IOMMU grouping determines whether devices behind the chipset can be cleanly isolated for VFIO passthrough.
