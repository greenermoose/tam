C-state

A processor power-saving state that a CPU core or package enters when it is idle.

A C-state is like the difference between dozing lightly on a couch and going into a deep sleep in bed. A light nap lets the processor wake up the instant work arrives, while a deep sleep saves much more battery and heat—at the cost of taking a tiny fraction of a second longer to wake up.

On Linux, C0 means the CPU is actively running instructions, while C1, C2, C3, and deeper states progressively gate clocks and cut voltage to idle parts of the chip. You can inspect available states and residency counters under /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpu0/cpuidle/ or with powertop.

Why doesn’t the CPU jump straight to its deepest C-state the microsecond it becomes idle?

Entering and exiting deep C-states costs time and energy to flush caches and restore voltages. If an interrupt arrives a few microseconds later, the wake latency and transition energy exceed the power saved, so the OS picks a shallower state for brief idle gaps.

C-states are ACPI-exposed processor idle states (_CST or intel_idle MWAIT hints) characterized by exit latency, target residency, and cache-coherency behavior (whether L1/L2 caches flush and snoops remain active). The Linux cpuidle subsystem uses governors such as menu or teo to predict idle duration from timer wheels and IRQ history, while /dev/cpu_dma_latency lets latency-sensitive workloads clamp the maximum permitted exit latency.
