Clipboard

A temporary holding area provided by the desktop environment for transferring copied or cut data between applications.

Think of the clipboard like a small holding tray on your desk. When you copy or cut a sentence or picture in one app, it waits on that tray until you paste it into another app—or until you copy something else, which replaces what was there before.

On Linux desktops, there are two separate mechanisms: the standard clipboard (Ctrl+C / Ctrl+V) and the primary selection, where highlighting text lets you paste it with a middle mouse click. When you copy from a browser or document, the source app offers multiple formats (plain text, HTML, or an image) so the receiving app can pick the best fit—and if you close the source app before pasting, the data can disappear unless a clipboard manager saves it.

Why does pasting into a plain text editor strip fonts and links from copied web text, while pasting into a word processor keeps them?

The source app advertises multiple MIME formats (such as text/plain and text/html), and each receiving app requests the richest format it supports.

In both X11 (ICCCM CLIPBOARD / PRIMARY selections) and Wayland (wl_data_device / zwlr_data_control_v1), clipboard transfer is lazy and peer-to-peer: the owner advertises offered MIME types without copying payload bytes upfront. On paste, data streams on demand over a pipe file descriptor (wl_data_offer.receive) or X11 property transfer (INCR for large payloads). Because ownership ends when the source client exits, persistence requires a clipboard manager to eagerly read and re-offer data; Wayland also restricts background reads to focused surfaces or privileged protocols to prevent silent snooping on copied secrets.
