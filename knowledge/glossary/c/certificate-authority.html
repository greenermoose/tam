Certificate authority

A trusted organization that verifies identities and cryptographically signs digital certificates to vouch for public keys.

A certificate authority works like a passport office for the internet. When you visit a website, you have never met its owner in person, so the site shows a digital passport signed by an authority your computer already trusts. Because your browser recognizes that signature, it knows the site’s encryption key genuinely belongs to that domain.

On Linux and in web browsers, a curated root trust store (such as ca-certificates in /etc/ssl/certs) lists the root CA keys trusted by default for HTTPS, package managers, and TLS connections. Rather than signing leaf certificates directly with an offline root key, CAs use intermediate certificates so a compromised intermediate can be revoked without replacing the root in every operating system.

Why do browsers reject a website’s valid-looking certificate if the server omits an intermediate CA certificate?

The client’s trust store only contains root CA keys, so without the intermediate certificate bridging the leaf certificate to a trusted root, the chain of signatures cannot be verified.

In hierarchical PKI (RFC 5280), a CA acts as a trust anchor issuing X.509 v3 certificates bound to Subject Alternative Names (SANs), key usage, and basic constraints (CA:TRUE, optional pathLenConstraint). Because a single compromised or mis-issuing CA can sign for any domain unless restricted by Name Constraints, the WebPKI relies on Certificate Transparency (CT) append-only Merkle logs (RFC 6962 / 9162) for public auditability, DNS CAA records (RFC 8659) to declare authorized issuers per domain, and revocation mechanisms (CRLs, OCSP, OCSP stapling, or CRLite) whose soft-fail defaults in many clients represent a known trade-off between availability and security.
