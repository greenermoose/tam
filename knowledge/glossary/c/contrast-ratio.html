Contrast ratio

Contrast ratio measures the difference in brightness between the brightest white and the darkest black an image or display can produce.

Think of reading black ink on crisp white paper in good light versus faded gray pencil on dim gray cardboard. A higher contrast ratio means blacks look deeper and whites look brighter, making text sharper and pictures more vivid. On a monitor or in a color theme, good contrast makes things easier to read without straining your eyes.

Display specifications often quote a “static” contrast ratio (such as 1000:1 on many IPS LCD panels or 3000:1 on VA panels), which compares full white to full black at a fixed backlight level. Be wary of huge “dynamic” contrast numbers (like 1,000,000:1), which measure white with the backlight cranked up against black with the backlight dimmed—not what you see in a single frame. In desktop theming and web accessibility, contrast ratio also measures foreground text against its background (for example, WCAG AA requires at least 4.5:1 for normal text).

Why can a monitor advertised as having a 1,000,000:1 dynamic contrast ratio still look washed out in dark scenes?

Dynamic contrast measures white and black at different backlight levels over time; within a single frame that contains both bright and dark areas, the panel is limited by its much lower static contrast ratio.

Photometrically, contrast ratio is L_max / L_min, where luminance L is measured in cd/m² (nits). Static (on/off) contrast compares full-screen white against full-screen black at identical display settings, whereas ANSI contrast uses a 16-rectangle checkerboard in one frame, exposing internal light bleed and local-dimming haloing. OLEDs achieve near-infinite on/off ratios because off pixels emit zero light, though ambient room reflections still cap effective perceived contrast. In UI accessibility (WCAG 2.x), contrast is computed from sRGB relative luminance as (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05), yielding a range from 1:1 to 21:1.
