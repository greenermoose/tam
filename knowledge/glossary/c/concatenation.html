Concatenation

Concatenation means joining two or more sequences—such as pieces of text, lists, or files—end to end in order.

Think of coupling train cars together, or taping two strips of paper end to end. Nothing in the middle is changed or reordered; the second piece simply starts right where the first piece ends. You use concatenation whenever you combine a first name and a last name into a full name, or glue two text files into one longer file.

In Linux shells and programming languages, concatenation appears constantly. The cat command gets its name from “concatenate”: cat a.txt b.txt > both.txt streams a.txt followed immediately by b.txt into both.txt. In shell scripts, placing strings or variables next to each other ("$dir/$file") concatenates them, whereas languages like Lua (..) or Python (+) use an explicit operator. Be careful when joining files or paths: concatenation adds no separator or newline unless you include one.

Why does cat a.txt b.txt sometimes join the last line of a.txt and the first line of b.txt onto the same line?

cat copies bytes verbatim without inserting a newline between files; if a.txt does not end with a newline character, b.txt starts immediately on that same line.

Formally, concatenation is an associative binary operation on strings or lists with the empty sequence as its identity element (forming a free monoid). In systems programming, naive repeated concatenation in a loop can degrade to quadratic time and heap fragmentation if each step allocates and copies the growing prefix; builders, ropes, or pre-sized buffers keep amortized cost linear. At the I/O level, cat concatenates byte streams via read(2)/write(2) or zero-copy copy_file_range(2), which works for plain text or TAR streams but corrupts structured formats (such as PNG or ZIP) that rely on length headers or trailing directories.
