Circuit

A continuous closed loop of conductive material that allows electric current to flow from a power source, through components, and back.

Think of a circuit like a circular water pipe with a pump. The pump pushes water around the loop to spin a waterwheel, and the water has to return to the pump to keep moving. In electronics, the battery or power supply is the pump, wires and chips do the work, and if the loop is broken anywhere—like flipping a switch off—the flow stops immediately.

Inside a computer, millions of tiny circuits route power and signals across printed circuit board traces and silicon chips, always returning to a shared reference voltage called ground (GND). When troubleshooting hardware, an “open circuit” is a broken path where no current flows, whereas a “short circuit” is an unintended low-resistance bypass directly to ground that can overheat parts or trip overcurrent protection.

Why does a broken ground wire stop a device from working even if the positive power wire is still connected?

Current can only flow around a complete closed loop. Without a return path to the power source’s reference (ground), charge cannot circulate through the device.

In lumped-element analysis (valid when physical dimensions are much smaller than signal wavelength), closed conductive networks obey Kirchhoff’s circuit laws—conservation of charge at each node (KCL) and zero net potential difference around any closed loop (KVL)—alongside reactive impedance (Z = R + jωL + 1/(jωC)). At high digital clock speeds, PCB traces act as transmission lines: splits or gaps in the return-current reference plane create impedance discontinuities, signal reflections, and ground bounce, making unbroken ground planes directly beneath signal traces essential for signal integrity.
