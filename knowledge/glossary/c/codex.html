Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with C / Codex

A codex is a book made of stacked, bound pages rather than a continuous scroll.

Instead of one long roll of parchment, a codex is made of separate sheets sewn together and protected by covers. This is the form books take today. Early codices were copied by hand and could be opened to any page, which made them far easier to use than scrolls.

The codex replaced the scroll in late antiquity and became the standard book form in medieval Europe. Pages were written on parchment or paper, often by a scribe working from an exemplar, with headings and notes added later. Because the text is divided into pages, reference and indexing became practical, shaping how documents are organized even after printing.

Codicology studies the codex as a physical object: quires of folded parchment, ruling, pricking and binding reveal how a manuscript was made and help date it. Digital editions preserve page breaks and rubrication as structural markup, while facsimiles capture the physical page. The codex's page-based structure underlies modern pagination, indexes and the idea of a document page.
