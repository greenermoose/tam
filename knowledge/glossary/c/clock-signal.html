Clock signal

A steady, repeating electrical pulse that synchronizes operations across digital circuits.

Think of a clock signal like a metronome or drummer keeping a marching band in step so nobody bumps into each other. Inside a computer, that steady beat—happening billions of times each second—tells the processor and memory when to take their next step together.

Clock frequency is measured in hertz (Hz); modern CPUs run at gigahertz (GHz), meaning billions of pulses per second. Frequency alone does not equal speed, though—processors also raise or lower their clock speed dynamically to save battery or avoid overheating, which you can inspect on Linux under /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpufreq.

Why can a 3.5 GHz modern processor outperform an older 4.0 GHz processor?

Clock frequency only counts ticks per second, not how much work happens per tick. Newer CPUs execute more instructions per cycle and have larger, faster caches and better branch prediction.

A clock signal is a periodic square wave distributed across balanced clock trees (such as H-trees) to edge-triggered flip-flops in synchronous logic. Timing closure requires every combinational path to satisfy setup and hold windows around the active edge, bounded by clock skew and jitter. Dynamic power scales roughly as C × V² × f, driving DVFS (Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling) and clock gating; signals crossing between asynchronous clock domains (CDC) risk metastability and require multi-stage synchronizers or Gray-coded FIFOs.
