CTLE (Continuous Time Linear Equalizer)

An analog filter inside a high-speed receiver that boosts weakened high-frequency signal edges on long copper links.

Imagine someone shouting down a long hallway: the sharp consonants get muffled while the low hum carries farther, so the words blur together. High-speed cables have the same problem—fast electrical pulses smear into each other over distance. CTLE works like a specialized hearing aid at the receiving end, sharpening those blurred edges back up so the chip can reliably tell 1s from 0s.

Copper traces and cables used by PCIe, DisplayPort, and USB4 muffle fast voltage transitions much more than slow ones, shrinking the signal "eye" at the receiver. CTLE amplifies high frequencies continuously before the receiver samples the bits, often working alongside transmitter pre-emphasis and a receiver Decision Feedback Equalizer (DFE).

Why can’t a receiver simply crank CTLE gain to maximum on every cable?

Because CTLE is a linear analog filter, it boosts high-frequency noise and crosstalk from neighboring wires along with the wanted signal. Too much peaking also causes signal overshoot and ringing, which can close the eye instead of opening it.

A linear analog peaking filter—typically a first- or second-order transfer function with a zero below Nyquist and poles near or above Nyquist—that compensates frequency-dependent channel insertion loss from conductor skin effect (scaling with the square root of frequency) and dielectric loss (scaling linearly with frequency). Unlike a DFE, which is non-linear and cancels post-cursor inter-symbol interference without amplifying broadband noise, CTLE amplifies high-frequency noise and FEXT/NEXT crosstalk proportionally to its peaking gain. During PCIe Gen3–Gen6 link training, the LTSSM equalization phase jointly tunes TX presets and RX CTLE/DFE coefficients to maximize eye margin, where PAM4 signaling (PCIe 6.0) leaves much tighter SNR headroom.
