Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with C / Color depth

The number of bits used to represent the color (and sometimes transparency) of each pixel in an image or on a display.

Think of color depth like the size of a box of crayons. A small 8-crayon box can only draw blocky, rough pictures, while a giant box with millions of shades lets a sunset fade smoothly from orange to blue without visible stripes. Color depth measures how many shades each dot on your screen can store.

Most standard images and monitors use 8 bits per color channel (red, green, and blue = 24 bits per pixel, giving 256 shades per channel and about 16.7 million colors). Higher-end monitors and photo or video workflows use 10 bits per channel (30-bit color) to prevent smooth gradients—such as dark skies—from showing stair-stepped “banding.”

Why does an 8-bit-per-channel PNG image often show up as “32-bit color” in image tools?

It allocates 8 bits each for red, green, and blue (24 bits) plus an extra 8-bit alpha channel for transparency, totaling 32 bits per pixel.

Color depth specifies component quantization precision—commonly 8-bit (XRGB8888 / ARGB8888, 24/32 bpp), 10-bit (XRGB2101010, 30 bpp), or 16-bit half-float (FP16, 64 bpp) in Linux DRM/KMS framebuffers and Wayland/Vulkan scanout. Greater bit precision lowers quantization error and banding after gamma/PQ EOTF curves or wide-gamut (Rec. 2020) transforms, though perceived output also depends on native panel bit depth versus FRC (Frame Rate Control) temporal dithering and link bandwidth (HDMI/DisplayPort DSC).
