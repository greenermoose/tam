Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with C / Cuneiform

Cuneiform is one of the earliest writing systems, made by pressing a stylus into soft clay to leave wedge-shaped marks.

Thousands of years ago scribes pressed a reed tool into wet clay. Each push left a wedge, and groups of wedges stood for words or sounds. When the clay dried the record lasted for millennia, which is why so many tablets survive.

Cuneiform began in Sumer around 3400 BCE for accounting, then developed to write Akkadian, Hittite and other languages. It combines logograms, which stand for words, with syllabic signs for sounds. Excavated tablets record laws, letters, epics and trade, and their decipherment in the nineteenth century opened the history of the ancient Near East.

Signs are built from a limited repertoire of vertical, horizontal and diagonal wedges made with a reed stylus; the same sign can be polyvalent, reading as a logogram or a syllable depending on context. Unicode encodes cuneiform in dedicated blocks beginning at U+12000, so rendering requires a font with that coverage and direction handling that varies by language and period.
