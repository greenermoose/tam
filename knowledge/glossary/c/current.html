Current

Current is the rate at which electric charge flows through a conductive path.

Think of water flowing through a pipe: voltage is the pressure pushing the water, while current—measured in amperes or amps—is how much water actually flows past a point each second. Electric current can only flow when there is a complete, unbroken loop (a closed circuit) from the power source, through the device, and back again.

One ampere equals one coulomb of electric charge passing a cross-section of a conductor per second. Under Ohm’s law (I = V / R), current rises when voltage increases or resistance drops. In computing and USB-C charging, devices negotiate higher voltages because heat wasted in a cable grows with the square of the current (P = I²R).

Why does USB-C Power Delivery raise voltage (for example, to 20 V) to deliver 100 W instead of pushing 20 A at 5 V?

Power equals voltage times current (P = V × I), while resistive heat loss in a wire scales with current squared (I²R). Sending 20 A at 5 V would generate sixteen times more cable heat than sending 5 A at 20 V for the same 100 W, requiring much thicker, heavier copper wires.

The macroscopic drift of mobile charge carriers (electrons in metals, electrons and holes in semiconductors, or ions in electrolytes) under an applied electric field, expressed as current density J = n·q·v_d = σ·E; individual carrier drift velocity v_d is typically millimeters per second even though the electromagnetic signal propagates near the speed of light. In hardware design, high current density drives Joule heating (I²R) and electromigration in fine IC interconnects, high-frequency AC experiences skin effect that crowds current toward the conductor surface and raises effective resistance, and rapid current transients (di/dt) across parasitic trace inductance induce supply droop and ground bounce (V = L·di/dt) unless damped by local decoupling capacitors.
