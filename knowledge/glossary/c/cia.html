CIA (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability)

The three foundational properties used to evaluate and design information security: Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability.

Think of the CIA triad like keeping a private journal in a locked desk: Confidentiality means only you can read it, Integrity means nobody can secretly erase or rewrite pages, and Availability means you can actually unlock the desk and read it whenever you need it. A system is only trustworthy when all three hold together.

In real Linux and computing setups, different tools protect each property: file permissions and disk encryption (such as LUKS) protect confidentiality, checksums (sha256sum) and package signatures protect integrity, and backups, RAID, or redundant servers protect availability. Notice the trade-offs—locking a system down so strictly that legitimate users cannot log in breaks availability.

Why doesn’t encrypting a file automatically guarantee its integrity?

Encryption hides the contents from unauthorized readers, but without an integrity check (such as a MAC or digital signature), someone can still alter or corrupt the scrambled data in transit or on disk.

Formal security models and cryptographic primitives map differently onto the triad: Bell–LaPadula enforces confidentiality (“no read up, no write down”), whereas Biba and Clark–Wilson formalize integrity (“no read down, no write up” and well-formed transactions). Modern protocols pair confidentiality and integrity via Authenticated Encryption with Associated Data (AEAD, such as AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305) so tampered ciphertexts are rejected before plaintext release. Availability sits outside pure cryptography—defending against resource exhaustion (SYN floods, algorithmic-complexity DoS) and crash or Byzantine faults requires rate limiting, redundancy, and replication trade-offs.
