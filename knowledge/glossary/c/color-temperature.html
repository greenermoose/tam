Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with C / Color temperature

A scale in kelvin describing whether white light appears warm (reddish-yellow) or cool (bluish).

Think of heating a piece of metal: at first it glows warm red-orange, and as it gets much hotter it turns bright white and then bluish-white. Surprisingly, lower color-temperature numbers (like 2700 K) look cozy and warm like candlelight, while higher numbers (like 6500 K or 9000 K) look crisp and cool like an overcast sky.

Most computer monitors and web graphics target a standard white point of 6500 K (daylight). When you turn on Night Light on a Linux desktop in the evening, it lowers the display’s color temperature (for example, toward 3500 K–4500 K) by dialing back blue light so the screen looks warmer and gentler in a dark room.

If you want your screen to look warmer and less blue at night, should you raise or lower its color temperature in kelvin?

Lower it. Warm, amber-red light corresponds to lower kelvin values (around 2700 K–4000 K), whereas higher kelvin values look cooler and bluer.

Color temperature is defined by Planck’s blackbody radiation locus in CIE chromaticity space; non-Planckian sources such as LEDs use Correlated Color Temperature (CCT), projecting (u, v) coordinates onto the nearest point on the Planckian locus, often paired with a Duv (green–magenta) offset. The CIE daylight standard illuminant (x ≈ 0.3127, y ≈ 0.3290, CCT ≈ 6504 K) anchors sRGB and Rec. 709; Linux compositors and ICC calibration (colord, Wayland color management) shift white points via 1D/3D gamma LUTs or Bradford chromatic adaptation at the cost of reduced peak luminance.
