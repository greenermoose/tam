CMOS - Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor

Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor—the dominant transistor circuit technology used to build modern microchips with very low idle power consumption.

Imagine two gates on a water pipe where one is always shut whenever the other is open. Because they never stay open at the same time, almost no water leaks when nothing is changing. CMOS chips pair opposite tiny switches in the same way, so computers and phones use very little power while idle and only draw noticeable power when bits are actively flipping.

CMOS logic pairs an n-channel MOSFET (pulling low) with a p-channel MOSFET (pulling high) so one transistor is off in any steady 0 or 1 state, keeping static power tiny and allowing billions of transistors on one chip. Watch out for a common naming quirk in PC hardware: “clearing the CMOS” refers to resetting firmware settings stored in battery-backed NVRAM/RTC, named after the low-power CMOS chip originally used for it.

Why does a CMOS processor draw much more power and run hotter under heavy load than when idle?

In steady states one transistor in each pair is off, so little current flows; most power is consumed during switching, when both transistors briefly conduct and internal capacitances charge and discharge.

Static CMOS gates pair complementary PMOS pull-up and NMOS pull-down networks between VDD and GND, delivering rail-to-rail swings, high noise margins, and near-zero DC quiescent current in ideal devices. Total power splits into dynamic switching (α × C_load × VDD² × f), short-circuit crowbar current during finite input edges, and—increasingly significant at sub-10nm FinFET/GAAFET nodes—subthreshold and gate-oxide leakage (I_leak × VDD). Guard rings and well/substrate ties are required to prevent latch-up across parasitic PNPN thyristor structures.
