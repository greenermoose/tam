CDR (Clock and Data Recovery)

A receiver circuit that extracts both the timing clock and the data bits from a single high-speed serial signal without needing a separate clock wire.

Think of listening to someone tap out Morse code without a metronome: after a few taps, you naturally lock onto their rhythm so you know when each beat lands. High-speed cables like USB and DisplayPort do the same thing—instead of running an extra wire just to carry a timing beat, the receiving chip listens to the incoming data pulses and reconstructs the beat itself.

At gigabit speeds, sending a separate clock wire fails because tiny length differences between traces (skew) cause the clock and data to drift out of phase. Line encodings such as 8b/10b, 64b/66b, or 128b/132b guarantee regular bit transitions so the CDR’s phase-locked loop (PLL) stays synchronized and does not drift during long runs of identical bits.

Why do high-speed serial links scramble or encode data (such as 8b/10b) before sending it to a CDR circuit?

A long string of all zeros or all ones has no signal transitions for the CDR to track, causing its recovered clock to drift out of phase and misread bit boundaries.

High-speed SerDes receivers implement CDR using either an analog PLL or a digital bang-bang (Alexander) phase detector that samples at both data-eye centers and edge transitions to drive a phase interpolator (PI) or VCO. Unlike source-synchronous parallel buses, embedded-clock links tolerate multi-UI lane-to-lane skew (handled later by deskew FIFOs) while trading off transition density overhead for bounded run-length and DC balance. Key failure modes include jitter amplification when the CDR loop bandwidth is too high, loss of lock under excessive deterministic jitter/ISI without CTLE/DFE equalization, and cycle slips during spread-spectrum clocking (SSC) if the loop bandwidth cannot track the modulation profile.
