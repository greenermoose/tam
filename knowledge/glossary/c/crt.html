CRT - Cathode-Ray Tube

A Cathode-Ray Tube (CRT) is a legacy vacuum-tube display technology that draws images by sweeping an electron beam across a phosphor-coated glass screen.

Before flat LCD and OLED screens existed, almost every computer monitor and television was a CRT—the heavy, deep glass box that sat on desks for decades. Inside the glass tube, a tiny beam of electrons painted the picture line by line from top to bottom faster than your eye could follow, making the glowing coating on the inside of the glass light up. Even though CRTs are rarely used today, modern display terms like “refresh rate,” “scanlines,” and “overscan” come from how CRTs worked.

Unlike a flat panel with fixed physical pixels, a CRT had no native resolution and could display multiple resolutions cleanly by changing how fast the electron beam swept horizontally and vertically. Because the phosphor glow faded almost immediately after the beam passed, CRTs had virtually zero motion blur and deep blacks, but flickered noticeably at low refresh rates like 60 Hz. On Linux, legacy X11 Modeline timings (hsync, vsync, front and back porch) still reflect the blanking intervals CRTs needed to return the beam to the next line or top of the screen.

Why do display timings still include horizontal and vertical “blanking” intervals on modern digital monitors?

Blanking intervals originated with CRTs, where the electron beam had to be turned off while magnetic coils steered it back to the start of the next line or top of the frame. Digital protocols kept the timing structure for compatibility and metadata packets.

A color CRT houses three thermionic electron guns (RGB) accelerated by a 20–30 kV anode potential and magnetically deflected by horizontal (15–130 kHz) and vertical (50–160 Hz) yoke coils through a shadow mask or aperture grille onto short-persistence phosphors. Geometry, convergence, and focus depend on analog deflection linearity, while luminance follows an approximate power-law response (gamma ~2.2–2.5) from grid-cathode voltage to beam current. Blanking intervals (front porch, sync pulse, back porch) allow flyback retrace and DC black-level clamping; Linux DRM/KMS still encodes these parameters in drm_display_mode and EDID detailed timing descriptors.
