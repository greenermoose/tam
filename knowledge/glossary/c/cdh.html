CDH (Computational Diffie-Hellman)

A cryptographic assumption stating that even if an attacker sees two parties’ public Diffie-Hellman values, computing their shared secret key is practically impossible.

Imagine two people each mix a secret paint color into a shared base color and trade the resulting mixtures in public. Even if someone watches the trade, they cannot unmix the public bowls or combine them to find the final secret color both people create privately. That one-way difficulty is what keeps key exchanges private over untrusted networks.

In practice, protocols like TLS (using X25519 or ECDHE) rely on CDH or its stronger cousin, Decisional Diffie-Hellman (DDH), when negotiating session keys. CDH is weaker than DDH—meaning an attacker who can distinguish a shared secret from random noise breaks DDH without necessarily being able to compute the secret itself—and both are implied by the Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP).

If someone discovers a fast way to solve the Discrete Logarithm Problem on a curve, does CDH still hold?

No. Solving DLP lets an attacker recover the private exponent from the public share and then compute the shared secret directly, so breaking DLP automatically breaks CDH.

Formally, in a cyclic group G of order q with generator g, CDH holds if for every probabilistic polynomial-time (PPT) adversary A, the probability of outputting g^(ab) given (g, g^a, g^b) for uniform a, b in Z_q is negligible in the security parameter. CDH is strictly no harder than DLP (a DLP oracle solves CDH) and at least as hard as DDH; in pairing-friendly groups where a bilinear map e(g^a, g^b) = e(g, g)^(ab) exists, DDH is easy while CDH can remain hard (gap Diffie-Hellman groups). Classic Diffie-Hellman key exchange without authentication or a KDF/MAC does not achieve IND-CCA security from CDH alone, and Shor’s algorithm breaks DLP (and thus CDH) in polynomial time on a fault-tolerant quantum computer.
