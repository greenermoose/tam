Framebuffer

A region of memory that stores the color values for every pixel currently shown on a display.

Think of a framebuffer as a digital painting canvas held in your computer’s memory. Every tiny dot (pixel) on your monitor has a matching spot on that canvas storing its color and brightness, and your screen constantly reads from it dozens of times a second to show you the picture.

On Linux, the display controller scans out a framebuffer from system RAM or GPU VRAM to drive the monitor’s video signal. Older kernels and simple consoles used the legacy /dev/fb0 (fbdev) interface, while modern systems use DRM/KMS (Direct Rendering Manager / Kernel Mode Setting) framebuffers managed by the GPU driver and Wayland compositor, plus efifb or simpledrm during early boot before the full GPU driver loads.

Why do modern compositors use two or more framebuffers (double or triple buffering) instead of drawing directly into the one currently being scanned to the monitor?

Drawing directly into the active scanout buffer causes screen tearing, because the display controller may read half of the old frame and half of the new one. Rendering into a back buffer and swapping pointers atomically at VBlank avoids tearing.

A DRM framebuffer (drm_fb) wraps one or more GEM/DMA-BUF backing objects with explicit geometry and memory layout metadata: width, height, FourCC pixel format (such as DRM_FORMAT_XRGB8888 or ARGB2101010), stride (bytes per row, aligned to GPU tile or cache boundaries), offset, and format modifiers (linear vs. tiled/compressed). Wayland compositors collect client wl_buffer surfaces—via wl_shm or zwp_linux_dmabuf_v1—and either composite them into a primary plane framebuffer or assign them directly to hardware overlay planes via atomic KMS commits (drmModeAtomicCommit) synchronized to VBLANK page-flip events.
