Fiber Optic

A transmission medium that carries information as pulses of light through thin, flexible strands of glass or plastic.

Instead of pushing electricity through copper wires, fiber optic cables send rapid flashes of light down hair-thin strands of ultra-clear glass. Because light travels fast and isn’t disrupted by nearby power lines, motors, or lightning, fiber optic lines can carry huge amounts of data over long distances without electrical static or signal loss.

Fiber optic cables work by total internal reflection: light enters a high-refractive-index glass core and bounces off the surrounding lower-refractive-index cladding without escaping. In practice, you’ll encounter two main types: single-mode fiber (SMF, narrow ~9 µm core, yellow jacket, driven by lasers for kilometers-long links) and multi-mode fiber (MMF, wider 50 or 62.5 µm core, aqua or orange jacket, driven by VCSELs or LEDs for short datacenter runs). Mixing SMF and MMF patch cables or transceivers causes severe signal loss or no link at all.

Why can’t you plug a single-mode fiber patch cable into a short-range multi-mode SFP transceiver and expect a reliable link?

Single-mode fiber has a ~9 µm core, while multi-mode transceivers launch light into a 50 or 62.5 µm spot. Most of the light misses the tiny single-mode core (high coupling loss), and modal and wavelength mismatches prevent a stable link.

Optical guidance relies on Snell’s law: rays exceeding the critical angle arcsin(n2 / n1) remain bound in the core. SMF supports only the fundamental LP01 mode (typically 1310 nm or 1550 nm), eliminating modal dispersion so reach is bounded by chromatic dispersion, polarization-mode dispersion, and Rayleigh scattering attenuation (~0.2–0.35 dB/km). MMF (850 nm) suffers modal dispersion as multiple propagation paths arrive at different times, capping bandwidth-distance products (e.g., OM4 at ~400 m for 10GBASE-SR). Optical-to-electrical conversion uses modulated laser diodes (DML/EML) on transmit and PIN or avalanche photodiodes (APD) feeding transimpedance amplifiers on receive; dirty end-faces or APC/UPC polish mismatches cause back-reflection and link flaps.
