FLIT (Flow Control Unit)

A fixed-size unit of data used by modern high-speed hardware interconnects to move information efficiently.

Imagine loading cargo onto a train where every car is the exact same size, rather than custom-building a different-sized crate for every box. Modern high-speed computer links use FLITs—uniform, fixed-size packets—so hardware can sort, check for errors, and forward data at extreme speeds without stopping to measure each piece first.

Older interconnects such as PCIe 5.0 and earlier sent variable-length Transaction Layer Packets (TLPs) wrapped in start and end framing bytes. Starting with PCIe 6.0 (as well as CXL and on-chip Networks-on-Chip), links switch to fixed-size FLITs (Flow Control Units). Fixed boundaries make it practical to apply Forward Error Correction (FEC) across noisy PAM-4 electrical lanes and simplify credit-based flow control between sender and receiver buffers.

Why did PCIe 6.0 switch from variable-length packets to fixed-size FLITs?

PAM-4 signaling doubles data rate per cycle but increases bit-error rates, requiring low-latency Forward Error Correction (FEC) and CRC on every transmission block. Fixed-size FLITs let the physical and data-link layers compute FEC and framing in predictable hardware pipelines without variable-length boundary parsing.

In PCIe 6.0, Flit mode replaces 128b/130b encoding and variable-length DLLP/TLP framing with a 256-byte FLIT (236 bytes payload, 6 bytes DLP/TLP header/control, 8 bytes CRC, and 6 bytes FEC, transmitted with 1b/1b encoding over PAM-4). Multiple small TLPs can pack into a single FLIT, or a large TLP can span consecutive FLITs, eliminating per-TLP DLLP Ack/Nak overhead at the link layer. In wormhole-routed Networks-on-Chip (NoCs), a packet is segmented into head, body, and tail flits; flit-level virtual-channel credit flow control prevents head-of-line blocking and deadlock while keeping router input buffers sized to a small multiple of the flit width.
