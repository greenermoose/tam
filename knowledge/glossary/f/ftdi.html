FTDI (Future Technology Devices International)

FTDI (Future Technology Devices International) is a widely used maker of bridge chips that convert USB connections into serial (UART) interfaces.

Modern laptops usually only have USB ports, while many circuit boards, routers, and microcontrollers still speak a simpler, older “serial” language over bare pins. An FTDI chip acts like a translator inside a small cable or adapter board: one side plugs into your computer’s USB port, and the other side talks serial to the hardware, letting you see boot messages or send commands to devices that have no screen or keyboard.

When you plug an FTDI adapter (such as an FT232R or FT2232H) into a Linux machine, the kernel’s ftdi_sio driver typically binds to it and creates a character device like /dev/ttyUSB0. You can inspect it with dmesg or lsusb and connect using a serial terminal like picocom or minicom. Unlike some cheaper USB-to-serial clones, genuine FTDI chips store unique serial numbers in EEPROM, which makes writing stable udev rules much easier when multiple adapters are plugged in.

Why do Linux users often need group membership (such as uucp or dialout) or a udev rule to open /dev/ttyUSB0?

The kernel creates /dev/ttyUSB0 owned by root and a serial-access group with restricted permissions so arbitrary unprivileged processes cannot snoop on or transmit over attached hardware. Adding your user to that group or matching the device’s USB VID/PID in udev grants access safely.

FTDI’s bridge ICs (including the single-channel FT232R/FT232H and multi-channel FT2232H/FT4232H) expose USB bulk endpoints that encapsulate asynchronous UART frames, synchronous FIFO, or MPSSE (Multi-Protocol Synchronous Serial Engine) modes for SPI/I2C/JTAG. Under Linux, the in-kernel ftdi_sio USB-serial driver handles tty line discipline integration and baud-rate divisor programming, while userspace tools such as OpenOCD or libftdi can detach the kernel driver via libusb to drive MPSSE directly. Common edge cases include latency-timer buffering (default 16 ms in hardware, tunable via sysfs latency_timer for low-latency protocols) and non-standard baud rates achieved via fractional clock dividers.
