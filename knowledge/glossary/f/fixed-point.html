Fixed-point

Fixed-point stores a fraction as an integer with an agreed scale that never changes.

If you store cents instead of euros, you can use whole numbers and still handle prices. The decimal point is fixed in your agreement, not stored in the number. Fixed-point binary works the same way with a chosen number of fractional bits.

With a scale of 256, the stored integer 384 means 384 / 256, or exactly 1.5. Eight fractional bits give steps of 1/256. Values such as 1/10 do not divide evenly by that scale and must be rounded. Fixed point uses ordinary integer arithmetic, so it is predictable and fast on hardware without a floating-point unit.

Can fixed-point represent every decimal amount exactly?

No. It represents multiples of its step exactly. Choose a step that matches the problem: hundredths of a currency unit for money, or a power of two when scaling with bit shifts.

A Q notation such as Q7.8 records the number of integer and fractional bits in a word. Addition needs the same scale on both operands; multiplication produces twice the fractional bits and normally needs a shift plus a defined rounding mode. The fixed exponent gives a constant absolute precision across the range, unlike floating point, whose relative precision is roughly constant. Range and resolution trade against each other in a fixed word width.
