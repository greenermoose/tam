Full Duplex

Full duplex is a communication mode that allows two devices to send and receive data at the exact same time.

Think of the difference between a walkie-talkie and a phone call. On a walkie-talkie, you have to say “over” and wait for the other person to finish before you can speak; on a phone call, both people can talk and hear each other simultaneously. Full duplex works like a phone call: because sending and receiving do not have to take turns, data flows much faster and neither side has to pause for the other.

In everyday computing, switched Ethernet and standard 3-wire RS-232 serial links run in full duplex, using separate transmit (TX) and receive (RX) paths so traffic in one direction does not block traffic in the other. You can verify link duplex on a Linux Ethernet interface with ethtool eth0. If one side of an Ethernet link is forced to full duplex while the other falls back to half duplex (a duplex mismatch), the half-duplex side sees late collisions and throughput collapses even though the link stays up.

What happens if two connected Ethernet ports disagree on duplex mode (one full, one half)?

The full-duplex side transmits whenever it wants without listening for incoming frames, while the half-duplex side interprets those simultaneous transmissions as collisions and drops or retransmits packets, causing severe packet loss and slow transfers.

Full-duplex operation achieves simultaneous bidirectional signaling either through physically separated channels (e.g., 100BASE-TX using distinct twisted pairs for TX and RX, or 4-wire RS-485), frequency-division duplexing (FDD), or active echo cancellation over shared media. Notably, 1000BASE-T and 10GBASE-T Ethernet transmit and receive concurrently on all four twisted pairs using hybrid circuits and adaptive digital signal processing (DSP) to subtract near-end echo and crosstalk (NEXT/FEXT) from the local transmitter. Because collisions cannot occur on point-to-point full-duplex links, CSMA/CD is disabled and flow control relies on IEEE 802.3x PAUSE frames or Priority Flow Control rather than physical backoff.
