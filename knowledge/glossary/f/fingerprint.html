Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with F / Fingerprint

Either a short cryptographic summary used to verify a public key or certificate, or a biometric scan of the ridges on a human finger.

Because a real digital security key is a huge string of characters that no human could easily read aloud, software shrinks it into a short “fingerprint”—a compact code you can compare at a glance. Just like a person’s physical fingerprint, even a tiny change to the key produces a completely different code, making it easy to spot a fake.

In computing, “fingerprint” has two distinct meanings depending on context: a cryptographic key fingerprint (a short hash such as SHA256:... of an SSH host key or TLS certificate used to verify you’re talking to the real server out of band), and a biometric fingerprint (ridge patterns captured by a laptop reader, managed on Linux by fprintd, to unlock a session or approve privilege prompts locally).

Why is comparing a 43-character SSH key fingerprint safe when the actual public key is hundreds of characters long?

The fingerprint is a cryptographic hash (such as SHA-256) of the public key. Because cryptographic hashes are collision-resistant, an attacker cannot practically generate a different key that produces the same short fingerprint.

Cryptographic fingerprints (such as OpenSSH SHA256 base64 digests over the wire-encoded key blob from ssh-keygen -lf, PGP 160-bit/256-bit hex digests, or X.509 SPKI pins) rely on second-preimage resistance so any bit-flip in key material yields an uncorrelated digest; legacy MD5 fingerprints are deprecated due to collision weaknesses. Biometric fingerprints, by contrast, are noisy analog measurements converted into minutiae templates stored locally (often inside a Match-on-Chip sensor or TPM-sealed store via libfprint/fprintd), where false-acceptance/false-rejection rates (FAR/FRR) govern security—making biometrics non-revocable local unlock factors rather than transferable cryptographic secrets.
