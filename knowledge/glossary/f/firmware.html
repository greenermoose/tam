Firmware

Low-level software stored permanently inside a hardware device that initializes and controls it.

Before your operating system even starts, your computer’s chips need basic instructions to wake up and talk to each other. Firmware is that built-in instruction set, saved right inside the hardware—not on your main drive—so it stays there even when the power is off. Printers, Wi-Fi cards, SSDs, and motherboards all rely on firmware to work.

Unlike normal programs stored on a filesystem, firmware lives in non-volatile flash or ROM on the motherboard (UEFI or BIOS) or on peripheral controllers (NICs, GPUs, NVMe drives). On Linux, motherboard firmware hands off hardware descriptions (via ACPI or Device Tree) to the bootloader and kernel, while many peripherals also require the kernel to load binary firmware blobs from /lib/firmware during driver initialization.

If you wipe and reinstall Linux on a new SSD, why does your computer’s UEFI settings menu still exist?

Because UEFI firmware is stored in a dedicated flash chip on the motherboard rather than on the SSD. Replacing or wiping the drive removes the operating system and bootloader, but leaves the motherboard firmware intact.

Firmware spans system boot firmware (UEFI PEI/DXE phases, or Coreboot/U-Boot) and embedded controller or device microcode (ACPI EC, Intel ME/AMD PSP, NVMe/Wi-Fi SoCs). Boot firmware initializes DRAM controllers, configures PCIe BARs and IOMMU/DMAR tables, and exposes runtime services (EFI_RUNTIME_SERVICES) and ACPI/SMBIOS tables before ExitBootServices() transfers control to the OS. Operational trade-offs and failure modes include bricked flash on interrupted updates (mitigated by A/B capsule updates and fwupd), ABI drift between kernel drivers and /lib/firmware blobs, and opaque proprietary firmware running in privileged modes (SMM, Ring -3) outside OS memory protection.
