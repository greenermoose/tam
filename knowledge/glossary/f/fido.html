Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with F / FIDO (Fast Identity Online)

An open family of authentication standards that replaces or strengthens passwords with cryptographic credentials bound to a user’s device or security key.

Instead of typing a password that you might forget or accidentally give away to a fake website, FIDO lets you log in by tapping a hardware security key or unlocking your phone or laptop. Your device proves who you are using secret math that never leaves the key—and even if you click a lookalike phishing link, the key refuses to sign in because the website address doesn’t match.

FIDO evolved from U2F (second-factor hardware tokens) into FIDO2, which combines two standards: WebAuthn (the browser and OS API talking to websites) and CTAP2 (the protocol talking to external USB, NFC, or Bluetooth authenticators). When you register a passkey or security key, it creates a unique public/private key pair scoped to that exact domain. Unlike SMS one-time codes or TOTP apps, FIDO verifies the site origin automatically during login, blocking phishing proxies.

Why does a FIDO2 security key resist phishing even if a user enters their username on a convincing lookalike domain?

During authentication, the client includes the real origin in clientDataJSON, and the authenticator checks that the Relying Party ID matches the stored credential. A lookalike domain produces a different origin, so the authenticator refuses to sign or produces a signature bound to the fake domain that the real server rejects.

A FIDO2/WebAuthn ceremony binds an assertion over authenticatorData || SHA-256(clientDataJSON) using ECDSA (P-256) or Ed25519 keys stored inside a hardware-backed keystore or CTAP2 authenticator. authenticatorData encodes the SHA-256 of the Relying Party ID, flags (UP user presence, UV user verification, BE/BS backup eligibility and state), and a monotonic signCount to detect cloned authenticators. On Linux, userland communicates with HID or NFC tokens via libfido2 over /dev/hidraw* (requiring appropriate udev permissions) or through desktop portals, with discoverable (resident) credentials enabling passwordless flows.
