Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with F / Firewall

Software or hardware that inspects network traffic and decides what to allow or block based on rules.

Think of a firewall as a security guard at the door of your computer or network. Every piece of data trying to enter or leave has to pass that guard, which checks a list of rules to decide whether to let it through or turn it away. This helps stop unwanted visitors or suspicious programs from reaching your machine.

On Linux, a host firewall filters incoming and outgoing packets by IP address, port, protocol, and whether a packet belongs to an already-established connection. Tools like nft (nftables) or frontends such as ufw and firewalld manage these rulesets, typically allowing outbound traffic while blocking unsolicited inbound connections unless a service port (such as TCP 22 for SSH) is explicitly opened.

Why can your browser still receive web pages when your host firewall blocks all unsolicited inbound connections?

Because a stateful firewall tracks outgoing connections in a state table. When you request a page, it records the outbound flow and automatically permits the returning response packets as part of that established connection, while still dropping unrelated inbound traffic.

Modern Linux firewalls rely on the Netfilter framework (nftables in kernel space, replacing legacy iptables/ip6tables/ebtables) or eBPF/XDP hooks for earlier, lower-overhead packet drops before sk_buff allocation. nftables evaluates tables containing hook-attached chains (ingress, prerouting, input, forward, output, postrouting) with verdicts (accept, drop, reject, jump), backed by nf_conntrack for stateful L4 tracking and NAT. Edge cases include asymmetric routing breaking conntrack state, conntrack table exhaustion under SYN floods (mitigated by notrack or SYNPROXY), and interactions between container bridges and host forward chains.
