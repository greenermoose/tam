FF (Form Feed, 0x0C)

A non-printing control character (hexadecimal 0x0C) originally used to advance a printer to a new page and commonly used in terminals to clear the screen.

Think of Form Feed like a “next page” button on an old typewriter or printer: instead of typing a visible letter, it told the paper to roll forward to the top of a fresh sheet. Today, even though most people aren’t feeding paper into line printers, you still trigger this same signal when you press Ctrl+L in a terminal to wipe the screen clean so you can see clearly again.

In ASCII and UTF-8, Form Feed is byte 0x0C (decimal 12, escape \f in C and Python), one of the C0 control codes. Unlike LF (0x0A), which moves down one line, FF originally meant a page break. Modern shells and line-editing libraries (like GNU Readline in Bash) intercept Ctrl+L to clear the visible viewport and redraw the prompt, whereas a raw 0x0C printed to a terminal or viewed in a file often renders as a newline or ^L.

Why does pressing Ctrl+L in Bash clear the screen even though printing a literal \f byte in a terminal often just moves the cursor down?

Because Bash (via Readline) binds the input byte 0x0C (Ctrl+L) to its clear-screen command, which emits ANSI escape sequences to wipe the display. A literal 0x0C written to standard output is interpreted by the terminal emulator itself, which typically treats it like a line feed rather than clearing the screen.

Defined in ECMA-48 / ISO/IEC 6429 as FF (0x0C), form feed has no standardized screen-clearing semantics in VT100/ANSI terminal emulators—most map 0x0C to LF-equivalent vertical motion (unlike CSI 2 J, which erases the display buffer). Its clear-screen role comes from interactive line editors (Readline’s clear-screen widget, Zsh’s clear-screen, and less) binding ^L. In source code, \f counts as whitespace in C/POSIX isspace() and historically marked logical section breaks (e.g., GNU coding standards and Emacs forward-page), while in PostScript/PCL printers it still triggers an explicit media eject.
