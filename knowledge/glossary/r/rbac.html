Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with R / RBAC (Role-Based Access Control)

Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) is a security model that assigns permissions to named roles rather than directly to individual user accounts.

Instead of handing out keys to every room one by one to each new employee, a company defines job roles like “Receptionist” or “Facilities Manager” and attaches the right keys to each role. When someone joins or changes jobs, you just assign or swap their role. On a computer, RBAC works the same way to keep permissions tidy and prevent people from keeping old access they no longer need.

On Linux, supplementary groups like wheel (administrator privileges via sudo or pkexec), audio, or video act as a simple form of RBAC: adding user alex to wheel grants administrative rights without editing per-user policy rules. Larger systems such as SELinux and Kubernetes use full RBAC engines to separate duties and enforce least privilege.

Why is RBAC easier to audit than assigning permissions directly to each user?

Permissions are grouped into a small set of named roles, so auditing who can perform an action only requires checking which roles hold that permission and which users belong to those roles, rather than inspecting per-user exception lists.

Formalized in NIST INCITS 359 (Core, Hierarchical, and Static/Dynamic Separation of Duty), RBAC maps Users → Roles → Permissions via UA and PA relations. Role hierarchies allow permission inheritance, while Dynamic Separation of Duty (DSD) prevents activating mutually exclusive roles in the same session. Unlike Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC), pure RBAC struggles with context-dependent rules (time of day, resource ownership, client IP), leading to role explosion unless paired with ABAC or parameterized scopes.
