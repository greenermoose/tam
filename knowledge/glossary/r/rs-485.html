RS-485 (TIA-485)

RS-485 (TIA-485) is a differential multi-drop serial bus standard designed for long cable runs (up to 1200 m) and electrically noisy environments.

Imagine trying to hear a whisper across a noisy factory floor. Measuring a single wire against ground (as RS-232 does) picks up interference from motors and power lines. RS-485 instead sends the signal over two twisted wires in opposite directions: electrical noise hits both wires almost equally, so the receiver looks only at the difference between them and cancels the noise out.

Unlike point-to-point RS-232, RS-485 supports a daisy-chained multi-drop bus of dozens of devices (commonly 32 unit loads, or more with fractional-load transceivers), which is why industrial protocols like Modbus RTU and stage lighting (DMX512) rely on it. On Linux, RS-485 adapters appear as /dev/ttyS* or /dev/ttyUSB* devices and may use kernel RS-485 mode (TIOCSRS485) to toggle driver-enable in half-duplex setups. Place 120-ohm termination resistors at both physical ends of the bus—not in the middle—to absorb signal reflections.

Why should 120-ohm termination resistors be placed only at the two ends of an RS-485 bus rather than at every device?

Termination resistors match the twisted pair’s ~120-ohm characteristic impedance to prevent signals from bouncing back at the open cable ends. Adding termination at intermediate devices would overload the bus drivers and reduce signal amplitude.

TIA/EIA-485 specifies a balanced differential multipoint interface (A/B lines) with a ±200 mV receiver sensitivity threshold across a −7 V to +12 V common-mode voltage range, operating in two-wire half-duplex or four-wire full-duplex topologies. Reliable buses require 120-ohm end termination, fail-safe biasing resistors (pull-up/pull-down) so an idle or open bus does not float inside the ±200 mV indeterminate region and trigger false start bits, strict half-duplex turn-around timing after the UART shift register empties, and a common ground reference or galvanic isolation to stay within the common-mode limit.
