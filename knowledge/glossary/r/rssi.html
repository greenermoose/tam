RSSI (Received Signal Strength Indicator)

A measurement of the incoming radio signal power reaching a wireless receiver, typically expressed in dBm for Wi-Fi.

Think of RSSI like how loudly you can hear someone speaking across a room—the closer you are and the fewer walls between you, the stronger the signal. Your laptop or phone uses this reading to decide how many signal bars to show and whether it can keep a steady Wi-Fi or Bluetooth connection.

In Linux tools such as iw dev wlan0 link or nmcli device wifi list, Wi-Fi RSSI appears as a negative number in dBm: around −50 dBm is very strong, −65 dBm is solid for everyday work, and −80 dBm or lower is weak and prone to dropped packets or slower speeds. Unlike a percentage bar, dBm is logarithmic, so every 3 dBm drop halves the received power.

Why can a connection at −65 dBm still perform poorly in a crowded apartment building?

RSSI only measures total received signal power, not background radio interference. If neighbouring routers create a high noise floor, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) shrinks, forcing the radio to drop to slower modulation rates and retransmit packets.

RSSI is an unstandardized, vendor-specific relative indicator in IEEE 802.11 (where hardware maps arbitrary integer steps up to RSSI_Max), though Linux nl80211/mac80211 drivers normalize it into NL80211_BSS_SIGNAL_MBM (millibels-milliwatt) or dBm via cfg80211. Link quality depends on SNR (signal minus noise floor) and channel utilization rather than RSSI alone; furthermore, 802.11k/v/r roaming thresholds typically trigger around −70 to −75 dBm, while asymmetric transmit power between an access point and a mobile client means high downlink RSSI does not guarantee reliable uplink frames.
