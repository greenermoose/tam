RFID (Radio-Frequency Identification)

Wireless identification technology that uses electromagnetic fields to power and read tiny tags or smartcards.

RFID is what lets a transit card, building key fob, or library book tag work when you tap or hold it near a scanner. Most RFID tags have no battery at all: the scanner’s radio field gives the tiny chip just enough energy to wake up and beam back its ID number.

In everyday computing, the most familiar form of RFID is NFC (Near-Field Communication) at 13.56 MHz, used by contactless payment cards, smartphones, and tap-to-authenticate hardware security keys. Unlike long-range warehouse tags that can be read from several meters away, NFC limits range to a few centimeters so you have to intentionally tap the reader. On Linux, smartcard and NFC readers typically interact through pcscd or the kernel’s NFC subsystem.

How can a passive RFID transit card respond to a reader if the card contains no battery?

The card’s internal antenna harvests energy from the reader’s electromagnetic field via induction, powering its microchip long enough to modulate and reflect a signal back.

RFID spans distinct physical coupling mechanisms and frequency bands: Low Frequency (LF, 125–134 kHz, inductive coupling, short range, low data rate), High Frequency (HF, 13.56 MHz, inductive coupling governed by ISO/IEC 14443 and ISO/IEC 15693, including NFC), and Ultra-High Frequency (UHF, 860–960 MHz, radiative backscatter per EPCglobal Gen2 / ISO 18000-63, multi-meter range). Passive LF/HF tags communicate via load modulation of the carrier, whereas UHF tags vary antenna impedance to backscatter modulated waves. Security varies sharply by tier: legacy 125 kHz tags often broadcast static UIDs in the clear, while modern ISO 14443-4 smartcards support mutual cryptographic authentication and anti-collision framing.
