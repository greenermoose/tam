Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with R / RSS (Resident Set Size)

RSS (Resident Set Size) is the amount of physical RAM currently held by a process, excluding pages swapped out to disk.

Think of a program’s memory like a desk and a filing cabinet. The filing cabinet is swap space on your drive, while the desk is your fast physical RAM. RSS shows how much space a running program is actively taking up on the desk right now—if too many programs have huge RSS values at once, physical RAM fills up and the system slows down.

Reported by ps, top, and tam-meter, RSS includes shared libraries mapped into a process (such as libc). Because of that, summing the RSS column across all running processes double-counts shared memory and often exceeds your machine’s total physical RAM. When you want a fair per-process share that adds up cleanly, check PSS (Proportional Set Size) instead.

Why does adding up the RSS of every running process in ps or top often produce a total larger than your computer’s physical RAM?

Each process’s RSS counts the full size of any shared libraries or shared memory pages mapped into it. If 50 processes share a 10 MB library, RSS counts that 10 MB in every process rather than dividing it among them.

In the Linux kernel, RSS corresponds to VmRSS in /proc/<pid>/status (and the second field of /proc/<pid>/statm in pages), summing resident anonymous private pages (RssAnon), resident file-backed mappings (RssFile), and resident System V/POSIX shared memory (RssShmem). Because copy-on-write pages after fork() and shared page-cache mappings are attributed in full to each mapping process, RSS overstates unique memory footprint; /proc/<pid>/smaps_rollup exposes Pss and private clean/dirty counts (USS) for accurate OOM-pressure analysis.
