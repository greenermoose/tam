RLL (Run-Length Limited)

A line-coding technique that limits the shortest and longest runs of identical bits between signal transitions on a wire or storage medium.

When data travels over a cable or is written onto a magnetic disk, the receiver needs to see changes in the signal to keep its clock in step with the sender. If there were a thousand zeros in a row with no change, the receiver could lose count of how many zeros passed. RLL encoding guarantees that the signal flips often enough to stay synchronized, without flipping so fast that the hardware blurs the bits together.

Historical hard drives used codes like RLL(2,7) to pack more data onto magnetic platters while keeping flux reversals within physical head limits. In modern computing, serial interconnects such as PCIe, SATA, and Ethernet achieve the same goal using block codes (like 8b/10b) or scrambling plus small headers (like 64b/66b), ensuring enough transitions for clock recovery and DC balance on high-speed links.

Why do high-speed serial links like PCIe and Ethernet still need transition guarantees even when using differential signaling?

High-speed serial links usually embed the clock in the data stream rather than running a separate clock wire. Without frequent transitions, the receiver’s clock-data recovery (CDR) circuit drifts out of phase, and long runs of ones or zeros cause baseline wander across AC-coupling capacitors.

An RLL(d, k) code constrains every pair of consecutive 1 transitions in the channel bitstream to be separated by at least d and at most k zeros. The lower bound d caps the maximum transition frequency, reducing inter-symbol interference (ISI) on bandwidth-limited channels and magnetic media; the upper bound k guarantees a minimum transition density so phase-locked loops in clock-data recovery stay locked. Encoding m data bits into n channel bits imposes rate overhead (for example, 20% in 8b/10b vs. ~3% in 64b/66b), trading link efficiency against bounded running disparity and simpler framing.
