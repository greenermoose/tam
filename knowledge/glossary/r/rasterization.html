Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with R / Rasterization

Rasterization is the process of converting mathematical shapes, curves or models into a two-dimensional grid of pixels for display.

Imagine drawing a smooth circle on graph paper and coloring whole squares to match it. Screens cannot display infinitely smooth curves; they use tiny colored dots called pixels. Rasterization decides which dots to light and what shade each should have so fonts and icons look clear.

In text rendering, FreeType turns outline glyphs into bitmaps at a chosen size, applying hinting and antialiasing; the same idea appears when drawing vector icons or three-dimensional scenes. Because pixels are discrete squares, diagonals look stair-stepped unless edge pixels are blended with the background.

Why does zooming into a rasterized image look blocky while zooming into a vector graphic stays sharp?

A raster stores fixed squares that only enlarge; a vector stores curves that the renderer can rasterize again at the new size.

Font rasterizers compute coverage for each pixel from the outline, optionally snap stems to the grid, and blend coverage with the background using gamma-aware arithmetic. Subpixel color rendering treats the red, green and blue emitters as offset samples for sharper horizontal detail, at the cost of color fringes and sensitivity to panel layout. A rasterized bitmap is size-specific; vector source data can be rasterized again at a new size.
