Rasterization

Rasterization is the process of converting mathematical shapes, curves, or 3D models into a two-dimensional grid of pixels for display on a screen.

Imagine drawing a smooth circle on graph paper and then coloring in whole squares to match it. Screens cannot display infinitely smooth curves; they are grids of tiny colored dots called pixels. Rasterization decides which dots to light up and what color each one should be so that fonts, icons, and 3D game scenes look clear on your monitor.

In Linux desktops and 3D graphics, rasterization happens both in software (such as FreeType turning vector font outlines into glyph bitmaps at your chosen DPI) and on the GPU (converting 3D triangle meshes into pixels). Because pixels are discrete squares, diagonal edges can look stair-stepped (“aliasing”) unless anti-aliasing blends edge pixels with the background.

Why does zooming into a rasterized image make edges look blocky, whereas zooming into a vector graphic stays sharp?

Once a shape is rasterized into a fixed pixel grid, zooming in only enlarges those existing squares. A vector graphic stores mathematical curves instead of pixels, so the renderer can re-rasterize it at the new size using more pixels for a smooth edge.

Hardware GPU rasterizers evaluate triangle edge equations across tile or 2×2 quad bounding boxes using top-left fill rules so shared edges neither double-draw nor leave hairline cracks. Perspective-correct attribute interpolation divides vertex attributes by clip-space w before linear screen-space interpolation and multiplies back by interpolated 1/w. Subpixel font rasterization (LCD RGB subpixel rendering) triples horizontal resolution by treating R, G, and B emitters as offset samples, requiring color-fringe filtering and breaking under rotated displays or non-standard subpixel layouts.
