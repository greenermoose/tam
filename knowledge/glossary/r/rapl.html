RAPL (Running Average Power Limit)

Running Average Power Limit—a hardware energy-monitoring and power-capping interface in Intel and AMD processors, exposed on Linux through the powercap subsystem.

Modern processors include a built-in power meter that tracks how much electricity the chip uses and lets the system set a wattage limit so a laptop stays cool and saves battery. On Linux, RAPL is the interface that reads those energy numbers and enforces those power limits.

Linux exposes RAPL domains (such as package-0, core, and dram) under /sys/class/powercap/intel-rapl/ as cumulative microjoule counters (energy_uj). Dividing the difference between two readings by elapsed time gives average watts, which tools like tam-meter use since energy_uj is restricted from direct unprivileged reads.

Why did Linux restrict unprivileged read access to RAPL energy_uj files?

Fine-grained power measurements can act as a side channel (the PLATYPUS attack) that leaks cryptographic keys or secret data based on instruction-dependent power draw, so access is restricted to privileged readers.

RAPL relies on model-specific registers (MSRs) updated roughly every ~1 ms via an on-die energy model scaled by MSR_RAPL_POWER_UNIT. Its 32-bit energy counters wrap under sustained load, requiring periodic sampling to avoid missed overflows; on the control side, PL1 (sustained TDP) and PL2 (short-term turbo ceiling with time window Tau) govern power capping alongside firmware and DPTF overrides.
