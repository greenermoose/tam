Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with R / RGB - Red, Green, Blue

An additive color model that creates colors by combining red, green, and blue light.

Computer monitors, phone screens, and TVs make millions of colors using only three basic lights: red, green, and blue. Mixing all three at full brightness makes white, turning them all off makes black, and adjusting their brightness creates every shade in between. This is the opposite of mixing paint on paper, where adding more colors makes things darker.

In digital graphics and CSS, RGB is commonly stored as 8 bits per channel (values from 0 to 255, or hex codes like #FF8000), giving 24-bit “true color,” often paired with an alpha transparency channel (RGBA). Because different monitors produce slightly different reds, greens, and blues, an RGB triple without a color profile (such as sRGB or Display P3) can look oversaturated or shifted on wide-gamut displays unless color management is active.

Why can the exact same RGB value like (255, 0, 0) look different on two monitors?

RGB values are device-dependent unless tied to a defined color space like sRGB; a wide-gamut monitor’s physical red primary is more saturated than a standard sRGB monitor’s red unless color management translates it.

RGB is a family of additive, trichromatic color spaces rather than a single absolute space; a concrete RGB space (such as sRGB, Display P3, or Rec. 2020) is defined by the CIE xy chromaticity coordinates of its R, G, and B primaries, its white point (commonly the 6504 K CIE daylight illuminant), and its Electro-Optical Transfer Function (EOTF/gamma curve). Converting between RGB spaces or to device-independent CIE XYZ uses a 3×3 linear matrix after inverting the non-linear transfer function, and high-dynamic-range (HDR) pipelines extend beyond 8-bit integer SDR to 10-bit or half-float linear representations using PQ (ST 2084) or HLG transfer functions.
