Rounding

Rounding replaces a value with the closest one a format can actually store.

If a ruler has only millimetre marks, a length halfway between two marks must be recorded as one of them. That choice is rounding. It happens constantly in computer arithmetic.

Common rules are round-to-nearest, round toward zero, round upward and round downward. Ordinary decimal rounding often sends a halfway value away from zero; computing systems commonly use round-to-nearest with ties-to-even, which sends a halfway value to the representation with an even last digit. Floating-point multiplication, division and conversions all round; a single operation can hide a small error that grows over many steps.

Why prefer ties-to-even over always rounding halfway up?

Always rounding up biases long sums upward. Ties-to-even chooses one direction or the other, so errors tend to cancel over many operations.

IEEE 754 defines four rounding-direction attributes: roundTiesToEven, roundTowardZero, roundTowardPositive and roundTowardNegative, plus an inexact exception flag. Correct rounding means the result is the representable value nearest according to the active rule, as if computed with unlimited precision. Quantization is the same idea in signal conversion; dither deliberately adds noise to make the error less structured. Rounding at intermediate steps, not just at the end, is the source of most numerical drift.
