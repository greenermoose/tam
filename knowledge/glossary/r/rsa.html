RSA

RSA is a foundational public-key cryptosystem whose security rests on the difficulty of factoring the product of two large prime numbers.

RSA works like a padlock with two different keys: a public key you can share with anyone to lock a message (or verify your signature), and a private key only you keep to unlock it (or create a signature). It relies on a one-way math trick: multiplying two huge prime numbers together is easy, but starting from the result and finding the original two primes takes ordinary computers an impossibly long time.

Named after Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman (1977), RSA is used for digital signatures (TLS certificates, SSH keys, software signing) and key transport. In practice, RSA must never be used “raw”: it requires safe padding—RSA-OAEP for encryption or RSA-PSS for signatures—and a large key (at least 2048 bits, or 3072 bits for 128-bit classical security). Modern systems increasingly prefer elliptic-curve algorithms such as Ed25519 and X25519 for smaller keys, faster speed, and fewer padding pitfalls.

Why is textbook “raw” RSA (computing m^e mod N directly without padding) never used in real-world cryptography?

Raw RSA is deterministic and multiplicatively homomorphic—multiplying two ciphertexts yields the encryption of the product of their plaintexts—and small messages or small exponents can be broken algebraically without factoring N. Randomized padding (OAEP or PSS) is essential for security.

RSA defines a trapdoor permutation f(x) = x^e mod N where N = pq for large primes p, q and ed ≡ 1 (mod λ(N)) (commonly e = 65537). Private-key operations typically use the Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT) mod p and mod q for a ~4x speedup, which requires post-computation verification to defeat Bellcore fault-injection attacks that otherwise recover p or q from a single corrupted CRT output. Deployments must also guard against Bleichenbacher padding oracles (in PKCS#1 v1.5), timing/cache side channels during modular exponentiation, and eventual breaks by Shor’s algorithm on fault-tolerant quantum computers.
