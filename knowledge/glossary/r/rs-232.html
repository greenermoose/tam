Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with R / RS-232 (Recommended Standard 232)

RS-232 (Recommended Standard 232) is a classic wired serial communication standard used for device consoles, modems, and industrial or laboratory equipment.

Before USB existed, computers talked to modems, printers, and switches one bit at a time over a serial cable—often using a 9-pin D-shaped plug. Even today, network switches, servers, and embedded boards include RS-232 serial ports because they are simple, reliable, and work before graphics or networking drivers even load.

On Linux, RS-232 ports appear as device nodes such as /dev/ttyS0 (built-in UARTs) or /dev/ttyUSB0 (USB-to-serial adapters), accessed with terminal tools like picocom or minicom at matching framing settings (for example, 115200 8N1). Unlike 3.3 V or 5 V TTL UART pins on microcontrollers, RS-232 uses inverted ±3 V to ±15 V signals, so wiring them directly without a level-shifting transceiver chip damages 3.3 V UARTs.

Why does connecting an RS-232 port directly to a microcontroller’s 3.3 V UART pins fail—and risk damaging the board?

RS-232 encodes bits using inverted voltages from −15 V to +15 V, whereas a 3.3 V TTL UART expects 0 V to 3.3 V and cannot tolerate negative or high positive voltages; a transceiver chip is needed to invert and shift the levels.

Standardized as EIA/TIA-232, it is an unbalanced, single-ended, point-to-point asynchronous interface referenced to a shared signal ground (GND). Logic 1 (mark) is −3 V to −15 V and logic 0 (space) is +3 V to +15 V, leaving −3 V to +3 V as an undefined transition band, typically over DE-9 connectors (TXD, RXD, plus optional RTS/CTS and DTR/DSR flow control). Because it is single-ended, total cable capacitance (historically ~2500 pF, roughly 15 m at 19.2 kbps and shorter at 115.2 kbps) limits reach, and ground potential differences between separately powered chassis can corrupt frames or damage line drivers.
