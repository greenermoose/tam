Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with R / RAID - Redundant Array of Independent Disks

Redundant Array of Independent Disks—a method of combining multiple physical storage drives into a single logical unit for higher speed, larger capacity, or fault tolerance.

Instead of keeping everything on one drive that could fail at any moment, RAID groups two or more drives together so they act like one. Depending on the setup, your computer can copy data onto both drives for safety (mirroring) or split files across drives to read and write faster (striping).

On Linux, software RAID is commonly managed with mdadm (mdraid) or built into filesystems like ZFS and Btrfs. Common layouts include RAID 0 (striping for speed, zero fault tolerance), RAID 1 (mirroring, 50% usable capacity), and RAID 5/6 (single or double parity); crucially, RAID protects against drive failure, not accidental deletion or corruption.

Why is RAID 1 not a substitute for a backup?

RAID 1 mirrors every write immediately to all disks in the array, so if you accidentally delete a file, encrypt it with ransomware, or corrupt the filesystem, that damage is mirrored across every drive at once.

Storage virtualization via Linux md, LVM-RAID, ZFS RAID-Z, or hardware controllers trades write amplification, rebuild time, and IOPS against redundancy. Classic parity arrays (RAID 5/6) incur a read-modify-write penalty on sub-stripe writes and suffer from the “write hole” on power loss unless closed by a journal/bitmap or copy-on-write semantics; long RAID 5 rebuilds on multi-terabyte HDDs also risk an unrecoverable read error on a surviving member.
