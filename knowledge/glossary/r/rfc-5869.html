RFC 5869 (HKDF)

The IETF specification for HKDF, a standard method for turning a shared secret into multiple independent encryption keys.

When two devices agree on a secret over the internet, they often need several different keys—one to encrypt messages, another to check that messages were not tampered with, and others for the return direction. Reusing the exact same key for every job is unsafe. RFC 5869 defines a recipe called HKDF that safely stretches and splits one starting secret into separate, independent keys.

HKDF works in two phases built on HMAC: Extract concentrates uneven randomness from an input secret (such as a Diffie–Hellman shared secret) into a fixed-length pseudorandom key, and Expand derives one or more application-specific keys tagged with context labels. Protocols like TLS 1.3 and Noise use HKDF so that compromising one derived key or changing a context label does not reveal the other keys.

Why does HKDF separate key derivation into Extract and Expand steps instead of hashing the secret directly?

Raw shared secrets (like Diffie–Hellman outputs) may not be uniformly random, and different roles need domain-separated keys; Extract produces a uniform pseudorandom key, and Expand safely derives multiple independent keys with distinct context labels.

IETF RFC 5869 (Krawczyk and Eronen, 2010) formalizes HKDF-Extract(salt, IKM) → PRK as HMAC-Hash(salt, IKM) (defaulting salt to HashLen zero bytes if omitted) and HKDF-Expand(PRK, info, L) → OKM by chaining T(i) = HMAC-Hash(PRK, T(i-1) || info || 0x0i) for L ≤ 255 × HashLen. Separating the two stages allows protocols such as TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446) to reuse a uniform PRK directly with Expand while binding handshake transcripts into info for domain separation, avoiding length-extension and related-key weaknesses of ad-hoc hash constructions.
