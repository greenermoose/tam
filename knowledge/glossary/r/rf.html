Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with R / RF - Radio Frequency

Radio-frequency electromagnetic signals used to carry information wirelessly through space.

RF stands for radio frequency—the invisible waves that carry signals between devices without cables. Wi-Fi, Bluetooth headphones, cellular phones, and car key fobs all use RF signals to send data through the air. Different technologies use different slices of the radio spectrum so they do not constantly talk over one another.

RF generally spans alternating electromagnetic signals from roughly 3 kHz to 300 GHz. In everyday computing, Wi-Fi (2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, and 6 GHz), Bluetooth (2.4 GHz), and NFC (13.56 MHz) share unlicensed bands, which is why crowded 2.4 GHz spaces often suffer interference from neighboring routers, Bluetooth devices, or microwave ovens. On Linux, tools like rfkill can soft-block or check hardware switches on RF transmitters.

Why does 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi often slow down in crowded apartments even when your signal bars look strong?

Because 2.4 GHz is a narrow, shared unlicensed band with only a few non-overlapping channels, nearby Wi-Fi networks and Bluetooth devices compete for the same airtime, causing collisions and retransmissions.

RF engineering models electromagnetic propagation and circuit behavior across frequency, wavelength, path loss, modulation, and transmission-line impedance matching (typically 50 Ω in wireless front ends to minimize reflections and VSWR). Higher carrier frequencies support wider channels and higher throughput (e.g., 160 MHz or 320 MHz channels in 5/6 GHz Wi-Fi) at the cost of higher free-space path loss and poorer wall penetration. Linux’s rfkill subsystem and cfg80211/nl80211 stack enforce regulatory domain rules (TX power limits, DFS radar detection, and channel availability) set in CRDA/regulatory.db.
