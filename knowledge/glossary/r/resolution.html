Resolution

The number of pixels across the width and height of an image or display.

Every digital picture and screen is made of a grid of tiny colored dots called pixels. Resolution tells you how many dots wide and how many dots tall that grid is, such as 1920 by 1080. More pixels give sharper detail, while fewer pixels can make text and edges look blocky—though how big things look on your screen also depends on the screen’s physical size and zoom settings.

In display settings, resolution is written as width × height (for example, 2560×1440 or 3840×2160). On modern high-density (“HiDPI” or 4K) laptop screens, running at native resolution without display scaling makes text tiny, whereas lowering the resolution below native forces the panel to interpolate and blurs text. Keeping native resolution and adjusting the scale factor (such as 150% or 200%) keeps fonts crisp while making UI elements comfortable to read.

Why does lowering a monitor’s resolution below its native setting usually make text look blurry?

Flat-panel displays have a fixed physical grid of pixels; sending a lower resolution forces the monitor or GPU to stretch and interpolate pixels across non-aligned physical dots instead of mapping each pixel 1:1.

Resolution describes logical or physical raster dimensions in pixels, which is distinct from pixel density (PPI/DPI) and display timing. On a link such as DisplayPort or HDMI, the active pixel count plus blanking intervals, refresh rate, and color depth determines the required pixel clock and link bandwidth (potentially requiring Display Stream Compression at high modes). Under Wayland and X11, fractional scaling trades sharpness or GPU work for layout size: Wayland compositors commonly render clients at the next integer scale (e.g., 2×) and downsample to the physical framebuffer, or use wp_fractional_scale_v1 to let toolkits rasterize directly at fractional sizes, while XWayland clients that lack scaling support may be upscaled by the compositor.
