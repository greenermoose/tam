Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with R / ROB (Reorder Buffer)

A CPU hardware buffer that lets instructions execute out of order for speed while committing their final results in exact program order.

Imagine a restaurant kitchen where dishes are cooked in whatever order is fastest—a salad comes out before a slow roast—but a server lines them up at the counter so Table 1 always gets its courses in the right sequence. A Reorder Buffer does the same thing inside a processor: it lets fast calculations finish early while holding their results until earlier instructions are done.

Modern CPUs use out-of-order execution so a slow memory load does not stall independent math instructions behind it. The ROB tracks every in-flight instruction from dispatch to retirement. If a speculative branch turns out to be wrong, or an instruction triggers a page fault, the CPU discards everything in the ROB after that point without corrupting visible registers or memory.

What happens to instructions sitting in the ROB after a mispredicted branch?

They are squashed (flushed) before retirement. Because their results have not yet been committed to architectural registers or memory, the CPU cleanly recovers the exact state at the branch.

Allocated in program order at rename/dispatch and retired FIFO from its head, the ROB is the central structure enforcing precise exceptions and in-order architectural state commitment on out-of-order microarchitectures. Each ROB entry records completion status, exception flags, and physical-to-architectural register mappings (or pointers to a separate physical register file and store buffer) so speculative stores never reach cache until retirement. ROB capacity—hundreds of micro-ops on modern cores—directly bounds the instruction window available to extract instruction-level parallelism (ILP) across long-latency LLC misses, while branch mispredictions or memory-ordering violations trigger partial or full ROB rollbacks.
