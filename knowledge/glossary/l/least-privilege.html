Least privilege

Giving every user, program, or process only the minimum rights needed for its job.

A hotel housekeeper’s keycard opens guest rooms on their floor during their shift, not the hotel’s bank vault—so a lost keycard does minimal damage. On a computer, least privilege means your browser or music player should not hold the keys to your whole system, so a bug in one app cannot wipe your files or take over the machine.

In daily Linux use, least privilege shows up when you work as an unprivileged user (such as alex) instead of root, split root powers into narrow Linux capabilities (like CAP_NET_BIND_SERVICE for binding port 80), and confine services with systemd sandboxing (ProtectSystem=strict, NoNewPrivileges=yes), namespaces, or seccomp filters.

Why is granting a web server only CAP_NET_BIND_SERVICE safer than running the whole server as root?

Binding to port 80 or 443 only requires permission to open privileged network ports. Granting CAP_NET_BIND_SERVICE alone means a remote bug in the server cannot modify system binaries, read other users’ private files, or load kernel modules.

Formulated by Saltzer and Schroeder (1975) to bound blast radius and eliminate ambient authority—where POSIX discretionary access control otherwise lets any process inherit its caller’s full UID/GID rights. Modern Linux enforces finer boundaries via PR_SET_NO_NEW_PRIVS across execve, capability bounding sets, LSMs (SELinux, AppArmor, Landlock), and privilege-separated architectures (such as OpenSSH’s sandboxed pre-auth child communicating with a minimal monitor); common failure modes include confused-deputy bugs, setuid helpers, and overly broad capabilities like CAP_SYS_ADMIN that remain effectively root-equivalent.
