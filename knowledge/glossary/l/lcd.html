LCD - Liquid-Crystal Display

A flat display technology that forms images by using liquid crystals to control how much light passes through each pixel.

Unlike an OLED phone screen where every tiny dot makes its own light, an LCD works like microscopic Venetian blinds in front of a bright lamp. A white backlight shines from behind, and millions of tiny liquid-crystal shutters twist open or closed to let the right amount of red, green, and blue light through for each pixel.

LCD panels come in types such as IPS (wide viewing angles, accurate color), VA (higher contrast), and TN (fast response, narrow angles). On Linux laptops, panel brightness is typically controlled through /sys/class/backlight/, and dark scenes may look slightly gray because a shared backlight leaks through closed pixels unless local dimming is used.

Why does displaying an all-black screen on a standard LCD still consume noticeable backlight power and glow slightly gray?

A standard LCD relies on a shared backlight that stays lit behind the entire panel, and liquid-crystal shutters cannot block 100% of that light. Unless the panel uses local dimming zones, black pixels still leak some backlight.

An LCD stacks an LED backlight (edge-lit or full-array mini-LED) and diffuser films behind crossed linear polarizers sandwiching a thin-film transistor (TFT) active matrix (a-Si, IGZO, or LTPS), a nematic liquid-crystal layer (TN, IPS, or VA), and an RGB color filter array. Pixel drive relies on AC voltage inversion (frame, line, or dot inversion) to prevent DC ionic image sticking, asymmetric gray-to-gray response with voltage overdrive (which can cause inverse ghosting), and predictable RGB subpixel stripes that font renderers such as FreeType exploit for subpixel hinting.
