Lattice

A regular grid of points in many dimensions used to build quantum-safe cryptography.

A lattice is like a repeating 3D crystal grid, except extended to hundreds of dimensions. If you know the secret, clean directions that step from one grid point to the next, finding the nearest point is easy; without that secret map, you only have skewed directions, and finding the closest point becomes hopelessly slow for any computer.

Structured lattice problems such as Learning With Errors (LWE) underpin NIST’s primary post-quantum standards ML-KEM (key encapsulation) and ML-DSA (digital signatures). In practice, lattice schemes run very fast on modern CPUs, though their public keys and signatures are larger—hundreds to a few thousand bytes—than elliptic-curve keys.

Why are cryptographers migrating from RSA and elliptic curves to lattice-based schemes like ML-KEM?

Shor’s algorithm on a large quantum computer can efficiently factor integers and solve discrete logarithms, breaking RSA and ECC. No efficient quantum algorithm is known for solving the underlying high-dimensional lattice problems, making them a strong foundation for post-quantum security.

A discrete additive subgroup of R^n generated by integer linear combinations of a basis B = [b_1, ..., b_k]. Worst-case lattice problems (GapSVP, SIVP) reduce to average-case LWE and Module-LWE over polynomial rings such as Z_q[X]/(X^256 + 1), which power ML-KEM (FIPS 203) and ML-DSA (FIPS 204) using Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) multiplication. Key engineering nuances include balancing decryption failure probability against noise margins, preventing side-channel leaks during binomial/rejection sampling, and sizing parameters against BKZ lattice-reduction attacks.
