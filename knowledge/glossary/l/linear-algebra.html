Linear algebra

Linear algebra is the study of lists of numbers and the simple ways they can be combined.

A recipe with several ingredient amounts can be treated as a list of numbers. Doubling the recipe doubles every amount. That kind of scaling and adding is the heart of linear algebra.

The main objects are vectors, which are lists, and matrices, which are grids. A linear combination multiplies vectors by numbers and adds the results. A dot product multiplies matching entries and adds them. These few operations describe rotations in graphics, mixtures of colours, lines through data and systems of equations.

Why is a matrix multiplication a list of dot products?

Each entry in the result comes from one row of the left matrix and one column of the right matrix. Multiplying matching entries and adding them is exactly a dot product.

Linear algebra works in vector spaces over a field and studies linear maps, which preserve addition and scalar multiplication. Matrices represent those maps once bases are chosen. Rank, null space, determinants, eigenvalues and condition number describe what a map does and how sensitive its inverse is. Floating-point implementations must consider numeric stability as well as the algebraic result; see LAPACK and BLAS.
