Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with L / LR (Long Reach / Link Register)

In networking, LR (Long Reach) is a fiber-optic standard for links up to 10 km; in ARM processors, LR is the Link Register that remembers where a function call should return.

The two letters LR mean two completely different things depending on whether you are looking at cables or computer chips. On a network switch or fiber transceiver, Long Reach means the module can send light pulses across miles of thin glass fiber—between buildings or across a campus—rather than just across a server rack. Inside an ARM processor (like the chip in a phone or Raspberry Pi), the Link Register works like a bookmark: when a program jumps to a helper routine, the chip jots down the return page number in LR so it knows where to continue afterward.

In Ethernet hardware, 10GBASE-LR modules use a 1310 nm laser over single-mode fiber (OS2) for spans up to 10 km, contrasting with 850 nm SR (Short Reach) modules that run over thicker multimode fiber for a few hundred meters. You can inspect transceiver type and optical power on Linux with ethtool -m eth0. In ARM assembly, calling a subroutine with BL (Branch with Link) saves the return address in LR (R14 on 32-bit ARM, X30 on AArch64) instead of pushing it straight onto the stack.

Why does plugging a 10GBASE-LR transceiver into a multimode fiber patch cable often fail or perform poorly?

LR uses a 1310 nm laser designed for the narrow 9 µm core of single-mode fiber. Multimode fiber has a much wider 50 µm or 62.5 µm core that causes severe modal dispersion and differential mode delay at 1310 nm unless a special mode-conditioning patch cord is used, and mismatches in power budget or connector polish (UPC vs. APC) can also drop the link.

IEEE 802.3 Clause 52 specifies 10GBASE-LR at 10.3125 GBd (64B/66B encoding) using a 1310 nm uncooled DFB laser over OS2 single-mode fiber with a 6.2 dB channel insertion-loss budget and receiver overload protection around +0.5 dBm (avoiding short patch cords without attenuation if high-power optics are paired). In ARM AArch64, X30 holds the return address on BL/BLR and is consumed by RET; non-leaf functions must spill X30 to the stack frame before issuing nested calls, and Pointer Authentication (PAC) signs LR with keys such as PACIASP/AUTIASP to mitigate ROP gadget chaining.
