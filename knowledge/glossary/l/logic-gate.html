Logic gate

A fundamental digital circuit that takes one or more binary inputs and produces a single binary output according to a logical rule.

Computers think using tiny electrical switches that are either off (0) or on (1). A logic gate is a simple building block made from those switches that follows a basic rule—like an AND gate that only turns on when both inputs are on, or a NOT gate that flips on to off. Millions or billions of these tiny decision-makers wired together are what let a processor add numbers and run programs.

Every combinational operation in a CPU—comparing two numbers, selecting a value with a multiplexer, or adding bits—is built from basic gates: AND, OR, NOT, NAND, NOR, and XOR. Notably, NAND and NOR are universal gates because you can wire copies of either one alone to build every other logic gate, which is why chip manufacturers often optimize their physical cells around NAND and NOR.

Why are NAND and NOR called “universal” logic gates in digital hardware design?

Any Boolean function—including NOT, AND, OR, and XOR—can be constructed solely by combining NAND gates (or solely by combining NOR gates). Because a single gate type suffices for all logic, chip designers can standardize physical transistor layouts around them.

In CMOS integrated circuits, logic gates are realized as complementary pull-up (PMOS) and pull-down (NMOS) transistor networks implementing Boolean functions, where NAND and NOR naturally invert and therefore require fewer transistors than non-inverting AND/OR gates. Real gates are characterized not only by their truth tables but by static noise margins, fan-out capacitance, dynamic switching power (proportional to C V^2 f), and propagation delay (t_pd). Timing closure in synchronous digital design requires the longest combinational path of gates between flip-flops—plus setup time and clock skew—to stay strictly below the clock period.
