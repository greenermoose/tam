Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with L / Latency

The time delay between initiating an action or request and receiving its first response.

Imagine ordering food at a drive-through versus hauling boxes in a truck. Latency is how long it takes from the moment you ask for something until the first bit of it reaches you—like waiting for a reply to a text message. Even if a connection can move huge files once it gets going, high latency still makes clicking links, typing remotely, or playing online games feel sluggish.

Latency is distinct from bandwidth: a wide pipe can move lots of data per second yet still take a long time for the first packet to arrive. On Linux, you measure network round-trip latency with ping, while storage and audio also have latency (such as NVMe vs. spinning-disk seek time, or kernel scheduling delay).

If you upgrade from a 100 Mbps to a 1 Gbps internet plan, why might video calls or SSH typing still feel just as laggy?

Upgrading bandwidth widens the pipe for bulk data, but it does not shorten the physical distance or routing hops to the server. Interactive tasks like SSH keystrokes send tiny packets governed by round-trip latency rather than peak throughput.

End-to-end latency is the sum of propagation delay (distance divided by signal speed), serialization delay (packet size divided by link rate), queuing delay (governed by Little’s Law and bufferbloat), and processing/interrupt/context-switch delay. Under load, queuing and head-of-line blocking—from deep router buffers, TCP in-order delivery, Wi-Fi contention, or scheduler runqueue waits—dominate tail latency (p99/p99.9) far more than median latency; mitigations include FQ-CoDel/CAKE AQM, TCP_NODELAY, io_uring or busy-polling, and PREEMPT_RT threaded IRQs.
