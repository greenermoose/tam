Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with L / Luminance

Luminance is the measured brightness of light emitted from or reflected off a surface, such as a computer monitor or a sheet of paper.

When you turn a laptop screen’s backlight up or down, you are changing its luminance—how much light reaches your eyes from each patch of the screen. It is different from the total light a lamp sends into a room: a tiny phone screen and a huge TV can have the exact same luminance if every square inch of both glows equally brightly.

Display specifications express peak luminance in candelas per square meter (cd/m²), often nicknamed "nits"—for example, 300 cd/m² for a typical office monitor or 1,000+ cd/m² for HDR highlights. In color management and CSS/desktop theming, relative luminance (Y in sRGB, scaled from 0 for black to 1 for white) is used to compute contrast ratios for readable text, because human eyes perceive green as much brighter than blue at the same physical power.

Why does pure green (#00FF00) have much higher relative luminance than pure blue (#0000FF) on an sRGB monitor?

Luminance is weighted by the human eye’s photopic sensitivity curve, which peaks in the green-yellow wavelengths and is far less sensitive to deep blue. In sRGB, green contributes roughly 71.5% of white’s luminance, red about 21.3%, and blue only about 7.2%.

Photometrically, luminance L_v = d²Φ_v / (dA cosθ dΩ) is radiance weighted by the CIE 1931 photopic luminosity function V(λ) and measured in cd/m². In display pipelines, sRGB relative luminance Y is computed only after linearizing gamma-encoded R′G′B′ values (applying the piecewise EOTF, roughly x^2.2) and taking the dot product 0.2126 R + 0.7152 G + 0.0722 B; HDR formats such as PQ (SMPTE ST 2084) encode absolute luminance up to 10,000 cd/m² directly in the transfer function rather than relative to display white.
