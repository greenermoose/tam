Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with L / Lock screen

A security barrier that hides an active desktop session and blocks input until the user re-authenticates.

When you step away from your computer, you do not want to close all your open apps just to keep others out. A lock screen covers your workspace and pauses access until you type your password or tap a security key, letting you pick up right where you left off when you return.

Locking the screen does not log you out: background downloads, terminal sessions, and open windows keep running while input is blocked. On Linux, idle timers or a shortcut like Meta + L trigger a session locker, and you can also check session lock state with loginctl. Keep in mind that a screen lock protects the graphical session, not physical disk access if the system is powered off without full-disk encryption.

What is the difference between locking the screen and logging out of a Linux desktop session?

Locking the screen keeps your user session, running programs, and open windows alive behind an authentication barrier, whereas logging out terminates the graphical session and closes its processes. Locking protects an unattended desk without losing your work, though it relies on the OS staying running and secure.

A Linux lock screen is implemented as a privileged or compositor-integrated session-lock client that authenticates via PAM (Pluggable Authentication Modules) while ensuring no unprivileged window can steal focus or inject events. Under X11, lockers historically relied on XGrabKeyboard and XGrabPointer, which are brittle against grab-stealing or DPMS/compositor races; under Wayland, protocols such as ext-session-lock-v1 give the compositor explicit authority to blank outputs, render the lock surface, and withhold input from all other clients until unlocked. System-level managers like systemd-logind coordinate lock/unlock signals across sleep-resume hooks (PrepareForSleep) so the session is locked before suspend completes.
