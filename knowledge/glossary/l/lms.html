LMS (Leighton-Micali Signature)

The Leighton–Micali Signature (LMS) scheme, a stateful hash-based digital signature standard designed to remain secure against quantum computers.

Digital signatures prove that a software or firmware update really came from its author and was not tampered with. Most older signatures rely on math problems that a future quantum computer could break, whereas LMS builds its security purely out of one-way cryptographic hashes—like locking a message inside a tree of tamper-evident seals. Because it is simple and fast to check, it is often used to verify device firmware at startup.

LMS is standardized in RFC 8554 and NIST SP 800-208, and is commonly paired with HSS (Hierarchical Signature System) to scale key lifetimes across multiple levels of trees. Unlike stateless schemes such as ML-DSA or SLH-DSA, LMS is stateful: the signer must strictly track which one-time keys have already been used, because reusing a leaf key compromises security. That makes LMS ideal for controlled signing environments like firmware release servers, rather than general-purpose laptops where VM snapshots or backups might roll back state.

Why is LMS well suited for signing firmware updates but risky to use on a cloned or snapshot-restored machine?

LMS uses one-time signature keys arranged in a Merkle tree, so the signer must never reuse a leaf index. Firmware signing happens in tightly controlled environments where state can be tracked reliably, whereas restoring a VM snapshot or cloning a disk could roll back the index counter and reuse a one-time key, breaking security.

Specified in RFC 8554 and approved in NIST SP 800-208, LMS combines Winternitz-style Leighton–Micali One-Time Signatures (LM-OTS) with a binary Merkle hash tree of height h, and extends to multi-tree hierarchies via HSS so a single root key can authorize up to 2^(h1 + h2 + ...) signatures without generating one massive tree upfront. Verification requires only standard hash evaluations (such as SHA-256 or SHAKE256) and produces fast, compact verification paths compared to XMSS, which omits bitmask-style randomization tweaks in LMS’s simpler domain-separated hashing. The critical operational constraint is monotonic state persistence: any duplicate use of an LM-OTS leaf index q reveals enough preimage chains to enable signature forgery.
