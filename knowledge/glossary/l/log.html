Log

A chronological record of events, status messages, and errors written by the operating system or an application.

When a program runs, it often keeps a running diary of what it did—starting up, finishing a task, or running into a problem. If something suddenly stops working, looking at that log is like checking the flight recorder on an airplane: it gives you clues about what happened right before the trouble started.

On modern Linux systems, systemd-journald collects kernel messages (dmesg), early boot output, and service stdout/stderr into a central binary journal queried with journalctl (for example, journalctl -u sshd or journalctl -p err -b), while some programs also write plain-text files under /var/log/. Log rotation (such as logrotate or journal size limits) prevents old logs from filling the disk.

Why does checking journalctl often give a clearer picture of a service failure than just reading the last error printed in a terminal?

journalctl combines timestamps, kernel messages, and service standard output/error in chronological order across the boot, letting you see preceding warnings or dependency failures—not just the final symptom—and filter by unit, priority, or boot ID.

System and application logs encode structured or unstructured event records carrying timestamps, syslog facility and severity levels (emerg through debug), and process metadata such as PID, UID, unit name, and boot ID. In Linux, kernel ring-buffer events (/dev/kmsg), syslog sockets, and service stdio streams converge in systemd-journald, which indexes key-value fields (_SYSTEMD_UNIT, PRIORITY) for fast queries while supporting forward-secure sealing and rate limiting to resist log-flooding DoS. High-volume services often emit JSON-structured logs or OpenTelemetry records so central collectors can parse fields without brittle regexes.
