LUKS2 (Linux Unified Key Setup version 2)

LUKS2 is the modern version of Linux’s standard disk-encryption format, adding stronger passphrase protection, redundant headers, and hardware-token support.

Older disk-encryption headers could be damaged by a sudden power loss while changing a password, and they did not easily work with modern security chips or USB keys. LUKS2 upgrades the lockbox at the front of your encrypted drive: it keeps a backup copy of the header in case of a crash, makes passphrases much harder for fast cracking rigs to guess, and lets you unlock the drive with a built-in TPM chip or a FIDO2 security key.

In modern distributions, cryptsetup luksFormat defaults to LUKS2 instead of LUKS1. Compared to LUKS1, LUKS2 supports up to 32 key slots (up from 8), stores extensible JSON metadata, and uses Argon2id by default so brute-forcing a passphrase requires significant RAM as well as CPU time. It also integrates with systemd-cryptenroll to bind unlock slots to a TPM2 chip or FIDO2 token, though some older bootloaders like GRUB have historically needed --pbkdf pbkdf2 for an encrypted /boot.

Why might you still need to specify PBKDF2 when formatting an encrypted /boot partition with LUKS2?

Argon2id is deliberately memory-hard (often requiring hundreds of megabytes of RAM), which early pre-boot environments such as GRUB may not have available or may not yet support in their minimal crypto code. Using PBKDF2 for the boot partition’s slot lets the bootloader unlock it while the root filesystem can still use Argon2id.

On disk, LUKS2 places dual redundant 16 KiB binary+JSON header areas (primary and secondary, each checksummed with SHA-256) followed by the binary keyslots area, allowing atomic metadata updates and recovery from partial writes. Metadata describes segments, digests, and up to 32 keyslots, plus token objects (e.g., systemd-tpm2, clevis) and online re-encryption state. LUKS2 also pairs with dm-integrity to provide authenticated encryption (AEAD, such as AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305 with HMAC) per sector, detecting offline ciphertext tampering at the cost of extra metadata blocks and write amplification.
