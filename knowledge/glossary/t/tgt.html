TGT (Ticket-Granting Ticket)

An initial Kerberos credential that lets a user request access tickets for individual network services without re-entering a password each time.

Imagine visiting a theme park where you show your ID once at the front gate and receive a wristband. Throughout the day, you show that wristband at each ride to get a ride ticket instead of going back to the gate with your ID. A TGT works like that wristband for network logins: you prove who you are once, and your computer uses the TGT behind the scenes to fetch passes for file shares, email, or servers.

In Kerberos on Linux, running kinit authenticates you to the Key Distribution Center (KDC) and stores a time-limited TGT in your credential cache, which you can inspect with klist. Whenever you connect to a Kerberos-aware service like SSH or NFS, your client uses the TGT to request a service ticket for that specific host—whereas a service ticket only works for one target service.

If you run kdestroy to clear your TGT, what happens to new Kerberos connections?

New connections fail to authenticate automatically because the client no longer has a TGT to present to the Ticket-Granting Service for new service tickets, so you must run kinit again to obtain a fresh TGT.

In Kerberos V5 (RFC 4120), the client obtains a TGT (krbtgt/<REALM>@<REALM>) via an AS-REQ/AS-REP exchange with the Authentication Service (typically using PA-ENC-TIMESTAMP or FAST/PKINIT pre-authentication). The ticket body—holding the session key and authorization data—is encrypted under the KDC’s krbtgt long-term key and remains opaque to the client, while the client receives a matching session key encrypted under its own key. Subsequent TGS-REQ exchanges present the TGT plus a fresh authenticator encrypted with the TGT session key; compromising a credential cache (FILE:, KEYRING:, or KCM:) exposes both for the ticket’s lifetime.
