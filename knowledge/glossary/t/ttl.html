Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with T / TTL (Transistor-Transistor Logic)

Classic 5 V (and colloquially 3.3 V) single-ended digital logic voltage levels used on board-level serial pins and microcontrollers.

When hardware tinkerers talk about “TTL” or a “TTL serial cable,” they mean low-voltage digital signals sent directly between chips on a circuit board—usually 3.3 volts or 5 volts. Older desktop serial ports used much higher voltages that can damage a small board like a Raspberry Pi or router, so knowing whether a port is TTL helps you pick the right adapter and avoid frying the hardware.

On Linux, USB-to-TTL UART adapters (such as FT232, CP2102, or CH340 dongles) appear as /dev/ttyUSB0 or /dev/ttyACM0 and connect to 3-pin debug headers (TX, RX, GND) on routers, single-board computers, and microcontrollers. Unlike RS-232, TTL UART signals are non-inverted (idle high) and low-voltage, and you must match 3.3 V versus 5 V logic levels unless the target’s pins are explicitly 5 V-tolerant.

Why can plugging a 5 V TTL adapter into a 3.3 V microcontroller’s RX pin damage the chip?

Because 5 V exceeds the 3.3 V input’s maximum rating, forward-biasing its ESD protection diodes into the 3.3 V rail and potentially burning out the I/O pad or power regulator.

Historically, Transistor-Transistor Logic (the 7400 bipolar family) operated from a 5 V supply with asymmetric thresholds (VIL ≤ 0.8 V, VIH ≥ 2.0 V, VOL ≤ 0.4 V, VOH ≥ 2.4 V), using multi-emitter input transistors and totem-pole outputs. In modern embedded Linux parlance, “TTL UART” almost always denotes uninverted 3.3 V or 5 V CMOS single-ended signaling, where CMOS VIH is typically 0.7 · VCC (3.5 V at 5 V VCC)—meaning a 3.3 V output may fail to meet a pure 5 V CMOS VIH even though it clears classic 5 V TTL’s 2.0 V threshold. Over long cables, unbuffered single-ended TTL suffers ground-bounce, crosstalk, and reflections compared with differential RS-422/485 or higher-swing RS-232.
