Touchscreen

A touchscreen is a display layered with a touch sensor that lets you interact directly with on-screen items using fingers or a stylus.

Unlike a touchpad, where you slide your finger on a separate pad to steer an arrow, a touchscreen lets you tap buttons, drag sliders, and zoom photos right where they appear on the glass. It combines the monitor and the pointing device into one surface.

Because a touchscreen maps directly to screen pixels, it reports absolute coordinates rather than relative motion—tapping the top-left corner activates whatever is drawn there. On multi-monitor Linux setups, the compositor or display server must map the touchscreen input device to the correct output and rotation (landscape vs. portrait), otherwise touching one screen moves the cursor on another.

What is the key difference between how a touchpad and a touchscreen map finger position to the screen?

A touchpad reports relative movement from the cursor’s current spot, whereas a touchscreen reports absolute coordinates tied directly to a specific display’s geometry and orientation.

Modern touchscreens use projected capacitive (PCAP) sensing over USB-HID or I2C-HID, reporting multitouch Type B evdev events with INPUT_PROP_DIRECT so userspace stacks (libinput) treat contacts as direct absolute coordinates rather than relative pointer deltas. Compositors apply a 2D affine calibration matrix combining output scale, offset, and rotation (wl_output transform) to align sensor axes with display pixels, and dispatch wl_touch events (down, up, motion, frame, cancel) with per-contact IDs for concurrent gestures and palm/edge rejection.
