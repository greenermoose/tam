Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with T / Teletype (teletypewriter, TTY)

An electromechanical typewriter-like machine that sent and received typed text over a wire, whose name and line model still underpin Unix and Linux terminals.

Before computer screens existed, people typed commands on a noisy paper-printing typewriter wired to a computer, and the computer typed its answers back onto the same roll of paper. Today’s terminal windows still carry the nickname “TTY” from those machines because the underlying idea—sending lines of text back and forth—stayed the same.

In Linux, tty survives as the device family for text consoles and serial ports (/dev/tty1, /dev/ttyS0), as well as pseudo-terminals (/dev/pts/0) used by terminal emulators and SSH. Because early teletypes printed one line at a time, Unix terminals still support canonical (“cooked”) mode, where the kernel buffers a whole line and handles Backspace before passing text to a program, versus raw mode used by full-screen editors like vim.

Why does running tty inside a graphical terminal window print something like /dev/pts/0 instead of /dev/tty1?

A graphical terminal emulator uses a pseudo-terminal slave (/dev/pts/N) paired with a master file descriptor held by the emulator, rather than a kernel virtual console (/dev/ttyN) or a physical serial line.

Electromechanical teletypes (such as the Teletype Model 33) communicated over asynchronous serial links (RS-232 or 20 mA current loops) with start/stop framing, 5-bit Baudot or 7-bit ASCII, and mechanical carriage-return/line-feed timing delays (reflected in stty delay flags). In Unix/Linux, the kernel TTY subsystem preserves this lineage in a three-layer architecture: the character device frontend, the line discipline (N_TTY by default, configured via termios(3) for ICANON, ECHO, and ISIG signal generation), and the low-level driver (UART, virtual console, or ptmx/pts pair).
