Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with T / Transistor

A three-terminal semiconductor switch or amplifier that controls electrical current using a voltage or current signal.

A transistor is a microscopic electronic switch with no moving parts. Imagine a water valve controlled by a light touch on a lever: applying a tiny electrical signal to one connection lets current flow between the other two connections, or shuts it off completely. Modern processors pack billions of these tiny switches onto a single sliver of silicon, flipping on and off billions of times a second to represent ones and zeros.

In digital computers, the most common transistor is the MOSFET (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor), which has gate, source, and drain terminals. Applying voltage to the insulated gate creates an electric field that opens or closes a conductive channel between source and drain, forming logic gates, memory cells, and bus drivers. Unlike mechanical relays, MOSFETs switch silently in picoseconds with almost no steady gate current, though they still dissipate heat when switching rapidly or leaking current when idle.

Why do modern CPUs need cooling fans if individual MOSFET gates draw almost no steady current?

Every time a CMOS gate switches, it charges and discharges tiny capacitances and briefly conducts short-circuit current, plus subthreshold leakage grows as transistors shrink; multiplied by billions of switches at gigahertz speeds, this produces tens or hundreds of watts of heat.

A MOSFET operates across cutoff (|VGS| < |Vth|), triode/linear (VDS < VGS − Vth, acting as a voltage-controlled resistor), and saturation (channel pinched off near the drain, current governed primarily by VGS). Digital logic uses Complementary MOS (CMOS), pairing pull-up PMOS and pull-down NMOS networks so static current is near zero and dynamic power scales as P = α · C · V² · f. At nanometer nodes, short-channel effects such as drain-induced barrier lowering and gate-oxide tunneling increase static leakage and force multi-gate geometries (FinFET, GAAFET) to maintain electrostatic control over the channel.
