Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with T / Twisted pair

A cable design where two insulated copper wires are twisted around each other to carry a signal while rejecting electrical interference.

Inside most Ethernet network cables, you will find pairs of thin wires twisted together like a braid. Twisting them is not just to keep them tidy: because both wires sit right next to each other and swap positions along the cable, outside electrical noise from power cords or motors hits both wires almost equally and cancels out, letting your network run faster and farther with fewer errors.

Twisted-pair cabling—most familiar as Cat5e, Cat6, or Cat6a Ethernet patch cables—carries differential signals: the transmitter sends opposite voltages on the two wires of a pair, and the receiver reads the difference between them. Unshielded twisted pair (UTP) relies on twist geometry alone for offices, whereas shielded twisted pair (STP/FTP) adds foil or braid for noisy industrial environments and needs proper grounding to avoid ground loops.

Why does untwisting a large section of an Ethernet cable near the RJ45 plug hurt network speed?

Untwisting breaks the balanced geometry of the pair, increasing crosstalk from neighboring pairs and external noise pickup, which causes frame errors and forces links to drop to lower speeds.

Twisted pair forms a balanced transmission line (typically 100 Ω differential characteristic impedance for Ethernet) where common-mode electromagnetic interference induces nearly equal voltages on both conductors and is rejected by the receiver’s differential front end. Varying twist lay lengths across the four pairs in a cable minimize near-end crosstalk (NEXT) and far-end crosstalk (FEXT). At higher frequencies (250 MHz for Cat6, 500 MHz for Cat6a supporting 10GBASE-T up to 100 m), alien crosstalk (ANEXT) between adjacent bundled cables, skin-effect attenuation, and return loss from impedance discontinuities dominate the link budget.
