TMDS - Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling

Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) is the high-speed electrical signaling method used to carry digital video and audio over DVI and HDMI cables.

Sending crisp video to a monitor requires moving billions of bits per second through a thin cable without them blurring together from electrical noise. TMDS solves this by sending each signal along a pair of wires with opposite voltages and encoding the data so the receiver can stay in sync with every pixel.

A standard single-link DVI or HDMI cable uses four TMDS differential pairs: three for red, green, and blue pixel data (plus audio and control packets during blanking intervals) and one dedicated TMDS clock pair. Unlike DisplayPort, which packets data into self-clocked lanes, TMDS relies on that separate clock lane and hits a bandwidth ceiling around 18 Gbps in HDMI 2.0 before HDMI 2.1 switches to Fixed Rate Link (FRL).

Why do long or cheap HDMI cables sometimes show sparkles or drop the signal at 4K 60 Hz?

At 4K 60 Hz, TMDS runs near its 600 MHz character clock limit (18 Gbps total), where cable attenuation and skew between the clock pair and data pairs degrade eye diagrams enough that the display loses clock lock or misreads symbols.

TMDS encodes each 8-bit video channel into a 10-bit symbol in two stages: first minimizing bit transitions using XOR or XNOR based on bit-count heuristics (encoded in bit 8), then optionally inverting the first 8 bits (signaled in bit 9) to maintain long-term DC balance across AC-coupled links. During horizontal and vertical blanking periods, the data lanes instead carry 10-bit characters with high transition density for synchronization and 4-bit-per-lane TERC4 error-protected data islands (carrying HDMI audio and InfoFrames). Dual-link DVI adds a second trio of data pairs sharing the same clock reference to double pixel throughput.
