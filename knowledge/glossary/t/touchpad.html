Touchpad

A touchpad is a flat, touch-sensitive pointing surface built into laptops and some keyboards that translates finger movements and gestures into pointer motion, clicks, and scrolling.

A touchpad replaces a separate mouse when you are using a laptop on the go. Sliding one finger moves the cursor, tapping can act as a click, and brushing two fingers together scrolls a web page—all without lifting your hands far from the keyboard.

On Linux, libinput handles touchpad input for Wayland compositors and Xorg, turning raw finger contacts into tap-to-click, natural scrolling, two-finger or edge scrolling, and multi-finger swipe/pinch gestures. Touchpads report relative motion like a mouse (moving from where the pointer already is) even though the sensor underneath tracks exact finger positions, and palm rejection temporarily ignores inadvertent touches while you type.

Why does resting your thumb lightly near the bottom of a touchpad while typing usually not jump the cursor?

Input stacks like libinput apply palm detection and disable-while-typing heuristics—ignoring large-area contacts, edge touches, and brief contacts triggered within milliseconds of keyboard events.

Laptop touchpads are typically capacitive I2C-HID or SMBus/PS2 devices emitting Linux evdev multitouch Type B slots (ABS_MT_SLOT, ABS_MT_TRACKING_ID, ABS_MT_POSITION_X/Y, plus optional ABS_MT_PRESSURE and ABS_MT_TOUCH_MAJOR). Clickpads integrate a single physical dome switch under the surface (BTN_LEFT) and rely on software finger-zone or multi-finger heuristics in libinput to distinguish left, right, and middle clicks, while pointer acceleration applies device-specific DPI normalization and speed curves before delivering relative motion events to the compositor.
