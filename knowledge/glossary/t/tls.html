TLS - Transport Layer Security

Transport Layer Security (TLS) is the cryptographic protocol that encrypts network connections and verifies the identity of the server—and optionally the client—over an untrusted network.

When you visit an https:// website or send email securely, TLS wraps your data in a locked envelope so anyone listening on the same Wi-Fi or internet cable sees only scrambled bytes. It also checks the site’s digital ID card so you know you are talking to your real bank rather than an impostor.

TLS sits between the transport layer (usually TCP) and application protocols like HTTP, IMAP, or SMTP, and it replaced the older, insecure SSL protocol. In practice, a TLS connection performs a handshake first—agreeing on a cipher suite, verifying an X.509 certificate chain against your system’s trusted Certificate Authorities (CAs), and deriving session keys—before encrypted application bytes flow; tools like openssl s_client -connect host:443 let you inspect that handshake and certificate directly.

Why does an expired or self-signed certificate trigger a browser warning even though TLS still encrypts the traffic?

Encryption alone only hides data from passive eavesdroppers; without a valid, trusted certificate matching the hostname, you cannot be sure you are not encrypting your traffic directly to an attacker performing a man-in-the-middle interception.

TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446) streamlines the handshake to 1-RTT using ephemeral (EC)DHE key exchange for mandatory forward secrecy, removes static RSA key transport and legacy CBC/RC4 ciphers, and restricts bulk protection to AEAD constructions such as AES-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305. After key schedule derivation via HKDF, both handshake messages (from ServerHello onward) and application records are authenticated and encrypted at the record layer. Operational trade-offs include 0-RTT early data replay risks, OCSP stapling or CRLite for revocation checking, and ALPN negotiation (e.g., h2, http/1.1) before application framing begins.
