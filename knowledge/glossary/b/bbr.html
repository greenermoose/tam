BBR (Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time)

A TCP congestion control algorithm that tries to send at the network's real capacity without filling queues unnecessarily.

When many computers share a network, they need rules for how fast to send data. Older TCP rules often slow down only after packets are lost. BBR takes a different approach: it estimates how much bandwidth the path can carry and how long a round trip takes, then tries to send at that pace. The goal is high throughput with lower delay.

BBR models the connection's bottleneck bandwidth (BtlBw) and minimum round-trip propagation time (RTprop). Instead of treating packet loss as the primary congestion signal, it probes to keep estimates current and paces packets to match the estimated delivery rate. On Linux, you can see available congestion control algorithms with sysctl net.ipv4.tcp_available_congestion_control and the current one with sysctl net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control. CUBIC remains the default on many systems, while BBR is often used where latency under load matters.

Why can loss-based congestion control create high latency?

If a sender keeps increasing its rate until buffers overflow, queues build up before packets are dropped. Those full queues add delay, sometimes called bufferbloat, even when the link is "busy".

BBR (developed by Google, with BBRv1 in Linux and later BBRv2/v3 iterations) operates around BtlBw and RTprop estimates, pacing at an estimated delivery rate and limiting inflight data near BDP (bandwidth-delay product). It periodically probes bandwidth upward and RTprop downward (by reducing inflight). Compared with loss-based CUBIC, BBR can reduce queueing delay but has had fairness and coexistence edge cases, especially with shallow buffers, loss-heavy paths, or competing loss-based flows. Deployment decisions should be based on measured path behavior, not algorithm name alone.
