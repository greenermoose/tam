Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with B / Bandwidth

The maximum rate at which data or signals can be carried across a communication channel or interface.

Think of bandwidth like the width of a highway: a four-lane highway can carry more cars per minute than a single-lane country road. Note that bandwidth is about how much data can fit through the pipe at once, not how fast a single packet travels from start to finish (that’s latency).

In networking and storage, bandwidth is usually measured in bits per second (Mbps, Gbps) or bytes per second (MB/s), and it represents a theoretical ceiling rather than what an application actually achieves. Real-world throughput is always lower than raw link bandwidth because of protocol headers, acknowledgments, congestion, and CPU or disk bottlenecks.

If a 1 Gbps Ethernet link only transfers files at 110 MB/s, is the link broken?

No. 1 Gbps equals 125 MB/s (1,000,000,000 ÷ 8), and after subtracting Ethernet, IP, and TCP header overhead plus acknowledgments, ~110–118 MB/s is normal achieved throughput.

In signal processing, bandwidth (B, in Hz) is the width of the frequency passband a physical medium supports; in networking, it denotes nominal bit-rate capacity (C). The two are linked by the Shannon–Hartley theorem: C = B log₂(1 + SNR) bits/s for an additive white Gaussian noise channel. Distinguishing nominal link rate, goodput (application payload bytes/s excluding retransmissions and framing), and bandwidth-delay product (BDP = C × RTT, which governs TCP window sizing) is essential when diagnosing underutilized high-speed links.
