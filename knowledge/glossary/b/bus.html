Bus

A shared communication pathway inside a computer that carries data, addresses, and control signals between components.

A bus is like a multi-lane highway inside your computer connecting the processor, memory, and parts such as drives or keyboards. Without a shared pathway, every single component would need its own dedicated bundle of wires to every other component, which would be impossible to fit on a circuit board.

On Linux, you encounter buses when inspecting hardware with tools like lspci (PCIe) or lsusb (USB). Older buses such as parallel PCI shared one set of wires among all attached devices, whereas modern interconnects like PCI Express use dedicated point-to-point lanes managed by switches so devices spend less time waiting for a turn.

Why did modern computers replace shared parallel buses like PCI with point-to-point links like PCIe?

At high clock speeds, signals on a shared parallel bus suffer from electrical skew and bus contention because every device competes for the same wires. Point-to-point serial links give each device dedicated lanes, enabling higher frequencies and simultaneous transfers.

A bus or interconnect defines electrical signaling, transaction framing, addressing, width, and arbitration rules. Shared-medium buses require an arbiter and suffer capacitive loading that scales with slot count; switched fabrics like PCIe use split-transaction packet protocols (TLPs over DLLPs and physical lanes with 8b/10b or 128b/130b encoding) where read requests and completions are decoupled, enabling out-of-order completion, DMA coherency, and dynamic lane-width negotiation.
