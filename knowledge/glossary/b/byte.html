Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with B / Byte

A group of eight bits treated as a single unit of digital information.

If a single bit is one light switch (on or off), a byte is a row of eight switches side by side. Eight switches can form 256 different patterns, which is enough to hold one small number, one basic text character, or a tiny piece of a color or sound.

On modern systems, memory and storage are addressed by the byte rather than by individual bits. When reading sizes in tools like ls -lh or df -h, watch the unit prefix: KiB and MiB count powers of 1024 bytes (binary), while kB and MB often count powers of 1000 bytes (decimal, common on drive labels).

Why does a 1 TB drive show up as roughly 931 GiB in Linux disk tools?

Drive makers define 1 TB as 1000⁴ bytes (decimal), while many OS tools report GiB using 1024³ bytes (binary). Dividing 10¹² by 1024³ gives about 931.3 GiB—the total byte count is identical, only the unit scale differs.

While an octet is strictly 8 bits, the C standard defines a byte as the size of char (CHAR_BIT bits, guaranteed at least 8) and the unit returned by sizeof; historical architectures such as the PDP-10 used 36-bit words with variable-width bytes (6, 7, or 9 bits). On hardware with 8-bit bytes, multi-byte integers introduce endianness (little-endian vs. big-endian byte order) and alignment constraints, where unaligned accesses can trigger bus faults or split-cache-line penalties.
