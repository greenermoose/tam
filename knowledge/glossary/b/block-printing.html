Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with B / Block printing

Block printing is a relief method in which raised, inked shapes on a carved block are pressed onto paper or cloth.

Carve a mirror image of a design into a wooden block, brush ink over the raised parts and press paper onto it: that is block printing. The same idea can repeat a picture or a page of text many times, long before any mechanical press existed.

Block printing arose in East Asia and reached Europe by the Middle Ages; whole pages of text and illustration could be cut into one wooden block. It differs from movable type, where individual letters are rearranged for each page. Woodblocks wear down and each page needs its own block, so the method suits repeated images and small runs, and survives in textile and craft printing.

Relief printing transfers ink only from the uncut surface, so carved channels stay blank and the block must carry a mirrored image. A full-page woodblock can yield thousands of impressions but is slow to carve, which is why reusable metal type changed European book production. The page, not the letter, was the reusable unit; this is the key contrast with movable type.
