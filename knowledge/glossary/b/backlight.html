Backlight

The light source mounted behind or along the edge of an LCD panel that makes the image visible.

Unlike OLED screens where each tiny dot makes its own light, an LCD screen works like a stained-glass window—it can block or tint light, but it cannot glow on its own. The backlight is the lamp behind the screen that shines through the pixels so you can see the picture.

On a Linux laptop, the display backlight is typically a strip of white LEDs controlled through /sys/class/backlight/ (for example, intel_backlight or amdgpu_bl0), where writing to brightness adjusts output up to max_brightness. Some panels dim using PWM (rapidly flickering the LEDs on and off), which can cause eye strain or visible flicker at low brightness, whereas DC (constant-current) dimming lowers current smoothly.

Why does setting an LCD backlight to 0 in /sys/class/backlight/ sometimes make the screen completely unreadable rather than just dim?

An LCD panel does not emit light; its liquid crystals only modulate light passing through. Turning the backlight off removes the illumination entirely, leaving only faint reflections from ambient room light.

Backlight drivers typically use a boost DC-DC converter driving series/parallel LED strings, modulated either by analog current-sense feedback (DC dimming) or a PWM signal on the converter’s enable/dimming pin (often 200 Hz to >20 kHz; low-frequency PWM risks stroboscopic artifacts). In Linux, the DRM/KMS subsystem and ACPI/platform drivers expose enum backlight_type (raw, platform, firmware) via sysfs, and desktop environments use logind or udev rules to grant unprivileged session users write access without pkexec or root.
