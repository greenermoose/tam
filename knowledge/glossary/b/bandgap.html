Bandgap

The energy gap an electron in a solid must cross before it can move freely and conduct electric current.

Imagine electrons sitting on a crowded lower shelf (where they are stuck in place) with an empty upper shelf above them (where they can move around as electricity). The bandgap is the height of the jump between those two shelves. Conductors have no gap at all, insulators have a huge jump, and semiconductors like silicon have a medium jump that engineers can control.

Between a semiconductor’s valence band and conduction band lies a forbidden energy range called the bandgap (E_g). Silicon has a bandgap of about 1.12 eV at room temperature, while wide-bandgap materials used in compact USB-C GaN chargers have much larger gaps (gallium nitride is ~3.4 eV). Heat or photons with energy ≥ E_g can excite electrons across the gap, while intentional doping adds nearby energy states so current can flow without needing full bandgap excitation.

Why can a silicon solar cell generate electricity from visible light, while pure glass does not?

Visible light photons carry roughly 1.6–3.1 eV, which exceeds silicon’s ~1.12 eV bandgap and excites electrons into the conduction band. Glass has a much larger bandgap, so visible photons pass through without enough energy to free electrons.

Defined as E_g = E_c − E_v (conduction band minimum minus valence band maximum) in a crystalline solid’s electronic band structure. Direct-bandgap semiconductors (e.g., GaAs, GaN) allow radiative electron–hole recombination without phonon momentum transfer, making them efficient for LEDs and laser diodes; indirect-bandgap semiconductors (e.g., Si, Ge) require phonon assistance. E_g determines intrinsic carrier concentration n_i ∝ exp(−E_g / 2k_B T) and optical absorption cutoffs, explaining why wide-bandgap devices (SiC, GaN) tolerate higher junction temperatures and breakdown fields with lower leakage.
