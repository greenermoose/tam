Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with B / BER (Bit Error Rate)

The fraction of transmitted bits that arrive flipped or corrupted over a communication channel.

Imagine whispering a 100-letter message across a noisy room and 1 letter gets misheard—your error rate is 1%. BER measures that same idea for digital data: out of all the 1s and 0s sent over a wire, fiber, or wireless link, it tells you how many arrived wrong because of noise or interference.

BER is calculated as error bits / total bits received and is usually written in scientific notation (such as 10−12 for high-speed wired links). A lower BER means a cleaner signal. Note that BER measures raw bit flips on the physical link, whereas packet loss measures whole frames or packets dropped higher up—often after a single bit error causes a checksum (CRC) failure.

If a Gigabit Ethernet link has a BER of 10−12, roughly how often would a single bit error occur at full 1 Gbps?

At 109 bits per second, 1012 bits pass every 1,000 seconds (about 16.7 minutes), so you would expect roughly one bit error every 17 minutes.

Formally the empirical ratio or statistical probability P_b of bit errors at the receiver decision circuit. In modern links, engineers distinguish pre-FEC BER (raw channel errors before Forward Error Correction, e.g., 10−4–10−5 on 25G/100G PAM-4 lanes) from post-FEC BER (residual errors after Reed–Solomon or LDPC decoding, typically required to be < 10−12 or < 10−15). For uncoded BPSK over AWGN, BER = Q(√(2 E_b / N_0)); in hardware testing, BER is measured with PRBS patterns (e.g., PRBS31) and visualized as a bathtub curve against sampling phase and voltage thresholds.
