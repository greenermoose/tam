Asymmetric encryption

An encryption method that uses a public key to lock data and a separate, mathematically linked private key to unlock it.

Think of a padlock with a slot in the top: you can hand out open padlocks (your public key) to anyone so they can drop a message in a box and snap it shut, but only you hold the single physical key (your private key) that can open those boxes. Because you never have to share your unlocking key, strangers can send you private messages safely.

Unlike symmetric encryption (such as AES), where both sides must already share the same secret key, asymmetric encryption lets two parties communicate securely over an untrusted network without a prior secret. In practice, systems like TLS and SSH use asymmetric algorithms (such as RSA or ECDH/X25519) only to authenticate or agree on a temporary session key, then switch to faster symmetric encryption for the actual data.

Why don’t TLS or PGP encrypt an entire large file directly with RSA?

Asymmetric math (large modular exponentiations or elliptic-curve operations) is orders of magnitude slower than symmetric ciphers like AES, and RSA can only encrypt payloads smaller than its modulus, so hybrid encryption wraps a symmetric key instead.

Formally, public-key encryption (PKE) or key encapsulation mechanisms (KEM) rely on trapdoor one-way functions or hard algebraic/lattice problems (e.g., integer factorization in RSA-OAEP, discrete logarithms in ElGamal/ECIES, or Module-LWE in ML-KEM). Plaintext or textbook RSA is deterministic and malleably insecure; production schemes require randomized padding (OAEP) or KEM+DEM composition to achieve IND-CCA2 security. Because Shor’s algorithm breaks RSA and ECC in polynomial time on a fault-tolerant quantum computer, modern protocols are migrating toward hybrid post-quantum KEMs paired with symmetric AEAD ciphers.
