Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with A / Abugida

A writing system in which each consonant sign carries a built-in vowel, changed or removed with a mark.

Think of a consonant sign that already includes an 'a' sound. To write a different vowel you add a small mark; to write a bare consonant you use a cancellation mark. Scripts such as Devanagari, used for Hindi and Sanskrit, work this way.

The basic unit of an abugida is consonant plus inherent vowel, not a single consonant. In Devanagari, क is ka, कि is ki and क् is a bare k with a virama. This differs from an alphabet, where consonants and vowels are separate equal letters, and from a syllabary, where each syllable gets its own sign. Most Brahmic-derived scripts are abugidas.

What does a virama do in Devanagari?

It suppresses the inherent vowel, leaving a bare consonant rather than a consonant plus 'a'.

The name was proposed by Peter Daniels to replace the older term 'alphasyllabary'. Unicode encodes a consonant plus dependent vowel signs as separate code points, usually combining marks, with a virama for vowel suppression. Dependent signs can be encoded after the base but drawn above, below or before it, so shaping engines apply Indic-specific GSUB and GPOS lookups to place them correctly.
