ATX (Advanced Technology eXtended)

The standard desktop PC specification defining motherboard dimensions, mounting holes, and power-supply connectors and voltages.

When you buy a standard desktop PC case, motherboard, and power supply from different companies, they still screw together and plug in without guesswork because they all follow the ATX standard. It ensures the power cables fit the motherboard sockets and the rear ports line up with the back of the case.

An ATX power supply provides +12V, +5V, +3.3V, and a +5VSB (standby) rail, which is why a desktop motherboard can wake from sleep on a power-button press or Wake-on-LAN packet even when the main rails are off. Variants like Micro-ATX and Mini-ITX shrink the motherboard footprint while keeping the same ATX 24-pin main power connector and mounting-hole alignment.

How can an ATX desktop turn on from a soft power button or Wake-on-LAN when the main system is off?

The power supply keeps the +5VSB standby rail energized whenever AC power is connected, powering the motherboard’s standby logic so it can assert PS_ON# to wake the main rails.

Introduced by Intel in 1995 and evolved through ATX12V (2.x/3.0), the specification defines mechanical clearances (305 × 244 mm for full ATX), the 24-pin main connector, dedicated 4/8-pin EPS +12V CPU feeds, and 12VHPWR/12V-2x6 high-wattage GPU cabling in ATX 3.0. Power sequencing relies on the motherboard pulling PS_ON# (active-low) to ground to start the main switching rails, after which the PSU asserts PWR_OK (Power Good) within 100–500 ms once voltages stabilize within ±5% tolerances, releasing the CPU from reset.
