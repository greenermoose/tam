Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with A / AVX (Advanced Vector Extensions)

A 256-bit SIMD (Single Instruction, Multiple Data) instruction-set extension for x86_64 processors that speeds up math-heavy workloads.

Normally, a processor works on one or two numbers at a time. AVX lets the processor pack several numbers side by side—256 bits wide—and add or multiply all of them in a single step. Think of it like carrying eight grocery bags in one trip instead of making eight separate trips; it makes tasks like audio, video, and 3D graphics much faster.

AVX (introduced with Intel Sandy Bridge and AMD Bulldozer in 2011) doubles the vector register width from 128-bit XMM registers (SSE) to 256-bit YMM registers and introduces non-destructive three-operand instruction syntax. Older laptops may lack AVX or AVX2, which is why Tamlinux builds baseline binaries for generic x86_64 unless tuned locally. You can check whether your CPU supports it by inspecting the avx flag in /proc/cpuinfo.

What happens if you run a binary compiled with -mavx on a CPU that lacks AVX?

The kernel delivers SIGILL (Illegal Instruction) and terminates the process as soon as it executes an AVX opcode, because the CPU decoder does not recognize the VEX-encoded instruction.

AVX introduces VEX-prefix encoding, 16 256-bit YMM0–YMM15 registers (aliased over XMM0–XMM15 in the lower 128 bits), and primarily targets floating-point operations (packed single/double); most 256-bit integer operations arrived later in AVX2. Key ABI and microarchitectural considerations include XSAVE/XRSTOR state management by the OS kernel (XCR0 bit 2), the AVX–SSE transition penalty when mixing legacy 128-bit SSE instructions without VZEROUPPER (due to dirty upper YMM halves), and frequency-scaling downclocking on some Intel microarchitectures during sustained 256-bit execution.
