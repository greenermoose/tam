AWG (American Wire Gauge)

A standardized numbering system for round wire diameter where smaller numbers indicate thicker wire.

AWG tells you how thick the copper inside a cable is. The counterintuitive part is that the numbers run backward: a 18 AWG power cord is much thicker and sturdier than a thin 28 AWG phone-charging cable. Thicker wires can safely carry more electricity without getting hot.

In computing and USB-C/USB-PD cabling, lower-AWG power conductors have lower electrical resistance, which reduces voltage drop and heat when carrying high current (such as 3 A or 5 A at 20 V). A thin 28 AWG USB cable may work for slow data transfer, but it will cause voltage sag and slow charging—or fail e-marker checks—under high-power loads compared to a 20 AWG cable.

Between a 20 AWG cable and a 28 AWG cable of the same length, which loses more voltage under a 3 A load?

The 28 AWG cable loses more voltage. Higher AWG numbers mean a thinner conductor with higher resistance per meter, so V = I × R produces a larger voltage drop and more resistive heating.

AWG is a logarithmic scale defined by 36 steps between 0000 (4/0, 0.4600 in / 11.684 mm) and 36 AWG (0.0050 in / 0.127 mm), giving a diameter ratio of 921/39 ≈ 1.12293 per step. Every 3 AWG decrease doubles cross-sectional area and halves DC resistance per unit length (annealed copper at 20 °C is ~1.724 × 10−8 Ω·m); every 6 AWG decrease doubles diameter. At high frequencies (e.g., USB4 or PCIe twinax), skin effect concentrates current near the conductor surface, making surface plating and geometry—not just DC AWG—dominant for insertion loss.
