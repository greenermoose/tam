Aliasing

A fast-changing signal can look like a slower one when it is sampled too rarely.

Imagine a wagon wheel filmed at the wrong frame rate. It can appear to turn slowly backwards even though it turns quickly forwards. Sampling a signal gives the same kind of illusion: a real high frequency is mistaken for a lower one.

A 29 kHz tone sampled 48,000 times per second is read as 19 kHz because 48 minus 29 is 19. The recording contains a false tone that was not in the original sound. An anti-alias filter removes frequencies too high for the sample rate before the ADC converts the signal.

Can a filter added after sampling remove aliasing?

No. Once the high frequency has been mistaken for a low one, the two are indistinguishable in the samples. The filter must work on the analog signal before conversion.

For a real sinusoid at f Hz sampled at Fs, aliases occur at |f - k Fs| for integer k. Frequencies inside the first Nyquist band, from 0 to Fs/2, are indistinguishable from their images in higher bands. Practical anti-alias filters need a transition band, so designers oversample or choose a higher Fs to leave room for the filter. Aliasing also appears in images as moire patterns and in graphics when a texture is minified without filtering.
