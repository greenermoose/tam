ARQ (Automatic Repeat Request)

Automatic Repeat Request—a communication rule where a receiver checks incoming data for errors and asks the sender to retransmit anything damaged or missing.

Imagine reading a phone number over a noisy connection: if part of it is garbled, you say “say that last part again” until you hear it clearly. ARQ works the same way for computers, letting two devices turn a noisy cable or Wi-Fi signal into a reliable connection by automatically re-sending lost or corrupted pieces.

You rely on ARQ every time you use Wi-Fi or load a web page over TCP: the receiver verifies a checksum or CRC, sends an acknowledgment (ACK) for good frames, and triggers a resend (via a negative acknowledgment NAK or a timeout) when frames go missing. The trade-off is latency: ARQ guarantees accuracy at the cost of waiting for retransmissions, which is why real-time voice and video calls often use UDP without ARQ rather than stalling playback.

Why do real-time video calls often avoid TCP’s ARQ retransmission behavior?

Waiting for lost packets to be detected and retransmitted adds jitter and stalls playback; real-time audio/video usually prefers dropping an occasional late frame over delaying newer frames.

ARQ protocols pair error-detecting codes (CRC-32 at link layers, 16-bit Internet checksum at transport) with sequence numbers, ACK/NAK feedback, and retransmission timers across three classical schemes: Stop-and-Wait (window size 1, throughput bounded by RTT), Go-Back-N (sliding window of size N, simple receiver, retransmits all frames from the first lost packet), and Selective Repeat (receiver buffers out-of-order frames and SACKs individual gaps, maximizing link utilization at higher buffer cost). Modern wireless links (LTE/5G, Wi-Fi) combine ARQ with Forward Error Correction in Hybrid ARQ (HARQ)—using Chase combining or Incremental Redundancy—to decode partially corrupted frames before requesting a full retransmission.
