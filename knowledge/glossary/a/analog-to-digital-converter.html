Guide / Glossary A-Z / Terms beginning with A / Analog-to-digital converter (ADC)

A circuit that turns a changing physical voltage into a digital code a computer can store.

A microphone produces a voltage that moves smoothly with sound. An ADC looks at that voltage at separate moments and writes a number for each one. Those numbers can then be saved, sent or processed.

Two settings describe most converters: the sample rate, or how often it looks, and the bit depth, or how finely it rounds each look. A 16-bit converter over 0 to 3.3 V has 65,536 possible codes. The input is quantized to the nearest code, so a small difference in voltage may produce no change in the number.

Why does a higher bit depth not fix a too-low sample rate?

Bit depth controls the size of each amplitude step. Sample rate controls which frequencies can be captured. They solve different problems, and neither substitutes for the other.

Common architectures include successive approximation, flash, pipelined and delta-sigma converters. Key parameters include integral and differential non-linearity, offset and gain error, noise floor, aperture jitter, reference drift and settling time. An ADC may saturate at its rails, and its effective number of bits can be lower than its nominal width because of noise and distortion. Sampling theory requires band-limiting before conversion to avoid aliasing.
