Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with A / AC - Alternating Current

Electric current that periodically reverses its direction of flow, rather than moving steadily in one direction like direct current.

Electricity from a wall outlet is alternating current: instead of flowing in a single straight line like power from a battery, the electrical push flips back and forth many times every second. Power grids use AC because it can travel long distances over power lines with less wasted energy, and a laptop’s power brick converts that AC from the wall into the steady DC power the computer’s circuits need.

Household mains power is AC—typically 50 Hz in Europe and much of the world, or 60 Hz in North America—meaning the current reverses direction 100 or 120 times per second. Unlike DC (used inside batteries, USB-C cables, and computer motherboards), AC voltage can be stepped up or down easily with passive transformers, which is why long-distance transmission uses high-voltage AC before stepping down for homes.

Why does a laptop power adapter have to convert wall AC into DC instead of feeding AC straight to the processor?

Digital logic circuits and lithium-ion batteries require a steady, low-voltage polarity (DC) to represent binary states and charge safely; feeding reversing high-voltage AC directly into them would immediately destroy the components.

Sinusoidal AC is characterized by RMS and peak amplitude (V_rms = V_peak / √2 for a pure sine wave, so 120 V_rms corresponds to roughly 170 V_peak), frequency f, and phase angle φ. In reactive loads containing inductance L or capacitance C, current leads or lags voltage, producing complex impedance Z = R + jX and a power factor cos(φ) that reduces real power P = V_rms · I_rms · cos(φ) relative to apparent power S. Switching power supplies (SMPS) in PC PSUs first rectify AC and apply Power Factor Correction (PFC) before high-frequency DC-DC conversion to limit harmonic distortion on the mains line.
