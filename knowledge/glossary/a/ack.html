ACK (Acknowledge, 0x06)

The ASCII control character 0x06 (Acknowledge) and the general signal protocols use to confirm that a message or packet was received intact.

When you send someone a text or package and they reply “Got it!”, that reply is an acknowledgment. In computing, ACK serves that exact purpose: it is a quick confirmation sent back to the sender so they know the data arrived safely and do not need to send it again.

Part of the C0 control codes, ACK (0x06, typed as Ctrl+F in some terminals) originally confirmed valid blocks over serial lines, often paired with NAK (0x15) to request a retransmission. In modern networking, TCP uses an ACK flag and acknowledgment number in its packet header to confirm which byte offset the receiver has successfully received, letting the sender advance its transmission window or resend lost segments.

What happens in a reliable protocol like TCP if the sender never receives an ACK for a transmitted packet?

The sender assumes the packet (or its ACK) was lost or corrupted in transit; after a retransmission timeout (or duplicate ACKs indicating a gap), it resends the unacknowledged data so nothing is silently dropped.

Beyond ASCII 0x06 in character-oriented protocols (such as BISYNC and XMODEM), ACK semantics differ sharply by layer: in TCP (RFC 9293), ACKs are cumulative (acknowledging the next expected sequence number, with optional SACK blocks per RFC 2018 for non-contiguous ranges), enabling sliding-window flow control and fast retransmit on three duplicate ACKs. At the hardware bus level, I2C uses a 9th-clock-pulse open-drain ACK (SDA pulled low by the target) after every byte, where a missing pull-down (NACK) signals an absent address, busy target, or end-of-read.
