Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with A / Abjad

A writing system whose letters stand for consonants, with short vowels usually left unwritten.

In an abjad, written signs mainly record consonants. An Arabic or Hebrew word can look short on the page while carrying vowels that a reader supplies from context. Vowel marks can be added, especially in teaching texts or scripture, but everyday text often omits them.

Abjads sit between alphabets and syllabaries: unlike a full alphabet they do not give short vowels equal standing, and unlike a syllabary their basic signs are consonants rather than whole syllables. Hebrew and Arabic are the best-known examples; early Phoenician writing was also an abjad. Arabic runs right to left, so a shaper must arrange joined letter forms before rasterization.

Why can the same Arabic word be read with different vowels?

Because short vowels are often omitted; grammar, context and optional diacritics decide.

The term was coined by Peter Daniels to contrast abjads with abugidas and syllabaries. Hebrew uses consonant letters called matres lectionis to hint at long vowels, while Arabic uses diacritics for short vowels. Unicode encodes consonants as ordinary code points and vowel marks as combining marks; HarfBuzz applies joining and mark-positioning lookups before a renderer draws the result.
