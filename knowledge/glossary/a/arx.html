ARX (Add-Rotate-XOR)

A cipher design style that builds encryption and hashing algorithms out of only three simple operations: modular addition, bit rotation, and exclusive OR (XOR).

Think of scrambling a row of numbers using only three basic moves: adding numbers that wrap around like a clock, sliding bits around in a loop, and flipping bits with simple yes/no rules. Because computers can do these three moves at a steady, predictable speed without looking anything up in a table, ARX-based tools can protect your data both quickly and safely.

In Linux and modern TLS libraries, ARX designs power algorithms like ChaCha20, BLAKE2/BLAKE3, and SipHash. Unlike older ciphers such as AES (which without hardware acceleration rely on S-box lookup tables), ARX avoids data-dependent memory reads, making software implementations naturally resistant to cache-timing side-channel attacks.

Why do software-only ARX ciphers resist cache-timing attacks better than table-based ciphers?

Because ARX uses only register-to-register arithmetic and bit operations, its memory access pattern never depends on secret key or plaintext bytes, so an attacker monitoring CPU cache hits cannot infer secret data.

ARX relies on the algebraic tension between linear operations over GF(2) (XOR and cyclic bit rotation) and non-linear carry propagation in addition modulo 2^n (typically n = 32 or 64) to achieve diffusion and confusion without S-boxes. While constant-time in software, ARX requires careful differential/rotational cryptanalysis (e.g., rotational-XOR and modular differential trails) and enough rounds to prevent low-Hamming-weight differences from propagating predictably. On CPUs with dedicated AES-NI or SHA extensions, hardware-accelerated block ciphers can still outpace scalar ARX, though SIMD-friendly designs like ChaCha20 and BLAKE3 narrow or reverse that gap via wide vector lanes.
