AES-256

The 256-bit key variant of the Advanced Encryption Standard, a symmetric block cipher that processes 128-bit data blocks in 14 rounds.

AES-256 is the heaviest-duty standard version of AES, using a 256-bit secret key—twice the key length of AES-128. People and organizations choose it for long-lived secrets like encrypted hard drives and backups, where data may need to stay private for decades even against future supercomputers.

Part of AES variants, AES-256 is commonly used for full-disk encryption (such as LUKS2 with aes-xts-plain64 and a 512-bit XTS key) and high-assurance TLS 1.3 suites. On CPUs with hardware AES acceleration (x86 AES-NI or ARMv8 Cryptography Extensions), the extra 4 rounds compared with AES-128 add only a modest latency cost in pipelined modes like GCM or XTS.

Why does cryptsetup / LUKS2 often show a 512-bit key size when configured to use AES-256 in XTS mode?

XTS mode splits its key in half: one 256-bit key encrypts the data blocks and a second independent 256-bit key encrypts the sector tweak, so aes-xts-plain64 with 256-bit AES requires a 512-bit total key.

AES-256 uses a 14-round Substitution–Permutation Network (15 round keys, 240 bytes of expanded key material) over a 128-bit block, providing ~128-bit brute-force resistance against Grover’s quantum search algorithm. While theoretical related-key attacks exist against AES-256’s key schedule (which mixes two 128-bit halves with an extra SubWord step), they do not apply when keys are chosen uniformly at random in standard AEAD or XTS modes; real-world failures stem instead from weak key-derivation parameters (low PBKDF2/Argon2id cost), nonce reuse in CTR/GCM, or XTS’s lack of ciphertext authentication against active disk tampering.
