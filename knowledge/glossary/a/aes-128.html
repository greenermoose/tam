AES-128

The 128-bit key variant of the Advanced Encryption Standard, a widely used symmetric block cipher that processes 128-bit data blocks in 10 rounds.

AES-128 is a fast, battle-tested digital padlock that uses a 128-bit secret key—a number so large that trying every possible key by brute force would take longer than the age of the universe on today’s computers. Because it balances strong security with top speed, it is the default choice for securing everyday web traffic and wireless networks.

Part of AES variants, AES-128 is the default cipher in many TLS 1.3 and WPA2/WPA3 configurations and runs at multi-gigabyte-per-second speeds on modern CPUs with hardware AES-NI or ARMv8 Crypto extensions. Compared with AES-256, AES-128 runs 10 rounds instead of 14 (roughly 40% fewer round operations in pure block throughput), while still providing full 128-bit classical security against key-recovery attacks.

If AES-128 is already unbreakable by classical brute-force computers, why do some organizations require AES-256?

Two reasons: compliance policies (such as government security suites) and a theoretical quantum computer running Grover’s algorithm, which could halve brute-force search work from 2^128 to roughly 2^64 operations—whereas AES-256 retains roughly 128-bit post-quantum brute-force resistance.

AES-128 operates on a 4×4 byte state matrix (128-bit block) through an initial AddRoundKey followed by 9 full SPN rounds (SubBytes, ShiftRows, MixColumns, AddRoundKey) and a final round omitting MixColumns, using a 176-byte expanded key schedule (11 round keys). In practice, AES-128’s security margin is often constrained by mode-of-operation limits (such as the 2^32-block / 64 GiB invocation bound in AES-GCM) rather than key size, though AES-128 has no practical related-key weakness in single-key AEAD usage unlike theoretical related-key attacks on AES-256’s key schedule.
