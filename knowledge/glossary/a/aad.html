AAD (Additional Authenticated Data)

Plaintext metadata—such as packet headers or routing addresses—whose integrity is protected by an authenticated cipher without being encrypted.

Imagine mailing a letter in a sealed envelope. You want the postal service to read the address on the outside so they can deliver it, so you cannot hide it. At the same time, you do not want anyone to secretly alter that address in transit. AAD works the same way for digital messages: it stays readable so networks can route the packet, while the encryption’s tamper-detection seal ensures any change to it is caught.

In Linux networking and TLS, protocols like WireGuard, QUIC, and TLS 1.3 pass packet headers, sequence numbers, or record types as AAD into AEAD ciphers such as AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305. Unlike the encrypted payload, AAD is not confidentiality-protected—any observer can read it—so you should never put secrets into AAD. However, it is bound into the authentication tag, so if an attacker modifies a header bit or swaps ciphertext between two headers, decryption fails and the packet is dropped.

Why does a protocol put packet headers in AAD instead of encrypting them?

Intermediate routers, switches, or the receiving parser often need to read headers (like protocol version, connection ID, or length) before decrypting the payload. Putting those headers in AAD keeps them readable while ensuring any tampering with the routing or framing metadata invalidates the authentication tag.

In AEAD constructions (RFC 5116), AAD is fed into the MAC/PRF alongside the ciphertext and typically length-encoded to prevent boundary ambiguity—for instance, GHASH over GF(2^128) in AES-GCM or Poly1305 over GF(2^130 - 5) in ChaCha20-Poly1305 (RFC 8439, where AAD and ciphertext are each zero-padded to 16-byte blocks followed by 64-bit little-endian lengths). Binding sequence numbers or transcript hashes into AAD prevents cross-context replay and header-substitution attacks; conversely, omitting version or algorithm identifiers from AAD can enable downgrade or confused-deputy attacks if keys are reused across contexts.
