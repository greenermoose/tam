ASPM (Active State Power Management)

A PCI Express (PCIe) power-saving mechanism that automatically puts idle high-speed expansion links into low-power sleep states without turning off the device.

Even when you aren’t actively saving a file to your SSD or downloading over Wi-Fi, the high-speed connection between those parts and your processor normally stays wide awake, using battery power. ASPM lets those connections take quick power naps whenever there’s a pause in traffic and wake up almost instantly when data needs to move again.

On Linux laptops, tuning ASPM can noticeably extend battery life, especially when NVMe SSDs, Wi-Fi cards, or Thunderbolt controllers support deep sleep states. You can inspect or override the kernel’s global policy at /sys/module/pcie_aspm/parameters/policy (default, performance, powersave, powersupersave), though some buggy PCIe devices or firmware tables disable ASPM or misbehave when forced into aggressive power saving.

What trade-off happens when you switch PCIe ASPM from performance to powersupersave?

Idle links enter deeper low-power states that cut wattage, at the cost of slightly higher wake-up latency when traffic resumes—and occasionally link instability on devices with buggy firmware.

ASPM governs hardware-initiated PCIe link-layer power transitions from L0 (active) into L0s (short-latency standby on one direction via electrical idle) and L1 (bidirectional clock/PLL shutdown), with optional L1.1/L1.2 substates using CLKREQ# sideband signaling to gate reference clocks and PLLs. Linux negotiates common exit-latency tolerances across root ports and endpoints via PCIe Link Capabilities/Control registers, honoring ACPI FADT ASPM flags unless overridden by pcie_aspm=force or per-device sysfs link/l1_aspm controls. Misconfigured exit latencies or broken CLKREQ# routing can cause AER correctable/uncorrectable errors, link drops, or excessive wake latency under bursty I/O.
