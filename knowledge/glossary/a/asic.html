ASIC (Application-Specific Integrated Circuit)

A custom silicon chip designed from the ground up to perform one dedicated task rather than general-purpose computing.

A regular computer processor is like a Swiss Army knife—it can run spreadsheets, web browsers, and games, though it isn’t specialized for any single one. An ASIC is like a single dedicated tool, such as a chef’s knife, built for one exact job—like driving a monitor, decoding video, or running a hardware security key—so it does that job faster and uses much less power.

On Linux systems, you encounter ASICs inside USB security tokens, network interface cards (offloading checksums or TLS), display controllers, and dedicated media encoders. Unlike an FPGA (which can be reprogrammed in the field) or a CPU (which executes software instructions), an ASIC’s logic is fixed at fabrication, trading flexibility for lower unit cost at high volume and much higher energy efficiency.

When would an engineer choose an FPGA over an ASIC for a specialized hardware task?

When production volume is too low to justify millions of dollars in ASIC mask and fabrication costs, or when the hardware logic needs to be updated after shipping, since FPGAs can be reconfigured in the field.

ASICs optimize PPA (power, performance, and area) by eliminating instruction fetch/decode overhead, general-purpose register files, and reconfigurable routing fabric (which imposes roughly an order-of-magnitude area and power penalty on FPGAs). Full-custom or standard-cell ASIC flows incur high non-recurring engineering (NRE) and photolithography mask costs, making them economical only at volume or when strict power/latency envelopes demand them. Because post-silicon logic bugs cannot be patched in hardware, ASICs rely heavily on formal verification, RTL simulation, and small programmable microcontrollers or ROM/patch-RAM hooks for firmware workarounds.
