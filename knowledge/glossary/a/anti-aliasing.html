Anti-aliasing

A graphics technique that smooths jagged, stair-stepped edges on curves and diagonal lines by blending edge pixels with the background.

Computer screens are made of tiny square dots called pixels. When you draw a slanted line or a curved letter on a grid of squares, the edges look blocky like a staircase. Anti-aliasing softens those steps by painting the edge squares in intermediate shades, tricking your eye into seeing a smooth curve.

On a Linux desktop, you most often tune anti-aliasing in font rendering (Fontconfig / FreeType) and in 3D games. For text, grayscale anti-aliasing blends black and white into shades of gray, while subpixel anti-aliasing (RGB) exploits the red, green, and blue sub-stripes of an LCD to triple horizontal sharpness—though subpixel rendering can show color fringing on rotated monitors or non-RGB OLED layouts.

Why can subpixel anti-aliasing look blurry or show colored fringes when you rotate a monitor 90 degrees?

Subpixel rendering assumes horizontal RGB stripes; rotating the display turns those stripes vertical, so the renderer’s color-channel offsets no longer align with the physical subpixels.

Aliasing occurs when a continuous signal (vector outlines or triangle edges) contains spatial frequencies above the Nyquist limit (half the pixel sampling rate), folding high frequencies into stair-stepping or shimmering. Spatial anti-aliasing mitigates this via pre-filtering (analytical pixel-area coverage masks, as in FreeType’s 256-level grayscale rasterizer) or post-filtering/multisampling: SSAA shades and samples at N× resolution before downsampling; MSAA evaluates coverage at multiple subpixel sample points per pixel while running the fragment shader once per pixel (failing on alpha-tested textures or deferred-shading edges); and TAA jitters subpixel projection matrices across frames and reprojects history buffers, trading ghosting on fast motion for low per-frame cost.
