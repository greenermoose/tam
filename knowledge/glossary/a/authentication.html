Authentication

The process of verifying that a user, device, or service really is who or what it claims to be.

Before a computer lets you read your email or change system settings, it needs to be sure you are really you and not someone else pretending. Typing a password, tapping a fingerprint sensor, or plugging in a security key are all ways of proving your identity—that check is authentication.

Authentication is commonly grouped into three factors: something you know (a password or PIN), something you have (a hardware token, SSH private key, or phone), and something you are (a fingerprint or face scan). Importantly, authentication only answers “who are you?”—deciding what you are allowed to do afterward is a separate step called authorization.

What is the difference between authentication and authorization?

Authentication verifies who a user or system is (for example, checking a password or SSH key), while authorization determines what that verified identity is permitted to access or do.

Formally, authentication establishes a principal’s identity via verifiable credentials, cryptographic challenge-response protocols (e.g., SSH public-key signatures, Kerberos tickets, FIDO2/WebAuthn assertions), or signed tokens (OIDC ID tokens, X.509 client certificates). On Linux, Pluggable Authentication Modules (PAM) decouple applications (sshd, login, sudo) from credential verification backends (pam_unix, pam_fido2, pam_sss). Robust protocols bind authentication to the session context (channel binding, origin checks, and nonces) to prevent relay, replay, and credential-phishing attacks.
