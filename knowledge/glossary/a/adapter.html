Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with A / Adapter

A hardware device or connector that converts power, signals, or physical plug formats so incompatible interfaces can work together.

Think of a travel plug adapter that lets a charger from one country fit into a wall outlet in another. In computing, an adapter does the same job for plugs, power, or signals: a power adapter turns wall electricity into safe laptop power, while a USB-C-to-HDMI adapter lets a modern laptop connect to an older monitor or projector.

Adapters fall into two practical categories: passive and active. Passive adapters only rewire compatible pins (for example, a simple physical jack adapter or a USB-C to DisplayPort cable using DisplayPort Alt Mode), whereas active adapters contain a chip that translates protocols or voltages (such as USB-C to Gigabit Ethernet, active DisplayPort-to-HDMI converters, or AC-to-DC USB-PD power bricks). In Linux, an active USB adapter shows up as its own device in lsusb and requires a driver, while a passive pin adapter is transparent to the OS.

Why does a cheap passive cable sometimes fail to connect a DisplayPort output to an HDMI monitor, while an active adapter works?

DisplayPort and HDMI use different electrical signaling and packet protocols; a passive adapter only works if the GPU port supports Dual-Mode DisplayPort (DP++) to output native HDMI signals, whereas an active adapter has an internal translator chip that works on any standard DisplayPort source.

Active adapters introduce protocol-conversion latency, bandwidth ceilings, and power-budget constraints: for example, USB-C docks negotiate alternate modes and wattage via USB Power Delivery (USB-PD) Structured VDM messages over CC lines while splitting SuperSpeed lanes between DisplayPort Alt Mode (2 or 4 lanes) and USB 3.x data. Network adapters (NICs) and host bus adapters (HBAs) similarly bridge PCIe or USB buses to physical media, exposing DMA rings, interrupt coalescing, and offload capabilities (ethtool -k) whose driver and firmware limits govern throughput and latency.
