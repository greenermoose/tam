Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with A / Antialiasing

Antialiasing softens the jagged edges of drawn shapes by giving edge pixels intermediate colors.

Screens draw with tiny square pixels, so a slanted letter edge can look like a staircase. Antialiasing paints the squares along that edge in partial shades of the text color, tricking the eye into seeing a smooth curve. It is why small letters on a screen look almost as smooth as printed ones.

Font rasterizers such as FreeType compute how much of each pixel a glyph outline covers and blend that coverage with the background. Grayscale antialiasing uses shades of gray; subpixel antialiasing uses the red, green and blue emitters of a liquid-crystal display as separate horizontal samples for extra sharpness. Subpixel rendering can show color fringes and depends on the panel's stripe order, so many desktops prefer grayscale.

Why can subpixel antialiasing show colored fringes?

It treats each red, green and blue emitter as a separate sample; a wrong stripe order or rotated panel makes the fringes visible.

Coverage rasterizers either sample the outline several times per pixel or compute exact area coverage, then apply gamma correction before blending in sRGB space; blending naive coverage makes edges look too light or too dark. Liquid-crystal subpixel filtering uses a short finite impulse response filter to reduce fringing and must adapt to rotated displays or non-RGB layouts. Hinting and antialiasing interact, because strong hinting collapses subtle coverage differences.
