Gamma

Gamma is a brightness curve that maps stored digital color values to the light levels shown on a screen.

Human eyes are much more sensitive to small differences in dark shadows than to small differences in bright highlights. If a computer stored light linearly, dark scenes would look blocky and banded. Gamma bends the scale so more steps are saved for the shadows and midtones, making photos, movies, and text look natural on a monitor instead of washed out or overly dark.

Most desktop images and web graphics use sRGB (roughly a 2.2 gamma curve) so that 8-bit values match human perception, while displays invert that encoding when emitting light. On Linux, tools like xgamma or compositor color settings adjust the display’s gamma ramp (via hardware LUTs), which is why changing gamma affects overall midtone brightness without moving pure black or pure white. Applying gamma encoding twice or skipping decoding makes images appear either crushed in the shadows or milky and washed out.

Why do image editors and 3D renderers convert sRGB images to linear space before blending colors?

Blending or blurring values directly in gamma-encoded space averages non-linear numbers, which produces unnaturally dark fringes between bright colors. Converting to linear light first ensures that mathematical mixing matches how physical light adds together.

In classical CRT displays, beam current followed an approximate power law (L ≈ V^γ with γ ≈ 2.2–2.5), so camera encoding applied an inverse opto-electronic transfer function (OETF) before quantization. Modern standards replace a pure power law with piecewise or perceptual transfer functions: sRGB uses a linear toe segment near zero joined to a 2.4 exponent (effective γ ≈ 2.2), while HDR pipelines use Perceptual Quantizer (PQ, SMPTE ST 2084) or Hybrid Log-Gamma (HLG, ITU-R BT.2100). In Linux DRM/KMS and Wayland color management, incorrect 1D LUT programming (GAMMA_LUT / DEGAMMA_LUT) or uncalibrated blending in non-linear framebuffer formats (DRM_FORMAT_XRGB8888 vs _SRGB) causes banding and hue shifts.
