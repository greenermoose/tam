Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with G / GTK (GIMP Toolkit)

A widely used open-source software toolkit for building graphical windows, buttons, menus, and other interface controls—most famously for GNOME and many Linux desktop applications.

When programmers build a desktop app, they usually do not draw every button, scrollbar, and text box from scratch. Instead, they use a ready-made kit of parts called a widget toolkit. GTK is one of the main toolkits on Linux, which is why many Linux apps share a similar, consistent look and feel.

GTK is written in C (with bindings for Python, Rust, Vala, and other languages) and contrasts with Qt, the other major Linux toolkit used by KDE. In day-to-day Linux use, you will often encounter both GTK3 and GTK4 applications: GTK3 apps commonly read styling and preferences from ~/.config/gtk-3.0/ and gsettings, while GTK4 (especially when paired with libadwaita) follows stricter, built-in adaptive styling and relies more heavily on XDG Desktop Portals for file choosers and dark-mode preferences.

Why might changing a custom GTK3 theme fail to change the appearance of a newer GTK4/libadwaita app?

GTK4 apps using libadwaita intentionally ignore arbitrary third-party GTK3 CSS themes to avoid broken layouts, relying instead on standardized color-scheme preferences (such as light/dark via XDG portals) and libadwaita’s own styling API.

Licensed under LGPL-2.1+, GTK builds on GLib and GObject for its type system, signals, and main event loop, Pango for internationalized text shaping/layout, and AT-SPI for accessibility. Architecturally, GTK4 replaces GTK3’s Cairo-centric immediate drawing and X11-first assumptions with GSK (GTK Scene Kit), which constructs render-node trees and dispatches them to GPU-accelerated Vulkan or OpenGL renderers (with a Cairo software fallback). It targets Wayland natively (using xdg-shell and fractional-scaling protocols) alongside X11, macOS, and Windows backends, while isolating sandboxed Flatpak/containerized apps through D-Bus XDG Portals.
