Guide / Glossary A–Z / Terms beginning with G / GPIO - General-Purpose Input/Output

A general-purpose pin on a chip or circuit board that software can configure either to read a voltage signal or to send one out.

Think of a GPIO pin as an unassigned electrical socket on a tiny computer board, such as a Raspberry Pi. Unlike a USB port or a headphone jack, it has no built-in job—it can be wired to a button so the computer knows when you press it, or wired to a small light so a program can turn the light on and off.

In Linux, user-space programs interact with GPIO lines through the character device interface (/dev/gpiochip*) using tools such as gpiodetect, gpioget, and gpioset from libgpiod, rather than the deprecated /sys/class/gpio sysfs interface. A pin’s behavior depends on its direction (input vs. output), active state (active-high vs. active-low), and internal pull-up or pull-down resistors that prevent an unconnected input from floating between voltages.

Why does an unconnected input pin often read random 0s and 1s if no pull resistor is enabled?

Without a pull-up or pull-down resistor tying the pin to a known reference voltage, the high-impedance input acts like a small antenna and picks up ambient electrical noise, causing its logic level to float unpredictably.

SoC pin controllers (pinctrl) typically multiplex each physical pad across several functions (GPIO, I2C, SPI, UART) and expose electrical controls for bias, slew rate, drive strength, open-drain/open-source modes, and hardware debouncing. The Linux gpio-cdev ABI (v2, GPIO_V2_GET_LINE_IOCTL) handles atomic multi-line requests, edge-event timestamps from CLOCK_MONOTONIC or CLOCK_REALTIME, and automatic release on file-descriptor close—avoiding the race conditions and leaked exports of the legacy sysfs API. Care is required when mixing voltage domains (e.g., 3.3 V vs. 1.8 V logic) or driving inductive loads without flyback protection, which can latch up or destroy the pad’s ESD diodes.
