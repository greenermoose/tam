Flicker, persistence and eye comfort

How changing light and viewing conditions can affect what you see and how comfortable a screen feels.

Several things can change over time

A screen can update its picture, vary its light output, or retain a trace of a previous image. Your visual system also adapts to light and can produce afterimages. These are different processes, and a headache during screen use does not by itself identify their cause.

Frequency, decay and comfort

Goal: distinguish screen refresh from flicker and find practical ways to make a screen more comfortable.

Worked example

A display advertised as 60 Hz updates its image 60 times per second. Its backlight may be steady or may be controlled by pulses at a different frequency. The refresh number alone does not say which.

Try it

Adjust text size, reduce glare, set a comfortable brightness and take regular breaks. Compare your comfort during ordinary use. Do not use flashing patterns to provoke symptoms.

If it goes wrong

If discomfort or headaches persist, consult an eye-care professional. Sudden or severe symptoms need appropriate medical attention; do not assume the screen is the cause.

Optical frequency describes the oscillation of a light wave and relates to its wavelength. Flicker means changes in light output over time, typically many orders of magnitude slower. Refresh is how often an image updates. Pulse-width modulation is one possible way to dim a light by changing its on and off times. CRT phosphors glow and decay after excitation; e-paper image retention and visual afterimages are different.

Check yourself

Does a 60 Hz refresh specification tell you the backlight flicker frequency?

No. Image updates and light modulation are separate; the specification alone does not establish the backlight waveform.

Temporal response and uncertainty

Temporal light modulation has frequency, modulation depth, waveform and duty cycle. Visibility and effects depend on luminance, contrast, field size, movement and individual sensitivity. Eye movements can expose temporal artifacts even when a stationary observer sees no ordinary flicker. There is no universal refresh rate that guarantees comfort.

Dry eyes, blinking, glare, focusing effort, posture and other factors can contribute to symptoms. Research on blue-filtering lenses has not established a general cure for screen eyestrain. Display persistence, phosphor decay, electrophoretic retention and visual adaptation have distinct mechanisms and timescales.
