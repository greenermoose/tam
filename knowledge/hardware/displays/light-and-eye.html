How the eye turns light into signals that the brain can interpret.

Light reaches the retina

Light is electromagnetic radiation. Its wavelength is the distance between successive wave peaks; its frequency is how many cycles pass each second. In empty space the two are related: shorter wavelengths have higher frequencies. Human-visible light is only a small part of the electromagnetic spectrum.

Your cornea and lens bend incoming light to form an image on the retina, a light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye. The iris changes the size of the pupil, controlling how much light enters. The retina sends signals through the optic nerve to the brain.

Rods, cones and color

Goal: explain why a screen can make many color sensations with three primary lights.

Try it

Look at a familiar image on a screen, then step farther away. Fine colored details may become harder to distinguish. Do not look at the sun or a bright light source for this exercise.

If it goes wrong

If the pixels are too small to see, use a magnified photograph of a screen rather than moving uncomfortably close. This exercise does not test eyesight.

Rods help us see in dim light. Most people have three cone types, called S, M and L for their relative sensitivity to short, medium and long wavelengths. Their broad sensitivity ranges overlap: a cone responds to many wavelengths, not one exact frequency. The visual system compares their responses.

Different mixtures of light can produce similar cone responses and look alike; this is a metameric match. Three independent primary lights can match many color sensations for a typical observer, but a real display cannot reproduce every visible color. Its available mixtures form its color gamut. People with different cone responses can see different matches.

Check yourself

Does each cone respond to only one wavelength?

No. Each cone type has a broad, overlapping range of sensitivity. The brain compares their responses.

From spectrum to perception

A photoreceptor response depends on the light spectrum weighted by its sensitivity. A single cone cannot distinguish wavelength from intensity on its own. Three cone responses provide a useful three-dimensional color-matching model for a standard observer under defined conditions, but physical spectra can differ while producing a match. Negative primary amounts in some mathematical matches show why a triangle of three real nonnegative display primaries cannot cover every visible chromaticity.

Rods, cone distributions, lighting, adaptation and color-vision differences affect appearance. Color science distinguishes a physical spectrum, a defined colorimetric match and subjective appearance.
