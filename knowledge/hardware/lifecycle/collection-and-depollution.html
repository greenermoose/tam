Guide / Hardware / Lifecycle and environment / Collection and depollution

When a device can no longer be used, collection systems decide whether it becomes someone else's parts or a hazardous waste problem. Depollution removes parts that can harm workers or the environment before any shredding.

Where a device goes after use

Many places run a take-back system: a route that accepts old equipment so its materials can be handled properly. Incoming goods are sorted. Anything still useful is set aside; the rest has hazardous parts removed before machines break it up.

Why it matters

Old devices can contain a battery, a mercury lamp or a lead-containing solder joint. If they are shredded without care, those substances can reach dust, air or water. Sorting first also saves parts that could still be used.

How to think about it

Think of a hospital triage desk. Some arrivals can go home, some need a specific treatment first, and only then does the rest of the work begin. The hazardous pieces are tagged and removed before the general processing line.

New words

Electronic waste
Discarded electrical or electronic products; also called e-waste.
Extended producer responsibility
A policy approach that makes producers responsible for collecting or treating their products at end of life.
Depollution
Removing hazardous parts from a device before it is broken up.
Hazardous fraction
The portion of a waste stream that needs special handling.

Worked example

A crate of returned equipment arrives at a collection site. The operator works in this order.

  1. Sort out devices that still work; these are reuse candidates, not shredding feed.
  2. Remove batteries and tape their terminals so they cannot short during handling.
  3. Take out mercury lamps and switches intact, and keep LCD backlights whole.
  4. Set aside CRT glass and assemblies, toner and capacitors.
  5. Only then send the remaining material towards shredding.

The ordering matters: once a device is shredded, the hazardous pieces are mixed into the rest and are much harder to control.

Try it

Find the take-back route available where you live and write down two kinds of device it accepts. For one of them, list the parts you would expect depollution to remove before shredding. Keep the note short and do not open a device to check.

If it goes wrong

You assume everything collected is recycled.
Collection is only the first step. Some material is recovered, some is downcycled and some is discarded; documentation for a batch is the evidence.
You break an LCD backlight during removal.
Mercury in some backlights is a hazard. Keep the assembly intact and follow the site's procedure.
You shred a device with its battery still fitted.
Damaged batteries can short, heat or ignite. Isolate and tape terminals before mechanical processing.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

Why sort for reuse before depollution?

A working device is a product, not waste feed. Sending it straight to shredding destroys the value of the whole assembly and wastes the resources already used to make it. Reuse candidates are therefore separated at the inbound gate.

Why are backlights and switches treated carefully?

Some contain mercury. If the glass breaks, mercury can escape into dust or the air. Keeping these parts intact during depollution keeps a hazardous substance out of the shredded stream.

How it works

EPA guidance warns that mercury in backlights, lead in solder and brominated flame retardants in housings, boards, cables and keyboards create dust and smelting risks if devices are processed without control. Take-back and extended producer responsibility arrangements fund and organise collection; UNEP take-back guidance describes why producer involvement matters.

Scale figures are flows, not fate. The Global E-waste Monitor reports 62 Mt of e-waste in 2022 and only 22.3% documented as formally collected and recycled. WEEE Directive 2012/19/EU sets a 65% collection goal from 2019, measured against the average electrical and electronic equipment placed on the market in the previous three years, or alternatively 85% of WEEE generated; category recovery targets also apply.

Edge cases and failure modes

  • A collection target measures what is gathered, not how much material is finally recovered.
  • Definitions of e-waste differ between schemes, so figures are not always directly comparable.
  • Informal processing can recover some metals while leaving hazardous fractions uncontrolled.

Check yourself

Why does a high collection rate not prove good material recovery?

Collection measures material entering a system. What happens next depends on sorting, depollution, downstream contracts and plant capability. A batch can be collected and still be downcycled, discarded or processed without full controls. Ask for final disposition evidence, not only an inbound total.
