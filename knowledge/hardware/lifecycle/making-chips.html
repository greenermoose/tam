Guide / Hardware / Lifecycle and environment / Making chips

A chip begins with a material much like sand and ends as a small tile carrying circuits. The work has two broad halves: preparing a blank wafer and building circuit layers on it, then cutting and packaging the finished pieces.

From sand to a small tile

Silicon dioxide is refined to a very pure form of silicon. The silicon is melted and pulled into a single-crystal rod, which is sliced into thin discs. Each disc is smoothed until it is flat enough for circuits to be built on it.

Why it matters

A single chip can hold billions of tiny switches. They are not carved one by one. Layers are added and patterned over the whole disc at once, so the same steps repeat many times before the disc is finished.

How to think about it

Imagine making a many-layered print. On each layer you coat the surface, shine light through a pattern, change the places the light selected, and smooth the surface for the next layer. When all layers are done, you cut the sheet into tiles.

New words

Wafer
A thin, polished disc of silicon on which chips are built.
Fab
A factory that builds circuits on wafers.
Die
One small rectangular piece cut from a finished wafer; it becomes a chip after packaging.
Photoresist
A light-sensitive coating that carries a pattern during fabrication.

The packaged die is later mounted on a printed circuit board with other parts.

Worked example

Follow one wafer through a simplified sequence. The real recipe repeats these steps many times and differs between makers.

  1. Grow or deposit a thin film, often by oxidation.
  2. Coat with photoresist and expose a pattern. Deep ultraviolet light passes through a mask or reticle; advanced fabs use extreme ultraviolet at 13.5 nm.
  3. Change the exposed regions by doping, usually ion implantation followed by heat.
  4. Deposit more thin films and add metal connections.
  5. Remove unwanted material with wet or plasma etching.
  6. Flatten the surface by chemical-mechanical planarisation, then measure the result.
  7. When every layer is complete, dice the wafer into dies.
  8. Attach each die to a substrate, interconnect it, and test it.

The lesson is that patterning, changing material and measuring repeat; the wafer does not move through a single one-way line.

Try it

Write the eight steps above from memory on a scrap of paper. Then mark which ones could repeat on the same wafer. Check your list against the worked example and note one step you would want to look up in a process specification.

If it goes wrong

You put dicing before the circuit layers.
Dicing is the last step of wafer processing. A blank die would carry no circuits.
You think photolithography removes material.
It transfers a pattern into photoresist. Etching is the step that removes unwanted material afterwards.
You assume every fab uses EUV.
EUV at 13.5 nm is used in advanced fabs. Many devices are made with DUV patterning through a mask or reticle.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

Why is the wafer polished before circuits are built?

Lithography needs a very flat surface to focus a pattern across the disc. Slicing leaves roughness, so lapping and polishing prepare a wafer that the later layers can use.

What is the difference between a die and a chip you can solder?

A die is the bare piece cut from the wafer. It is attached to a substrate, interconnected and tested to become a packaged chip that can be mounted on a board.

How it works

Chip manufacturing describes the front-end sequence as a repeating cycle rather than a single pass. Each patterned layer must line up with the ones below it, so metrology feeds corrections into later steps. Packaging is a separate back-end discipline: die attach, interconnect and test are governed by assembly practices such as those described in IPC standards.

Edge cases and failure modes

  • EUV needs a different light source and mask approach from DUV; it is not a drop-in replacement in every fab.
  • A defect in one layer can be repeated by every later layer. Metrology catches drift before a whole disc is lost.
  • Package choices change thermal and electrical behaviour. The same die can appear in several package forms.

Check yourself

Why does planarisation matter after metallisation?

Metal and film steps leave an uneven surface. Later lithography has a limited depth of focus, so a layer that is not flattened can print poorly or fail to align. Chemical-mechanical planarisation restores a surface the next pattern can use.
