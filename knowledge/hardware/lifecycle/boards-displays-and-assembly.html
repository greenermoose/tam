Guide / Hardware / Lifecycle and environment / Boards, displays and assembly

Chips become useful when they are mounted on a board and linked to other parts. This page follows a printed circuit board through fabrication, a liquid-crystal display through cell assembly, and a set of parts through final assembly.

Layers joined into a product

A board starts as a sheet of woven glass fibre in epoxy with copper on its faces. Circuits are drawn into that copper. Small parts are soldered onto the board, a display panel is built from layers of glass and light, and the parts are fitted into a case.

Why it matters

The way a product is joined decides what can be repaired. A part that is plugged into a slot can often be replaced with a simple tool. A part soldered or bonded to a board needs more care, and sometimes specialist work.

How to think about it

Think of a city built in layers. The board is the road network, the soldered parts are the buildings, the display is a window, and the final assembly puts walls around everything and connects the services.

New words

FR-4
The common glass-fibre epoxy material used for copper-clad boards.
Solder mask
A protective coating over copper, leaving openings where parts are attached.
Surface-mount part
A small component soldered onto the surface of a board.
Backlight
A light source behind a liquid-crystal panel; the panel itself does not glow.
Box build
The final stage that fits a built board and other parts into a product.

Match a step to a fault

Worked example

Three observations from a production line, each traced to a step rather than guessed at.

  1. A board has no continuity through a hole. The drilling and desmear steps prepare the hole; plating then lays down copper. A break points to that chain, not to the solder mask.
  2. Some surface-mount joints are dull and cracked. Paste stencilling, placement and reflow are the suspects; check the reflow profile before blaming the parts.
  3. A display is evenly dark with no image. Check the backlight, polarisers and driver bonds before assuming the pixels have failed.

The habit is to ask which step could produce exactly this evidence.

Try it

Look at a device you can open safely or at a clear product photograph. List three parts that plug in and three that are soldered or bonded. For one soldered part, write which assembly step would have attached it.

If it goes wrong

You assume every part can be unplugged.
Many parts are soldered or bonded. Check the board view and service information before applying force.
You blame the chip for a dark screen.
A backlight, a polariser or a driver bond can produce the same symptom. Work through the layers from light outwards.
You treat one maker's sequence as universal.
The exact order varies by maker. Learn the purpose of each step so you can adapt to a different line.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

Why is solder mask printed after copper is etched?

Etching removes unwanted copper to leave the circuit. The mask then protects the copper that remains and leaves openings at the places where parts will be soldered. Printing it earlier would not define those circuits.

Which assembly method suits a through-hole connector?

Wave soldering is still common for through-hole parts: the board passes over a wave of molten solder. Surface-mount parts are usually stencilled with paste, placed and reflowed instead.

Fabrication, panel build and box build

Reference

FR-4 is woven glass fibre in an epoxy copper-clad laminate, specified for boards by IPC-4101. A typical subtractive PCB flow is:

  1. Clean the laminate and apply etch resist.
  2. Drill holes, desmear the hole walls, and plate through-holes and circuitry.
  3. Strip the resist and etch away unwanted copper.
  4. Print solder mask and apply a surface finish.

Surface-mount assembly (SMT) stencils solder paste, places parts and reflows the board. IPC-A-610 defines the acceptability criteria for the finished assembly. Wave soldering remains common for through-hole parts.

An LCD build makes TFT-array glass and colour-filter glass separately, injects and laminates liquid crystal between them, attaches polarisers, bonds driver integrated circuits, fits the backlight and tests illumination. Transparent conductors such as indium tin oxide form pixel electrodes. An OLED panel uses emissive materials instead of a liquid-crystal layer and does not need the same backlight.

Box build adds the PCBA, heatsinks, fans, cables and firmware loading, then runs functional and burn-in tests. The exact sequence varies by maker.

How it works

Material choices connect the board to the wider supply chain: USGS mineral summaries describe where conductors come from, while EPA guidance on PCBs and display materials explain why certain substances need control during manufacture and at end of life.

Edge cases and failure modes

  • Desmear and plating faults appear only after drilling, so a visual check of the finished mask cannot find them.
  • Reflow can hide voids inside joints; acceptability criteria describe what is visible and what needs further inspection.
  • A bonded display stack may be excellent optically but awkward to repair; assembly ease and optical quality pull in different directions.

Check yourself

Why can a panel pass an illumination test and still show no image?

Illumination checks the backlight path. Image formation also needs polarisers, transparent electrodes, an intact liquid-crystal layer and working driver bonds. A fault in any of those can leave a uniformly lit panel with no pixels changing.
