Guide / Hardware / Lifecycle and environment / The life of a computer

Every computer has a story before it reaches you and after you stop using it. Materials are gathered, parts are made, the product is assembled, used, repaired and passed on, and finally its materials are recovered or discarded.

One device, many stages

A computer is not made in one place or at one time. Sand-like material becomes silicon, chips are built on thin slices of it, and other parts are made and joined. The finished machine may serve several owners before its parts are separated again.

Why it matters

What you do at one stage changes what happens later. A device that is repaired and passed on stays a product for longer. A device that is discarded without care becomes electronic waste, a stream that needs careful handling because it contains both useful materials and hazardous parts.

How to think about it

Think of a journey with checkpoints. At each checkpoint someone asks a different question: where did this material come from, how was it made, can it be fixed, who can use it next, and what can be recovered at the end. No single badge answers all those questions.

New words

Electronic waste
Discarded electrical or electronic products; also called e-waste.
Life-cycle assessment
A method for studying environmental effects across the stages of a product's life.
Product carbon footprint
A result that reports greenhouse-gas emissions attributed to a product within a stated boundary.
Depollution
Removing hazardous parts before a device is broken up.
Downcycling
Reusing a material in a lower-value product rather than the same product again.

Worked example

A school receives a box of donated laptops. The box is not simply "waste" or "reuse"; each machine needs a decision.

  1. A laptop boots to the desktop and its battery holds a charge. It stays in use after its data is handled.
  2. A second laptop boots only with the charger attached. Its battery is a replaceable module, so a repair is worth checking.
  3. A third laptop shows no signs of life. A first-line check may still find a display or power fault rather than a dead board.
  4. A fourth has no repair path. It goes to collection, where reuse candidates are sorted out before depollution.

The point of the example is that the stage is decided by evidence, not by the label on the box.

Try it

Choose one device you know and write down its current stage. Name one observation that supports your choice and one question you would ask before moving it to the next stage. Keep the note short; you are practising the decision, not the device.

If it goes wrong

You called a working second-hand device waste.
Waste status depends on whether it is discarded and on local rules. A device passed to another user is still a product.
You assumed one number describes the whole life.
Results depend on the boundary, the product and its actual use. Ask what a figure covers before comparing two claims.
You treated "recycled" as proof of a new product.
The output may be downcycled into a lower-value use. Ask what was recovered and where it went.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

Why check a device before calling it e-waste?

Because its next stage is not obvious. A working machine, a machine with one failed module and a machine for parts take different routes. A first check can keep a usable product in service and avoids sending hazardous parts to the wrong stream.

What question does a life-cycle view add to a repair decision?

It asks what is preserved, not only what is replaced. Repair often keeps more of the existing product's resources in use, but the useful comparison is product-specific: the model, the failed part and the expected remaining life all matter.

How it works

A life-cycle assessment defines a functional unit and a system boundary, then inventories inputs and outputs across the stages. ISO 14040 and ISO 14044 set the methodology. A product carbon footprint is one result within such a boundary, not a complete measure of every environmental effect. Do not attach a universal per-computer mass of material to this page: figures depend on the product mix and the study.

Comparisons must be like for like. Repair and upgrades usually save more of the existing product's embodied resources than buying new, but the useful comparison is product-specific. A machine with a cheap replaceable battery and years of service left is a different case from one with a failed soldered processor. Global context matters too: the Global E-waste Monitor reports flows at world scale, not the fate of one device.

Edge cases and failure modes

  • A device held for parts may be counted as reuse by one scheme and as waste by another; check the definition in use.
  • A "recycled" claim can hide downcycling or energy recovery; ask what output was made.
  • Rules differ by region. The right to repair is not one worldwide law; the EU Repair Directive covers particular goods under particular conditions.

Check yourself

Why does a life-cycle comparison need a stated boundary?

Because emissions and material flows shift between stages. A comparison that counts factory energy but ignores use, transport or end of life can reverse a conclusion. State the functional unit and stages included, then compare only claims built on the same basis.
