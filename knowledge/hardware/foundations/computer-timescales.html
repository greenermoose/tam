This page explains how fast different computer operations happen, and uses a human-scale analogy to make nanoseconds, microseconds, and milliseconds easier to compare.

Before this

Digital logic and binary computation

Computers work on timescales far smaller than human perception. A processor may complete billions of tiny steps each second, while a disk seek or network round trip can feel instant to a person and still be enormously slow to a CPU. Learning these timescales helps you understand why caches exist, why refresh rate matters, why latency feels different from bandwidth, and what changing a clock speed can and cannot do.

Why computer time is hard to feel

People are good at judging seconds, minutes, and days. Computers work in nanoseconds and microseconds. The numbers are so small that they all sound like "basically instant" - but to a computer, they are very different.

A human-scale analogy

Imagine we stretch time so that 1 nanosecond (1 ns) becomes 1 second. In that scale:

Real computer timeHuman-scaled time (1 ns = 1 s)
1 nanosecond (ns)1 second
1 microsecond (µs)16 minutes 40 seconds
1 millisecond (ms)11.6 days
1 second (s)31.7 years

This is why a CPU waiting for main memory is like a person taking a quick break, while waiting for a disk or network can be like waiting days or years in that same stretched scale.

New words

Hertz (Hz)
One cycle per second. A 3 GHz CPU clock ticks 3 billion times per second.
Millisecond (ms)
One thousandth of a second (0.001 s).
Microsecond (µs)
One millionth of a second (0.000001 s).
Nanosecond (ns)
One billionth of a second (0.000000001 s).

Hertz, refresh rate, and overclocking

Hertz means cycles per second. It is used for many things: CPU clock speed (GHz), monitor refresh rate (Hz), audio sample rates, and timer ticks. The same unit does not mean the same kind of work.

A 60 Hz display redraws every 16.7 ms. A 120 Hz display redraws every 8.3 ms. That can make motion look smoother, but only if the system can produce frames fast enough.

Overclocking means running a component faster than its default rated speed. More clock cycles per second can improve performance, but it also increases heat, power use, and the chance of instability. The gain is limited by cooling, voltage, workload, and the rest of the system.

If it goes wrong

The CPU frequency fields are missing
Some drivers do not expose minimum and maximum MHz through lscpu. Treat them as unavailable; an instantaneous clock reading does not establish the processor's limits.
The display-mode path does not exist
Connector names vary. Check the connected output in desktop display settings or inspect /sys/class/drm/ for its actual name. A missing path is not a timing measurement.

Check yourself

If 1 nanosecond is scaled to 1 second, about how long is 1 millisecond?

About 11.6 days. A millisecond is one million nanoseconds, so 1,000,000 seconds is roughly 11.6 days.

Why can a higher refresh rate make motion look smoother even when the image content is the same?

Because the display updates more often (for example every 8.3 ms at 120 Hz instead of 16.7 ms at 60 Hz), so movement and camera pans have smaller visual steps if new frames are available in time.

How it works

A clocked digital system advances work on clock edges; cycle time is the inverse of frequency (3 GHz → ~333 ps). But end-to-end performance is shaped by the whole path: pipeline depth, IPC, cache hierarchy, memory latency/bandwidth, storage queues, scheduler delay, and network RTT/jitter. Overclocking raises frequency/voltage margins, but may hit thermal/power limits and reduce stability; undervolting/DVFS trade the opposite direction. Display timing is a fixed budget per frame (16.7 ms at 60 Hz), so missed deadlines become dropped frames or stutter.

Edge cases

  • Averages hide tails: p99 latency matters more than mean for interactive feel.
  • Units collide: Hz can describe CPU clocks, refresh rate, PWM, or audio sampling - same unit, different subsystem.
  • Faster clock is not always faster: thermal throttling, memory bottlenecks, or lower IPC can erase gains.
  • Human perception is not uniform: people notice input lag, jitter, and frame pacing differently than raw throughput.

Check yourself

Why is a human-scaled latency table useful, and what can it hide?

It makes orders of magnitude intuitive by mapping ns/µs/ms to familiar time. It can hide variability, tail latency, queueing effects, and the fact that modern CPUs overlap work (out-of-order execution, prefetching, pipelining), so a "memory access" cost is not always paid as a simple blocking wait.
