Guide / Learn step by step / From raw materials to a second life

This path follows a computer from the materials inside it to the point where another owner can use it again. Each stage names the lens to read with, so you can move from a plain overview to precise standards and failure modes.

The tasks are decisions you can make on paper. Nothing here asks you to open a device, remove a battery or change a system.

Outcome: After this path, you can judge manufacture, repair, reuse and recycling claims from raw materials to a second life.

Prerequisites: none beyond reading

Time: About 50 minutes, including checkpoints and the capstone.

If you're stuck: read the program manuals with tam man <command>, list built-in help with tam help, or search this guide with tam search -- <term>.

Stage 1: See the whole life

Start with the sequence, then name the materials that make each stage possible. Knowing the stages helps you place a device and ask the right question at each checkpoint.

  1. The life of a computer — materials, manufacture, use, repair, reuse and recovery in one view.
  2. Computer materials — the metals, polymers and display chemicals that do the work.

Checkpoint

Answer before you go on.

Why is a working second-hand device not simply waste?

Its next stage is decision, not assumption. If it works or can be repaired and its data is handled, it can stay a product for another owner. Waste status depends on whether it is discarded and on local rules.

Expected sandbox state: No sandbox in this stage; no simulated files or output are expected.

Stage 2: How it is made

Read the two manufacturing stages in order: first the chip on its wafer, then the board, display and finished product. Notice that both processes repeat steps and measure the result.

  1. Making chips — wafers, repeated patterning, doping, metallisation and packaged dies.
  2. Boards, displays and assembly — circuit boards, soldered parts, display panels and box build.

Checkpoint

Answer before you go on.

Why does a dark screen not prove the main board has failed?

Assembly knowledge separates the layers. The backlight, polarisers, display cable and driver bonds can all produce a dark screen while the board still reaches the firmware setup screen. A flashlight test separates backlight faults from a failed panel.

Expected sandbox state: No sandbox in this stage; no simulated files or output are expected.

Stage 3: Keep it working

Now use what you know about assembly to diagnose a fault, then prepare a repaired device for another owner. Evidence comes before parts, and a backup comes before sanitisation.

  1. Diagnosing and repairing — first-line checks, static control, replaceable modules and rework.
  2. Reuse and data — back up, sanitise with a chosen category and verify the result.

Checkpoint

Answer before you go on.

Why must verification come after sanitisation?

A method only counts if it covered every storage location and completed successfully. Verification is the evidence of that result. It also gives the next owner a short record of what was done and what was checked.

Expected sandbox state: No sandbox in this stage; no simulated files or output are expected.

Stage 4: End of life

When a device can no longer serve a user, collection and depollution protect the next stage. Separation and refining then recover what the plant and the market can actually use.

  1. Collection and depollution — sorting, take-back and the hazardous parts removed before shredding.
  2. Material recovery — magnetic, eddy-current, optical and density separation, then metal refining.

Checkpoint

Answer before you go on.

Why does a collection total not prove that material was recycled?

Collection measures what entered the system. The final outcome depends on sorting, depollution and downstream contracts. A collected batch can be reused, downcycled, discarded or processed without full controls, so ask for evidence of final disposition.

Expected sandbox state: No sandbox in this stage; no simulated files or output are expected.

Stage 5: Judge the claims

Finish by reading the labels and standards that surround all these stages. The key skill is naming the category of a claim before deciding what it proves.

  1. Standards and labels — product content, management systems, assessment methods and facility certifications.

Checkpoint

Answer before you go on.

Why is a facility certification not a product label?

A certification applies to a place and its documented practices. A product label applies to a named product category. Knowing which one you have prevents a claim about recycling operations being read as a claim about every device a company sells.

Expected sandbox state: No sandbox in this stage; no simulated files or output are expected.

Capstone: plan for a small office refresh

A small office has ten laptops. Two work well, five need a replaceable module and three have no repair path. Write a one-page plan that gives each group a stage, names the evidence needed before a decision, and states what a vendor would have to show to support a recycling claim. Use no grams-per-device figures.

Reveal a sample answer

Place the two working laptops in reuse, with a backup, a verified sanitisation category and a note of the next owner. Put the five repairable laptops through first-line checks: charger and battery, signs of life, firmware setup and a flashlight test. Name the module to replace, apply static control, and only then repeat the checks. Send the three unrepairable machines to collection, after confirming that reuse candidates are sorted out and that batteries, mercury parts and other hazardous fractions are removed before shredding. For a recycling claim, ask which facility handled the batch, what downstream tracking exists to final disposition or SERI registration, and which fractions were recovered, downcycled or discarded. Avoid product comparison claims without stating the model, the failed part and the expected remaining life.
