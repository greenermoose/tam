Sorting puts items in order, such as names alphabetically or numbers from smallest to largest. Ordered data makes many later tasks much easier.

Why order helps

A sorting algorithm arranges items by a rule. Once a list is ordered, finding, merging and comparing groups become simpler.

Why it matters

Different sorts make different trade-offs. Some are quick on large lists; some use little extra memory; some keep equal items in their original order.

How to think about it

Imagine alphabetising index cards. You can repeatedly find the smallest card, or split the pile into smaller piles and merge them. Both end ordered, but they do different amounts of work.

What is a stable sort?

A sort that keeps equal-key items in the same relative order they had in the input.

Why might stability matter?

When records carry extra fields, preserving input order among ties can keep earlier decisions meaningful.

How it works

Comparison sorts have an O(n log n) lower bound in the general case. Counting and radix sorts can beat it when keys come from a bounded alphabet. Practical libraries often use hybrid algorithms: quicksort-style partitioning with insertion sort for small runs and guards against bad pivots.

Edge cases and failure modes

  • Already-sorted input is worst case for naive pivot choices.
  • Comparison functions that violate transitivity can corrupt results.
  • Stability costs memory or bookkeeping in many implementations.
  • External sorts must account for I/O, not just comparisons.
