Large language models turn text into small pieces, learn patterns from them, and use those patterns to choose a next piece. Several algorithms work together to do that.

What a language model does

A large language model, or LLM, predicts what text is likely to come next. It has learned those predictions from a great deal of examples.

Why it matters

That one prediction, repeated many times, can produce an answer, a summary or a story. Understanding the stages helps you judge what a model can and cannot do.

How to think about it

Imagine writing a sentence with a helpful autocomplete that has read many books. It does not know your thoughts; it chooses a likely next word from patterns it learned.

What just happened

Text is split into tokens, tokens become vectors, transformer layers combine context, and logits score possible next tokens. A selection step chooses one and the process repeats. The file is a sample map of the pipeline, not a trace from a live model.

What are tokens?

Small pieces of text, such as words or parts of words, that the model works with.

What do logits represent?

Scores for possible next tokens before they are turned into probabilities and sampled.

How it works

Training adjusts weights with gradient descent so predicted token probabilities match the training data. Inference uses a key-value cache to avoid recomputing earlier context for each new token. Attention cost grows with context length, which is why long prompts are slower and use more memory.

Edge cases and failure modes

  • Rare tokens and unusual languages can be represented poorly.
  • Floating-point precision changes output slightly across hardware.
  • Context windows truncate or evict earlier tokens.
  • Probabilities are not guarantees; plausible false output is possible.
