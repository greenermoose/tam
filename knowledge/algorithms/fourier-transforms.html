Fourier transforms

A Fourier transform describes a signal as a combination of waves. Instead of asking "what is the value at each moment?", it asks "how much of each frequency is present?"

The big idea

Many complicated signals are made by adding simpler waves together. A musical chord is several notes at once. A picture edge is a sharp change that can be described by many spatial frequencies. A Fourier transform is a mathematical way to move between the time view and the frequency view.

Why it matters

The frequency view makes some problems easier. Noise can be reduced by removing unwanted frequencies. Audio formats can compress sound by keeping the frequencies humans notice most. Images, radio, medical scans, and scientific measurements all use Fourier ideas.

How to think about it

Imagine a smoothie. In the time view, you taste the blended drink at each sip. In the frequency view, you know how much strawberry, banana, and yogurt went in. The Fourier transform is the recipe-finding step: it tells you how much of each ingredient wave appears in the mixture.

From time samples to frequency bins

Goal: understand what the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) computes and how to interpret its output.

Worked example

A computer usually has samples, not a continuous wave. If you sample a signal N times, the DFT produces N frequency values. Each value says how strongly a particular frequency matches the signal.

$ python3 - <<'PY'
import math
N = 8
# signal = 1.0*sin(2*pi*1*k/N) + 0.5*sin(2*pi*2*k/N)
x = [math.sin(2*math.pi*1*k/N) + 0.5*math.sin(2*math.pi*2*k/N) for k in range(N)]
for m in range(N):
    re = sum(x[k]*math.cos(2*math.pi*m*k/N) for k in range(N))
    im = -sum(x[k]*math.sin(2*math.pi*m*k/N) for k in range(N))
    mag = math.hypot(re, im)
    print(f"bin {m}: magnitude {mag:.3f}")

Illustrative output from a simple DFT calculation. Bins 1 and 7 mirror each other for a real signal, as do bins 2 and 6.

What just happened

  1. The input had two sine waves: one at frequency 1 and a weaker one at frequency 2.
  2. The DFT compared the signal against sine and cosine waves at each bin.
  3. Large magnitudes appeared at the matching bins, showing which frequencies were present.
  4. The mirrored bins are normal for real-valued input; the useful information is in the first half plus the Nyquist details when applicable.

Try it

Change the signal to use only frequency 2, or add a constant offset. Notice how the spectrum changes. Keep N small so you can inspect every bin.

If it goes wrong

The strongest bin is not where you expect
Check that the frequency is an integer number of cycles over N samples. If it is not, energy spreads across nearby bins; this is called spectral leakage.
You see unexpected symmetry
Real input signals produce conjugate-symmetric DFT output. That is expected, not an error.

Check yourself

If a signal is made of two pure tones, what should its DFT magnitude show?

Strong peaks at the bins corresponding to those two tones (and their mirror bins for a real signal), with small or zero magnitude elsewhere if the tones align neatly with the sample window.

How it works

The DFT projects a sampled signal onto complex exponentials. Each coefficient measures correlation with one basis frequency. The transform is linear and invertible, so no information is lost for the sampled signal. In practice, windowing, sampling rate, and finite precision determine what you can reliably observe.

Edge cases

  • Aliasing: sampling too slowly folds high frequencies into lower ones.
  • Leakage: a frequency not aligned to a DFT bin spreads energy across bins; windows trade leakage for main-lobe width.
  • DC and Nyquist bins: for real input, bin 0 and bin N/2 (when N is even) are special and not mirrored the same way as other bins.
  • Scaling conventions: libraries differ in where they place the 1/N factor; check documentation before comparing results.

Advanced usage

For large N, computing the DFT directly costs O(N^2). The Fast Fourier Transform computes the same DFT in O(N log N), making frequency-domain methods practical for audio, images, radar, and scientific computing.

Check yourself

Why is the convolution theorem important for performance?

Direct convolution of long signals is expensive. The convolution theorem lets you transform both signals, multiply pointwise in the frequency domain, and transform back. With an FFT, this can be much faster than direct convolution for sufficiently large inputs.
