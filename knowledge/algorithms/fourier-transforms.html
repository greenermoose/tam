A Fourier transform rewrites a signal as amounts of different frequencies. It is useful because many tasks are easier when you work with frequencies instead of moments in time.

From waves to ingredients

A sound or radio signal changes over time. A Fourier transform asks which steady waves, at which strengths, add up to that changing signal.

Why it matters

Finding a loud tone, removing noise, or compressing audio becomes simpler when the signal is described by its frequency ingredients.

How to think about it

Think of a recipe written two ways: one lists actions over time, the other lists ingredient amounts. Both describe the same dish. The transform changes the description, not the signal.

What just happened

The strongest pair is bins 1 and 7; the quieter pair is bins 2 and 6. For real-valued samples, bins above the midpoint mirror lower bins. The magnitudes are sample numbers from an eight-point transform, not a live calculation on your machine.

Try it

Run grep 2.000 spectrum.txt. The two remaining non-zero bins tell you a second, softer wave is present.

If it goes wrong

I expected only one strong bin.
Real signals produce mirror pairs. Both bin 1 and bin 7 describe the same tone.
grep found nothing.
Type the decimal point exactly: grep 4.000 spectrum.txt.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

What does a large magnitude mean?

That frequency is present with a large amplitude in the signal.

Why do bins 1 and 7 both light up?

For real-valued input, the transform has conjugate symmetry; the upper bin mirrors the lower one.

How it works

The discrete transform sums each sample multiplied by a complex exponential. A fast algorithm such as the FFT computes the same bins with fewer operations. Windowing reduces spectral leakage when the signal does not repeat exactly inside the sampled window.

Edge cases and failure modes

  • Real inputs give conjugate symmetry; only about half the bins are independent.
  • Finite windows smear energy into neighbouring bins.
  • Bin spacing is sample rate divided by window length.
  • Zero padding interpolates the display but adds no new information.
