Guide / Systems and computing / Important algorithms for modern computing / Fast Fourier Transform

A Fast Fourier Transform finds the same frequency information as a direct transform, but reuses repeated work. That reuse makes long signals practical to analyse.

What the FFT changes

The Discrete Fourier Transform asks how much of each frequency is in a signal. A direct answer checks every sample against every frequency. The Fast Fourier Transform, or FFT, gets the same answer by sharing work between those checks.

Why it matters

Audio, images and radio all contain frequencies. Without the FFT, analysing a long recording would take far longer than it needs to.

How to think about it

Imagine adding a long column of numbers. Pairing neighbours first, then pairs of pairs, saves repeated additions. The FFT uses the same idea with wave calculations.

What just happened

For eight samples the two counts are close. By 4096 samples the direct method needs over sixteen million combinations, while the FFT needs about forty-nine thousand stages. These are sample work counts for a power-of-two input, not timings from your machine.

Check yourself

Decide your answer before you reveal it.

Which column grows faster as N doubles?

The direct column. It is proportional to N squared; the FFT column is proportional to N times log2(N).

Why do the examples use powers of two?

The classic Cooley-Tukey FFT splits the input neatly when N is a power of two. Other sizes need padding or more general decompositions.

How it works

The Cooley-Tukey algorithm recursively splits a transform into even and odd samples, reusing the fact that twiddle factors repeat. In-place variants store each butterfly's result where its inputs were, reducing memory traffic.

Edge cases and failure modes

  • Floating-point roundoff accumulates differently from the direct sum.
  • Real-valued inputs produce conjugate symmetry across bins.
  • Non-power-of-two sizes need mixed-radix or Bluestein methods.
  • Bad memory layout can cost more than the saved arithmetic.
