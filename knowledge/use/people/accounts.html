User accounts and groups

This page explains how Linux identifies your user account, keeps your home folder separate from other people's files, and organizes permissions through groups.

Who is using the computer?

Each person using a computer can have their own account with a separate name, password, and home folder under /home/. One account cannot open another person's private files by default.

Why it matters

Separate accounts keep personal documents, browser profiles, and preferences isolated while letting everyone share the installed operating system cleanly.

How to think about it

Your username is your nametag; your UID number is the numeric badge Linux checks underneath; and groups are shared clubs (such as audio or printer access) that grant specific device or admin rights.

New words

UID
The numeric user identifier Linux attaches to your account and every file you create.
GID
The primary numeric group identifier for your account.
Home directory
Your personal folder (usually /home/<username>) for documents and user configuration.

Inspect your account and plan user changes

Goal: read your account name, UID, home path, and group memberships safely, and understand the reversible boundaries before adding or changing another account.

Before you start: inspecting your own account requires no privileges. Creating, changing groups for, or deleting accounts requires administrator approval and should be planned with a clear rollback decision first.

Worked example

Inspect your own user ID, home folder, and current groups read-only:

$ id
$ printf 'Home: %s\n' "$HOME"
$ getent passwd "$USER"

To check whether another local user exists without creating or altering anything, query the local user database read-only:

$ getent passwd guest

Creating an unprivileged account with its own home directory uses standard Linux account tools (useradd -m <name> followed by passwd <name>), and adding supplementary access such as wheel uses usermod -aG <group> <name>. Notice the -a (append) flag: running usermod -G without -a replaces all supplementary groups instead of adding one.

Try it

Run id and ls -ld "$HOME" in a terminal. Verify who owns your home folder and check that the permission bits keep other accounts out of private directories.

If it goes wrong

A newly added group does not take effect immediately
Group memberships are assigned at login. Sign out and sign back in (or start a fresh login session) before testing access.
Forgot -a with usermod -G
Because missing -a drops previous supplementary groups, always record id -Gn <user> before modifying any group list.

Some groups (such as wheel for sudo or docker for the Docker socket) are effectively administrator-level. Only grant them to trusted accounts, and check tam-sudo-docker --configured before altering container group settings.

Check yourself

Answer before revealing the explanation.

Why is usermod -aG <group> <user> safer than omitting -a?

-a appends to existing supplementary groups; without it, usermod -G overwrites the user's previous group list.

After adding your account to a new group, why might a running terminal still behave as if you are not in it?

Running processes keep the group list initialized when your login session started until you open a new login session.
